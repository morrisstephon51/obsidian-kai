# Assignment 2: Interpreting Figurative Language (Chapter 3)

Using the four steps from Figure 3.2, page 101: isolate the vehicle, articulate its characteristics, select the characteristics most significant in context, then make the interpretive leap to the tenor.

### 1. "Play it by ear"

- **The vehicle** is the ear, taken from playing music by listening instead of reading sheet music.
- **The characteristics of the vehicle** are that a musician who plays by ear hears a song and reproduces it without any notation in front of them. There is no plan on paper. They work from memory and instinct and adjust in real time. It is a skill that takes years to build, not a shortcut around one.
- **The characteristics that seem most important in context** are the missing sheet music and the adjusting in real time. When people use this phrase about plans, they keep those two traits and quietly drop the years of training.
- **The interpretation** is that the phrase is a claim of competence, not an admission of being unprepared. Saying "we will play it by ear" does not mean you have no plan. It means you are skilled enough that you do not need one. That is why it sounds confident instead of careless. I play piano, so I notice the metaphor hands people credit for an ability most of them have not actually earned.

### 2. "A Band-Aid fix"

- **The vehicle** is the Band-Aid, the adhesive bandage.
- **The characteristics of the vehicle** are that it is small, cheap, and fast. It goes on the outside of the body. It covers the wound so nobody can see it. It is made for minor cuts, it falls off in a day or two, and it does nothing about infection or anything happening underneath the skin.
- **The characteristics that seem most important in context** are that it sits on the surface and that it hides the wound from view.
- **The interpretation** is that the phrase is a sharper insult than people realize. Calling something a Band-Aid fix is not only saying it is temporary. It is saying the fix makes the problem look handled, so whoever applied it gets to walk away believing the job is done. The criticism lands on the fixer's attention, not just on the repair.

### 3. "Drinking from a firehose"

- **The vehicle** is the firehose.
- **The characteristics of the vehicle** are that it moves an enormous volume of water under high pressure. It takes several trained people to hold one steady. It was built to fight fires, not to give anybody a drink. Putting your mouth on it would knock your teeth out.
- **The characteristics that seem most important in context** are the pressure and the mismatch between the tool and the use. The volume is not just large, it is hostile.
- **The interpretation** is that the phrase admits the water is exactly what you wanted. You were thirsty. The problem is the delivery, not the content. It also carries a built-in excuse, because nobody can drink from a firehose, so failing to absorb it is not your fault. It is a complaint about how something was taught, dressed up as a joke about the student.

### 4. "Spinning your wheels"

- **The vehicle** is a car's wheels spinning in mud, snow, or ice.
- **The characteristics of the vehicle** are that the engine is running, the wheels are turning fast, fuel is burning, and there is plenty of noise and motion. None of it produces forward movement. Worse, the spinning digs the hole deeper. The usual reaction is to press harder on the gas, which makes the hole worse.
- **The characteristics that seem most important in context** are full effort with no traction, and effort that actively makes the situation worse.
- **The interpretation** is that this phrase is not about laziness. It describes somebody working hard and getting nothing for it. The insult goes to the situation, not to the person. It also carries a warning that most people miss, which is that more effort is not the fix. You need traction. That means changing something about the surface you are on, not revving the engine harder. Sending out the same application fifty more times is pressing the gas.

### 5. "The plug is in"

- **The vehicle** is an electrical plug going into an outlet.
- **The characteristics of the vehicle** are that the plug carries power that already exists in the wall to a device that cannot use it yet. Nothing is created. The power was there the whole time and the device was ready the whole time. The connection is the entire event. It is a small physical act with an immediate and complete result, and it is binary. Either it is in or it is not.
- **The characteristics that seem most important in context** are that the power already existed and that connection was the only missing piece.
- **The interpretation** is that the problem was never ability on either end. Both sides were already capable, and what was missing was the link between them. The metaphor also puts the work in the right place. You do not build the power and you do not build the device. You close the gap. The word "plug" already means the person who connects you to something, so both meanings say the same thing, which is that access is the product.

# Assignment: A Short History of Thinking about Thinking (page 94)

I can think fast when I've done the thing enough times. I play piano, and if I'm playing in front of people and I hit a wrong note, there's no time to stop and work out what happened. My hands keep going and usually cover it before my brain catches up. If I stopped to think, everybody in the room would hear the gap. That's the fast thinking the reading is talking about. It only works because I've put in the hours. I'm not smarter in that moment, I just have the reps.

Money is the opposite. Right now every dollar counts, so I slow all the way down. If something costs real money I'll look it up, compare it, then sit on it for a day before I decide. I've noticed I do this with small stuff too, which isn't always smart. Sometimes I spend more time on the decision than the decision is worth.

I don't think I need every possible outcome. I used to believe that's what being careful meant. Now I think going after every outcome can be a way of not deciding at all. If I can live with the worst realistic version of a choice, I move.

An action I got from childhood is that I speak to people. If I walk into a room I say something to everyone in it. You didn't walk past grown folks in my house without speaking. I still catch myself doing it in places where nobody expects it, like an elevator, and people look at me funny. I've never once decided to do it. It just happens.

The word is "sir" and "ma'am." I say it without thinking, to anybody older than me and to a lot of people who aren't. That came straight from home and I never questioned it until this assignment. It's a reflex, not a choice. What's interesting is that I know some people hear it as too formal, or as something from the South, and it's neither of those to me. It's just what respect sounds like in my ear because that's the sound I grew up around. That's the part the reading made me notice. I've been carrying an opinion about a word my whole life and I never once asked where I picked it up.
