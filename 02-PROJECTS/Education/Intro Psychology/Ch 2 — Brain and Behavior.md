# Ch 2: Brain and Behavior

> [!summary] The chapter in one line
> Your behavior runs on two message systems: the **nervous system** (fast, electrical and chemical, made of neurons) and the **endocrine system** (slower, using hormones in the blood). The **cerebral cortex** handles thinking, language, and planning; the **subcortex** keeps you alive. The **prefrontal cortex** is what lets you choose a long-term reward over a short-term one, and that skill (**self-regulation**) can be practiced.

## Unit 1: The Nervous System

### The neuron

A **neuron** is a nerve cell, the basic unit of the nervous system. Neurons carry signals throughout the body. Each one has four main parts:

| Part | What it does | Memory hook |
|---|---|---|
| **Dendrites** | Branch-like fibers that **receive** messages from other neurons | Tree branches catching signals |
| **Soma** (cell body) | **Combines** incoming signals; holds the nucleus | The decision-maker |
| **Axon** | Long fiber that **carries** the message away from the soma | The cable |
| **Axon terminals** | Endings of the axon that **pass** the signal to the next neuron's dendrites or soma | The sender |

**Direction of a signal:** dendrites → soma → axon → axon terminals → next neuron

### How neurons communicate

Neurons use two things:
1. **Electrical impulses** (action potentials) *inside* the neuron
2. **Chemical messengers** (neurotransmitters) *between* neurons

**Ions** are atoms or molecules with a positive or negative electrical charge. Ions inside and outside the neuron are what give it its electrical activity.

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Myelin** | Fatty coating on some axons that insulates them and speeds up the signal |
| **Ion channels** | Tiny openings (proteins) in the cell membrane that open or close to let specific ions in or out |
| **Resting potential** | A neuron at rest; the **inside is negative** (about –70 millivolts) and the outside is positive |
| **Threshold** | The charge level a neuron must reach to fire |
| **Action potential** | An electrical impulse that travels down the axon when the neuron fires. **Sodium ions (Na⁺) rush in** and reverse the charge inside. |

### The action potential in 4 steps

1. **At rest.** Inside of the axon is negative, outside is positive.
2. **Firing.** Ion channels open and positive **sodium ions (Na⁺) rush in**. The inside briefly becomes positive and the outside becomes negative.
3. **Moving.** This zone of reversed charge travels down the axon like a wave.
4. **Resetting.** Behind the wave, positive ions (**potassium, K⁺**) flow back out, restoring the negative inside. The axon returns to its resting state, ready to fire again.

> [!tip] Short version
> Negative at rest → sodium rushes in (flips to positive) → the flip travels down the axon → potassium flows out (flips back to negative).

### Neurotransmitters

**Neurotransmitters** are chemicals that carry messages across the **synapse** (the small gap between two neurons). One neuron releases them, and they attach to **receptor sites** on the next neuron.

| Type | Effect on the next neuron |
|---|---|
| **Excitatory** | Makes it **more** likely to fire |
| **Inhibitory** | Makes it **less** likely to fire (calms the brain down) |

Neurotransmitters are involved in everything the brain does: thinking, feeling, moving, remembering. Their balance affects mental and physical health, so they help explain **mental illness, drug effects, and behavior**.

### Neuropeptides

**Neuropeptides** don't carry a direct message. They **regulate** how other neurons work, affecting memory, pain, emotion, hunger, pleasure, and stress.

> [!example] Teacher's analogy
> A **neurotransmitter** is like a **text message** (a direct message between two neurons).
> A **neuropeptide** is like your **phone settings** (it changes how messages are received or prioritized).

**Two neuropeptides to know:**
- **Enkephalins.** Natural painkillers that ease pain and stress.
- **Endorphins.** Released by the **pituitary gland**. They relieve pain **and** actively create feelings of pleasure or **euphoria**. Linked to the **runner's high**, acupuncture, extreme sports, and the **placebo effect**.

Both are part of the brain's **reward and survival system**. Some people can get hooked on repeated endorphin release (long-distance running, adrenaline sports).

### Neuroplasticity and neurogenesis

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Neuroplasticity** | The brain's ability to **reorganize itself** and form new connections throughout life. Supports learning, memory, and recovery from injury. |
| **Neurogenesis** | The **production of new brain cells**. Linked to learning, memory, emotional regulation, and adapting to change. |

> [!warning] Myth
> "Adults can't grow new brain cells" is **false**. Adults do produce new neurons, especially in the **hippocampus**.

### Putting it together: the twig-snap story

| What happens | System involved |
|---|---|
| You hear a twig snap behind you in the dark. Sensory neurons in your ears send signals toward the brain. | **Somatic nervous system**, part of the **peripheral nervous system (PNS)** |
| The signal reaches your brain for processing (auditory cortex). It passes neuron to neuron: soma → axon → terminals → neurotransmitters → next neuron. | **Central nervous system (CNS)** |
| Your brain reads the sound as danger. Heart rate rises, pupils widen, digestion slows, muscles get ready. Adrenal glands release adrenaline. | **Autonomic nervous system** → **sympathetic** branch (**fight or flight**) |
| You decide to run. Neurons carry the message from brain to leg muscles. | **Somatic nervous system** (voluntary movement) |
| You're safe. Heart rate and breathing slow, digestion resumes. | **Parasympathetic** branch (rest and calm) |

> [!tip] Remember the two autonomic branches
> **S**ympathetic = **S**tress (fight or flight). **P**arasympathetic = **P**eace (calm down).

## Unit 2: Brain Research

### Imaging brain *structure*

| Method | How it works | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **CT scan** | X-rays | Structural problems: bleeding, strokes, tumors, head trauma, skull fractures. Used in **emergencies** because it is fast, widely available, and cheap. |
| **MRI** | Magnetic field and radio waves | Detailed images of **soft tissue**: gray vs. white matter, tumors, lesions, inflammation, developmental abnormalities |

### Localization of function

**Localization of function** is the idea that specific abilities (movement, speech, emotion) are controlled by **specific areas** of the brain. Researchers test this in several ways:

| Method | How it works | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **ESB** (electrical stimulation of the brain) | A mild electrical current is applied to one area, usually during surgery, to see what movement or sensation it triggers | Shows what an area *does* |
| **Ablation** | Surgically removing or destroying a small portion of brain tissue to see what function is lost | Invasive; mostly animal studies or rare medical cases |
| **Deep lesioning** | A thin wire delivers high-frequency current to destroy a small, targeted area deep in the brain | Used to disable malfunctioning areas, e.g. **severe epilepsy**. More targeted than ablation. |

### Measuring brain *activity*

| Method | How it works | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| **EEG** | Electrodes on the scalp record **brain waves** (electrical activity near the brain's surface) | Seizure disorders, sleep stages, monitoring during anesthesia |
| **PET scan** | A **radioactive tracer** is injected; active areas use more **glucose**, so they light up | Detecting abnormal function: Alzheimer's, schizophrenia, tumors |
| **fMRI** (functional MRI) | Same machine as an MRI, but tracks **oxygen / blood flow** over time. Active areas need more oxygen. | Seeing which areas are active during a task (the lecture's example: detecting lying) |

> [!tip] Keep them straight
> **CT and MRI** show *what the brain looks like*. **EEG, PET, and fMRI** show *what the brain is doing*.
> PET = **glucose** + tracer. fMRI = **oxygen** + blood flow. EEG = **electricity**.

## Unit 3: The Cerebral Cortex

The **cerebral cortex** is the thin, wrinkled outer layer of the brain. It is mostly **gray matter** but holds about **70% of the neurons in the CNS**. It makes human abilities possible: abstract thinking, language, tool-making, learning complex skills, and handling social situations. The **wrinkles increase surface area**.

### Two hemispheres

- The cortex is split into a **left** and **right hemisphere**.
- They communicate through a thick band of fibers called the **corpus callosum**.
- **Lateralization** means each hemisphere specializes in certain functions.
- Each hemisphere controls the **opposite side** of the body.

| Left hemisphere | Right hemisphere |
|---|---|
| Controls the **right** side of the body | Controls the **left** side of the body |
| **Analytical and verbal** | **Creative and contextual** |
| Language, reading, writing, math, logic, step-by-step processing, time, rhythm, detail | Spatial awareness, recognizing faces and patterns, reading emotions, tone of voice, nonverbal cues, big picture, creativity, understanding jokes and sarcasm |

> [!tip] Teacher's memory trick
> **Left** = **L**ogic, **L**anguage, **L**ists
> **Right** = **R**hythm (the lecture's version; your book may say emotion/spatial), **R**ecognition, **R**elationships

**Hemispheric dominance:** most people favor one side, which shows up in things like handedness or which eye you use to look into a microscope.

### Split-brain patients

When the **corpus callosum is cut** (sometimes done to treat severe epilepsy), the two hemispheres can no longer talk directly. This is called a **split-brain** patient.

The experiment shows that each hemisphere **receives input from and controls the opposite side**, and that **speech lives in the left hemisphere**:

| Shape flashed to... | Goes to... | Can the patient say it? | Which hand can pick it out by touch? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Right** visual field (circle) | **Left** hemisphere | **Yes**: "I saw a circle" | **Right** hand only |
| **Left** visual field (triangle) | **Right** hemisphere | **No**: says they saw nothing | **Left** hand only |

**The takeaway:** part of the brain can *know* something and act on it even when the person can't put it into words.

### The four lobes (plus the cerebellum)

| Lobe | Location | Main job |
|---|---|---|
| **Frontal** | Front | Sense of self, **motor control**, higher mental abilities (reasoning, planning) |
| **Parietal** | Top, behind the frontal lobe | **Body sensations**: touch, temperature, pressure |
| **Temporal** | Sides, by your temples and ears | **Hearing**, language comprehension, auditory memory |
| **Occipital** | Back | **Vision** |
| *Cerebellum* | Lower back, under the lobes | Posture, coordination, muscle tone, memory of skills and habits |

> [!tip] Memory hook
> **F**rontal = **F**orward thinking. **P**arietal = **P**hysical touch. **T**emporal = **T**unes (hearing, by the temple). **O**ccipital = **O**ptics (vision).

### Key areas inside the lobes

**Frontal lobe**
- **Prefrontal cortex.** The very front. The brain's **command center** for **executive functions**: planning, decision-making, self-control.
- **Association areas.** Combine information from multiple senses.
- **Broca's area.** In the **left frontal lobe**. Handles **speech production**. Damage means the person **knows what they want to say but struggles to say it**: speech is slow, effortful, and ungrammatical.
- **Primary motor cortex.** At the back of the frontal lobe. Controls **voluntary movement**. Body parts needing precision (like hands and fingers) get **more space** on the motor map. Thanks to neuroplasticity, experience reshapes it (a piano player gets more space for the fingers).
- **Mirror neurons.** Fire both when **you** do an action and when you **watch someone else** do it.

**Temporal lobe**
- **Wernicke's area.** In the **left temporal lobe**. Handles **understanding language**. Damage means the person **speaks fluently but the words don't make sense**.

**Occipital lobe**
- **Primary visual cortex.** Receives input from the eyes and starts decoding what you see.

> [!warning] Broca vs. Wernicke (a classic exam question)
> **Broca's** = **B**roken speech (can understand, can't produce). Frontal lobe.
> **Wernicke's** = **W**ord salad (can speak fluently, but it's meaningless). Temporal lobe.
> Both are in the **left** hemisphere.

## Unit 4: The Subcortex and the Endocrine System

### The subcortex

The **subcortex** is everything **beneath** the cerebral cortex. It runs the basic survival functions you take for granted: breathing, heartbeat, hunger, sleep. It has three regions: **hindbrain, midbrain, forebrain**.

**Hindbrain** (the oldest, most primitive part, shared with many animals)

| Structure | Job |
|---|---|
| **Medulla** | Vital functions: **heart rate, breathing, swallowing**. Damage is usually **fatal**. |
| **Pons** | Regulates **sleep and arousal**; acts as a bridge between brain areas |
| **Cerebellum** | Coordinates voluntary movement, **balance, posture** |
| **Reticular formation** | A network of cells and fibers running through the medulla and brainstem. Keeps the brain **alert and awake** (arousal, attention). |

**Midbrain**
- Sits above the hindbrain and **connects the hindbrain to the forebrain**. Involved in sensory processing and reflexes.

**Brainstem**
- Where the spinal cord thickens and joins the brain. Almost **all communication between the cortex and the body** passes through it.

**Forebrain** (the most advanced part of the subcortex)

| Structure | Job |
|---|---|
| **Thalamus** | The brain's **sensory switchboard**. Routes incoming sensory information to the right part of the cortex, for **every sense except smell** |
| **Hypothalamus** | Small but powerful. Regulates **hunger, thirst, body temperature**. Works directly with the **endocrine system**. |
| **Limbic system** | Controls **emotion and memory**. Includes: |
| → **Amygdala** | **Emotion and fear** |
| → **Hippocampus** | **Forming memories** |

> [!tip] Memory hooks
> **Medulla** = **M**ust have to live (heartbeat, breathing).
> **Thalamus** = the **T**raffic director for senses (not smell).
> **Hippocampus** = memory **campus**, where memories are made.
> **Amygdala** = the **alarm** (fear).

### The endocrine system

The **endocrine system** is a second messaging system that also shapes behavior, mood, and even personality.

| Nervous system | Endocrine system |
|---|---|
| Fast | Slower |
| Electrical and chemical | Chemical only |
| Uses **neurotransmitters** | Uses **hormones** |
| Messages travel through neurons | **Glands** release hormones into the **bloodstream** or lymphatic system |

**The glands**

| Gland | Location | Hormone / job |
|---|---|---|
| **Pineal** | In the brain | Releases **melatonin**, which regulates the **sleep-wake cycle**. Melatonin rises in the dark and drops in light. Phone screens at night trick the brain into thinking it's daytime. |
| **Pituitary** | Just below the brain; size of a pea | The **"master gland."** Controls other glands (thyroid, adrenals, ovaries, testes) and releases **growth hormone**. It takes its orders from the **hypothalamus**. |
| **Thyroid** | Neck | Controls **metabolism** (how fast the body uses energy). **Overactive**: jittery, anxious, weight loss. **Underactive**: sluggish, weight gain, depressed mood. |
| **Adrenal glands** | On top of the **kidneys** | Release hormones for the **fight-or-flight** response: racing heart, energy spike |
| **Pancreas** | Abdomen | Listed on the lecture diagram (regulates blood sugar) |
| **Ovaries / testes** | Reproductive organs | Listed on the lecture diagram (sex hormones) |

**Adrenal hormones**
- The **adrenal medulla** releases **epinephrine** (adrenaline) and **norepinephrine**.
- **Epinephrine = fear.** **Norepinephrine = anger.**

> [!tip] Hook
> **Epi** = **E**scape (fear). **Nor-epi** = **N**ot backing down (anger).

**Anabolic steroids** are a **synthetic (lab-made) version of testosterone**.

## Unit 5: Psychology in Your Life: Self-Regulation

### The marshmallow test

- **Self-regulation** is the ability to **resist temptation** and control your behavior to reach a goal.
- **The test** (designed by psychologist **Walter Mischel**): a child is given one marshmallow. They can eat it now, **or** wait a few minutes without touching it and get **two**.
- Some kids ate it right away. Others used distraction to hold out.
- **The follow-up:** researchers tracked the children for decades. The kids who **waited** tended to be healthier, happier, more successful professionally, more financially stable, and had **better SAT scores**.
- **The real point:** it isn't about marshmallows. It's about **delayed gratification**, giving up a small reward now for a bigger reward later. Health, success, and savings all come from small repeated choices, not one-time efforts.

> [!important] Self-regulation is a skill, not a trait you're born with
> It can be **built through practice**. Every time you stop yourself from reacting on impulse, you strengthen it. Kids who ate the marshmallow right away can still change course later in life. The goal isn't perfection. It's learning to **pause, think, and then choose** instead of just reacting.

### Link back to the brain

The **prefrontal cortex** (front of the frontal lobe) handles **executive functions**, which let you:
- set goals and make plans
- focus on what matters and tune out distractions
- control impulses and emotions
- monitor progress and change strategies when needed

### Hot system vs. cool system

Self-regulation is a battle between **short-term** and **long-term** thinking:

| | **Hot system** | **Cool system** |
|---|---|---|
| Type | Emotional | Thinking (cognitive) |
| Brain area | **Limbic system** | **Prefrontal cortex** |
| What it says | "You deserve it. Do it now." | "Your future self will thank you." |
| Focus | Short-term | Long-term |

The goal of self-regulation is to **cool down the hot system** so the **cool system can lead**.

### Two strategies

| Strategy | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Selective attention** | Focus on **anything except** the temptation | Go for a walk, drink water, play with your kids, put your phone in another room |
| **Cognitive reappraisal** | **Reframe** how you think about the situation | Instead of "I'm missing out" on a night out, think "I'm saving money and skipping extra calories." For the kids: "If I don't eat this now, I get two later." |

**Real-life examples of poor self-regulation:** going out Friday night instead of studying for an exam; spending a tax refund on impulse instead of paying bills.

> [!question] Personal reflection (not graded)
> - Where in my life am I regulating well? (gym, healthy food, studying ahead of time)
> - Where am I not?
> - What's one small delayed-gratification habit I could start this week?

## Exam Traps

> [!warning] Hemispheres control the opposite side
> Left hemisphere → **right** side of the body. Right hemisphere → **left** side.

> [!warning] Signal direction
> Dendrites **receive**, the axon **sends**. The signal goes dendrites → soma → axon → terminals.

> [!warning] Sodium in, potassium out
> Firing = **sodium rushes in** (inside turns positive). Resetting = **potassium flows out** (inside turns negative again).

> [!warning] Structure vs. activity scans
> CT and MRI show **structure**. EEG, PET, and fMRI show **activity**.

> [!warning] Thalamus handles all senses except one
> **Smell** skips the thalamus.

> [!warning] Neurotransmitter vs. neuropeptide
> A neurotransmitter **sends** a message. A neuropeptide **changes how** neurons respond to messages.

## Self-Quiz

> [!question]- 1. Name the four parts of a neuron in the order a signal travels through them.
> Dendrites → soma → axon → axon terminals.

> [!question]- 2. What is the charge inside a neuron at rest?
> Negative (about –70 mV). The outside is positive.

> [!question]- 3. What happens during an action potential?
> Ion channels open, sodium ions (Na⁺) rush in, and the inside of the axon briefly turns positive. This reversal travels down the axon, then potassium (K⁺) flows out to restore the resting state.

> [!question]- 4. What does myelin do?
> It insulates the axon and speeds up the signal.

> [!question]- 5. What's the difference between excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitters?
> Excitatory ones make the next neuron more likely to fire. Inhibitory ones make it less likely.

> [!question]- 6. How are endorphins different from other painkilling chemicals?
> They don't just block pain. They also create pleasure or euphoria (for example, runner's high).

> [!question]- 7. Is it true that adults can't grow new brain cells?
> No. Neurogenesis happens in adults, especially in the hippocampus.

> [!question]- 8. In the twig-snap story, which branch of the nervous system triggers fight or flight, and which one calms you down?
> Sympathetic triggers fight or flight. Parasympathetic calms you down.

> [!question]- 9. Which scan would a doctor use first after a head injury in the ER, and why?
> A CT scan, because it's fast, widely available, and cheap.

> [!question]- 10. What does a PET scan track, and what does an fMRI track?
> PET tracks a radioactive tracer carried to areas using more glucose. fMRI tracks changes in oxygen and blood flow.

> [!question]- 11. What is the difference between ablation and deep lesioning?
> Both destroy brain tissue. Ablation removes a portion surgically. Deep lesioning uses a thin wire and electrical current to destroy a small, targeted area, so it's more precise.

> [!question]- 12. What connects the two hemispheres?
> The corpus callosum.

> [!question]- 13. A split-brain patient sees a triangle in their left visual field. Can they name it? Which hand can pick it out?
> They can't name it (the right hemisphere has no speech). The left hand can pick it out by touch.

> [!question]- 14. Name the four lobes and the main job of each.
> Frontal: motor control, reasoning, planning, sense of self. Parietal: touch, temperature, pressure. Temporal: hearing and language. Occipital: vision.

> [!question]- 15. Someone knows what they want to say but can only speak slowly and ungrammatically. Which area is damaged?
> Broca's area (left frontal lobe).

> [!question]- 16. Someone speaks fluently but their words make no sense. Which area is damaged?
> Wernicke's area (left temporal lobe).

> [!question]- 17. What do mirror neurons do?
> They fire when you perform an action and when you watch someone else perform it.

> [!question]- 18. Damage to which structure is usually fatal, and why?
> The medulla, because it controls heart rate and breathing.

> [!question]- 19. What does the thalamus do?
> It acts as the sensory switchboard, sending sensory information to the correct area of the cortex (all senses except smell).

> [!question]- 20. Which gland is the "master gland," and what controls it?
> The pituitary gland. It is controlled by the hypothalamus.

> [!question]- 21. What does melatonin do, and which gland releases it?
> It regulates the sleep-wake cycle. The pineal gland releases it.

> [!question]- 22. Epinephrine is linked to which emotion? Norepinephrine?
> Epinephrine: fear. Norepinephrine: anger.

> [!question]- 23. What did the long-term follow-up of the marshmallow test find?
> Kids who waited tended to be healthier, happier, more successful, more financially stable, and had better SAT scores.

> [!question]- 24. Which brain area powers the hot system, and which powers the cool system?
> Hot: limbic system. Cool: prefrontal cortex.

> [!question]- 25. What is the difference between selective attention and cognitive reappraisal?
> Selective attention means focusing on anything except the temptation. Cognitive reappraisal means reframing how you think about the situation.
