# Ch 2: Reading Analytically

> [!summary] The chapter in one line
> Read to become **conversant** with a text, not just to get the gist. Start with specific words and sentences, figure out what the text is reacting to and what it assumes, then use it as a lens on something else. Writing helps you read better, and reading helps you write better.

## What you should be able to do (chapter objectives)

1. **Be conversant** with a reading: able to talk about it without looking everything up, instead of only knowing the general impression.
2. Explain **what is at stake** by finding its **organizing binaries**.
3. Use **paraphrase** to unlock implications and start interpreting.
4. Use **Passage-Based Focused Freewriting (PBFF)** to come up with ideas.
5. **Uncover assumptions**: what the reading must already believe, given what it openly claims.
6. **Apply a reading as a lens** to think about another text or subject.

---

## 1. Beyond the Banking Model

- **Paulo Freire** criticized the **banking model of education**. Teachers "deposit" information, and students hand it back on exams.
- His point: information in, information out **does not teach thinking**. Being able to repeat someone's ideas doesn't mean you can think about them.
- The fix is to **multitask**: take in the information *while* you start working out your response to it.

## 2. Becoming Conversant (vs. Reading for the Gist)

| Reading for the gist | Becoming conversant |
|---|---|
| General impression | Can discuss specifics from memory |
| Passive, just moving through | Active: marking, rephrasing, questioning |
| "Hook in a cloud," nothing to hold onto | Anchored in actual words and sentences |

**How to do it:**
- **Read with the grain.** First understand the piece fairly, *on its own terms*.
- **Suspend judgment.** Think *with* the piece before arguing against it.
- **Go local first.** Focus on individual sentences, words, and short passages, then build up.
- Reading is **physical as well as mental**: underline, circle, write in the margins.

### Do The Method while you read (from Ch. 1)
Mark:
- **Exact repetitions** (words that repeat)
- **Strands** (kinds of words that repeat, like words that are all about money or all about water)
- **Binaries** (words that oppose other words)

Then be ready to say **which ones matter most** and why.

## 3. Standard Moves When Writing About a Reading

1. **What's at stake?** What is opposed to what, and why does it matter to the writer?
2. **What does it want to accomplish?** This isn't always what it openly argues. Frame this with **Pitch / Complaint / Moment** (see §7).
3. **Make the implicit explicit.** What is it saying beyond what it states? (This is **Move #3 of the Five Analytical Moves** from Ch. 1.)
4. **Consequences.** If we think the way the reading suggests, what follows?
5. **Evidence check.** How well does the evidence support the claim?

> [!quote] Ted Connor, Music: what "critical reading" means
> Go beyond summary and quick judgment. Take notes on content *and* commentary, then end with **three points** (a main idea, an interesting part of the argument, the author's reasons for writing, rhetorical techniques). Look at *how the argument is built* and its **ideological position**.

## 4. Words Matter

- **Anchor your analysis.** Starting from a general impression is like "putting a hook in a cloud." There's nothing specific to rephrase, draw implications from, or trace back to assumptions.
- **Robert Scholes:** bad readers replace the words on the page with their **own associations or preset ideas**, so they replay their own views instead of taking in the writer's.
  - ➜ Read **what is actually written, not what you think might be written.**
- Language **shapes** how we understand the world. It doesn't just reflect it. Being able to **name** something is key to understanding it.
  - Example: **"hunger" vs. "food insecurity."** Picking a different term changes how urgent the issue feels. (This connects to **Assignment 1**.)

### Word histories
- Word histories are tied to **cultural histories**. A big reason languages change is **people moving** over time.
- Think of words that came into English from immigrant cultures.
- Activity: **Try This 11.9, Tracing Word Histories (p. 409)** → **Assignment 2**.

### Latinate vs. Anglo-Saxon diction
- Style guides often say to use short Anglo-Saxon words instead of long **Latinate** ones.
- The counterpoint: Latinate words have **several parts**, so **one word can do the work of a phrase or a whole sentence**.
  - *hypodermic* = "under the skin"
  - *philosophy* = "love of wisdom" (Greek, passed into Latin)
  - *exogenous* = "outside its kind" (*ex-* = out)
  - *symbiotic*, *systemic* each pack a lot of meaning into one word
- The downside: some Latinate words just sound fancy (*prevaricate* = lie).

> [!quote] Dawn Lonsinger, English & Creative Writing
> She wants students to **play** with language, notice how flexible and strange it is, and "fall in love with the strangeness and coolness of words."

## 5. Close Reading: The Pointing Sequence

Three activities done **in order**:

```
Pointing  →  Paraphrase × 3  →  Passage-Based Focused Freewriting (PBFF)
(get lines     (unpack one        (write your way to an
 out loud)      passage)           idea about the whole)
```

### ① Pointing
- Class members pick sentences they think are important and **read them aloud one at a time**.
- **No comments** before or after, **no raising hands**, **no interruptions**.
- Purpose: get the reading's language out loud so everyone hears what others noticed.

### ② Paraphrase × 3
1. Pick a **short passage**.
2. Find **synonyms for all the key terms**.
3. **Rephrase it again** several times.
4. **Compare** the versions you wrote.
5. Decide what you **now notice** about the original's meaning.

> [!tip] Why it works
> Each synonym is a little different from the original word. Those differences show you what the original word choice was doing.

### ③ Passage-Based Focused Freewriting (PBFF)
- The textbook calls it **"probably the single best way to arrive at ideas about what you are reading."**
- Unlike regular freewriting (Ch. 1), it **stays focused on one representative passage**. The idea: if you understand how *one piece* works, you'll understand the *whole* better.
- Freewrites often become **starting points for longer papers** or portfolio pieces.
- Read **pp. 63–65** for the full discussion.

#### Worked example: David Foster Wallace, "This Is Water" (Kenyon commencement)
> "There are these two young fish swimming along and they happen to meet an older fish swimming the other way, who nods at them and says 'Morning, boys. How's the water?' And the two young fish swim on for a bit, and then eventually one of them looks over at the other and goes 'What the hell is water?'"

**Wallace's point:** the most obvious, important realities are the **hardest to see and talk about**. We stop noticing what has become deeply familiar because we've stopped *choosing* to notice it. **Clichés/"banal platitudes"** can hold life-or-death truths.

**What makes the sample freewrite a good PBFF:**
- ✅ **Stays grounded** in the chosen passage and keeps coming back to it
- ✅ **Starts with description and details**, not a big idea
- ✅ **Brings in an outside definition** (of cliché) and connects it to Wallace
- ✅ Includes a **personal response** without drifting into unrelated personal stories
- ✅ Tries for an **interpretive conclusion, but tentatively**
- ✅ Ends up **reading against the grain**: it notices a tension. Wallace says we can *choose* what we think about, but also that we're shaped by water we *can't see*. That tension is a starting point for more writing.

> [!note] Takeaway
> Freewriting gives ideas room to form before you force them into a neat, focused shape too early.

> [!quote] Bruce Wightman, Biology: start with what the writers were worried about
> Don't summarize. Start with **what the writers were worried about**: what problem they had and what they already believed. Then dig into the **details and evidence**: what do they *really* know versus what they *think* they know? You can't call out weak spots until you understand the writer's problem.

## 6. The Pitch, the Complaint, and the Moment

| Term | What it is | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| **The Pitch** | The position the writer **most wants readers to accept**, even if it's **never stated outright** | *What is this piece trying to get me to believe?* |
| **The Complaint** | The position the writer is **pushing back against**, wants to challenge, or wants to reframe | *What made the writer want to write this in the first place?* |
| **The Moment** | The **historical and cultural context** the piece was written in | *When and where was it written? What else was going on?* |

> [!warning] Watch for this on the quiz
> The slide on this is worded confusingly: it puts "(the claim or thesis)" next to *the complaint*. The complaint is the view the writer **argues against**. The pitch is the writer's **own** position. Don't mix them up.

**The Moment, examples:** someone writing about the Civil War **during Reconstruction** would see it differently than someone writing **during the 20th-century Civil Rights movement**. A piece from **1930** can mean something very different from one written in **2015**.

📖 Read **p. 69** for two full examples: **Obama's first inaugural address** and Wallace's **"This Is Water."**

## 7. Uncovering Assumptions

- **Assumptions** are the unstated ideas a piece takes for granted.
- **The key question:** *"Given its overt claim, what must this reading also already believe?"*
- You're **reasoning backward**, rebuilding the chain of thinking that led the writer to their position.
- Steps:
  1. Pick a statement from the reading.
  2. Ask what **unstated ideas** it depends on.
  3. Work back to the premises: *if it believes this, what else must it believe?*
  4. Ask how those assumptions change your understanding of the **whole reading**.
- Judge **both the claim and the assumptions under it.** An argument can be logical and still rest on a shaky assumption.

> [!example] Practice example (made up for practice, not from the book)
> **Statement:** "Schools should ban phones so students can focus."
> **Assumptions:** phones are the main distraction · focus comes from removing things rather than from engagement · students can't regulate their own phone use · a school has the authority to control personal devices.

**Fieldwork idea from the text:** for a week, write down interesting statements you overhear (ads, billboards, memes, jokes, editorials). Pick the best ones and list the **unstated assumptions** behind each.

> Slide 28 refers to a textbook example about asking teachers to judge humor. The setup isn't in the slides, so check that page in the book.

## 8. Reading Against the Grain

- **With the grain** = understand the piece on its own terms (do this first).
- **Against the grain** = notice what the piece says **that it may not have meant to say**.
- Writers can never fully control what they communicate. Words always say more (and less) than intended. Think of an email you thought was clear that someone read the wrong way.
- **Example: *Jane Eyre*.** Jane keeps mentioning that she's plain, which suggests looks don't matter much. Read against the grain, the novel's **obsession** with plainness shows that it secretly believes **looks *do* matter**.

## 9. Tracking & Reformulating Binaries

### Tracking
- **Binary** = a pair of opposites (open/closed, ugly/beautiful, global/local).
- **Tracking binaries = tracking the thinking** in a piece.
- Binaries are the places where **a piece argues with itself** and works out what it believes.

#### Worked example: James Howard Kunstler, *Home from Nowhere* (1996)
> "Sometime in the past forty years we ceased to speak of ourselves as **citizens** and labeled ourselves **consumers**… Consumers, unlike citizens, have no responsibilities, obligations, or duties to anything larger than their own needs and desires…"

- **Organizing binary:** **public vs. private**
- Kunstler **renames** that binary several times, and each new name develops the idea:

| Public side | Private side |
|---|---|
| citizen | consumer |
| responsibilities / obligations / duties | own needs and desires |
| the common good | the self |
| adult (implied) | infantile |
| civic life / public realm | private wants |

- That's how he gets to his conclusion: *this is why "we cannot solve any of our social problems."*

> [!warning] The trap: either/or thinking
> If you jump to a binary that's too broad or not quite accurate, you end up **oversimplifying** and seeing everything as one extreme or the other. Remember that the binary is where the writer is still working things out. It isn't the final answer.

### Reformulating (steps)
1. **Find several** opposing pairs.
2. **Define and analyze** the key terms.
3. **Question** whether the binary is accurate, and **rephrase** the terms.
4. Replace **"either/or"** with **"to what extent?"**
5. **Complicate** the binary: find evidence that doesn't fit it, then come up with **better-worded binaries** that match what's really at issue. Sometimes the two "opposites" turn out to be two sides of the same story.

📖 Examples on **p. 75**.

## 10. Applying a Reading as a Lens

- Look at a **new subject through the reading's ideas** and try to **think in its terms**.
- Test how well the reading **explains features** of your subject.
- You may need to **adjust the lens** (refocus it) so it fits the new material.
- 📖 Example applied to a speech on **p. 78**.

> Example: use Wallace's "water" idea as a lens on social media feeds. What have we stopped noticing because it's everywhere?

## 11. Reading Like a Writer: Text Marking

- The best way to get better at writing is to be the most **attentive reader** you can.
- Study how other writers work.
- Mark the text with **circling, selective underlining, and margin notes**. "Selective" means don't highlight everything.
- Goal: notice **the words and word choices most responsible for the reading's meaning and effect**.
