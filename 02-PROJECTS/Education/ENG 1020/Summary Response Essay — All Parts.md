# PART II: Summary Paragraph (Week 5)

"Black Men and Public Space" by Brent Staples first appeared in 1986, when Staples was a young graduate student at the University of Chicago and years away from the editorial board of the *New York Times*. The essay is short and the tone is calm, which is part of what makes it land. Staples does not raise his voice. He reports on his own life the way a journalist reports on somebody else's.

He opens on a deserted street in Hyde Park with a white woman in her early twenties walking ahead of him. She looks back, takes him in, and runs. Staples calls her "my first victim," and the word does a lot of work. He was twenty two, new to Chicago, and had just discovered that he could alter public space simply by being in it. He describes himself as a softy who could barely take a knife to a raw chicken. None of that mattered. What mattered was how he looked at night on an empty street.

From there he stacks up the evidence. He hears car locks thunk down as he crosses in front of traffic. He is followed through a jewelry store, where the owner brings out a Doberman. He runs into a magazine office on deadline and gets chased by security who assume he is a burglar. He mentions a Black colleague in Waukegan who was picked up as a murder suspect and almost jailed. Each story is told plainly and then set down. He is not building to outrage. He is building to a total.

Staples also turns the essay on its history. He grew up in Chester, Pennsylvania, around real violence. He watched tough guys get locked up, and he lost people, including his brother. He says he was one of the timid ones. That background makes his point sharper, not softer. He knows exactly what danger looks like, and he knows he is not it.

He ends with what he actually does about it. He gives people room. He keeps his distance on subway platforms. He stays calm and pleasant on late walks. And he whistles melodies from Vivaldi and Beethoven, because people who hear classical music coming down the sidewalk relax and sometimes even hum along. He compares it to the cowbell a hiker wears in bear country. The essay closes there, on a man making noise so he will not be mistaken for a predator.

# PART III: Response Paragraph (Week 6)

Staples is right about the thing that matters most, which is that the danger runs in both directions. The woman who ran from him was afraid. He was in more danger than she was. A frightened driver, a frightened shop owner, or a frightened police officer can end a Black man's evening or his life over nothing. Staples wrote that in 1986 and it reads like it was written last week. That is the part of the essay I would not argue with at all.

The piece got personal for me at the whistling. I play piano, and classical is where I started. So I know what it means to use music as a signal that you are safe. Staples picked Vivaldi and Beethoven for a reason. He needed a sound that the people around him already filed under harmless, and those composers came pre approved. That is a small, sharp observation about how this country works. It also stings, because he did not choose that music the way I choose mine. He chose it the way you choose a tool.

This is where I push back. Staples presents the whistling as a solution, and I read it as a survival tactic dressed up as one. He gives people wide berth. He waits away from nervous strangers. He keeps his voice easy. That is a full time job, and it is a job he never applied for. The woman on the street got to keep running without ever learning anything. He is the one who went home and changed how he moves through the world. When a stranger's fear becomes my responsibility to manage, the fear has already won. Staples knows this, and the cowbell line tells you he knows it. A hiker wearing a cowbell is not fixing anything. He is just trying not to get mauled.

The psychologist Claude Steele read the same moment and named it. Steele opens *Whistling Vivaldi* (2010) with Staples' story, and he uses it to explain stereotype threat, the pressure a person feels when they know a stereotype about their group could be applied to them at any moment. Steele's point is that the pressure itself takes a toll. It occupies your attention and wears you down even when nothing bad happens that day. Steele would say the whistling is not just protection from other people. It is Staples trying to get out from under a weight he is carrying whether or not anyone is watching. I think Steele is right, and I think it strengthens the essay rather than correcting it.

So the two readings fit together. Staples wrote about what he does on the sidewalk, and Steele explains what it costs him internally. Where I land is that the burden is still on the wrong person, and naming that is not the same as fixing it. Staples never claims to have fixed it. He just tells you what he does at night so he can get home.

## References

Staples, B. (1986, December). Black men and public space. *Harper's Magazine*. (Originally published as "Just walk on by: A Black man ponders his power to alter public space," *Ms.*, September 1986.)

Steele, C. M. (2010). *Whistling Vivaldi: And other clues to how stereotypes affect us*. W. W. Norton.

# PART IV: Final Draft (Week 7)

## Fear Is Not His to Carry: A Response to Brent Staples

This summary response essay discusses "Black Men and Public Space" by Brent Staples. Staples is a journalist who has spent decades on the editorial board of the *New York Times*, but he wrote this essay in 1986, when he was a graduate student in his twenties at the University of Chicago. It first appeared in *Ms.* as "Just Walk on By" and was revised for *Harper's* later that year. The essay is about what happens when a man discovers that strangers see him as a threat, and about what he starts doing to keep himself safe from their reaction. Though Staples is right that a Black man learns to manage other people's fear in public, his solution of whistling Vivaldi is a survival tactic and not a remedy, because it asks the person being feared to carry the cost of a problem he did not create.

Staples is right about the thing that matters most, which is that the danger runs in both directions. The woman who ran from him was afraid. He was in more danger than she was. A frightened driver, a frightened shop owner, or a frightened police officer can end a Black man's evening or his life over nothing. He wrote that in 1986 and it reads like it was written last week. That is the part of the essay I would not argue with at all.

The piece got personal for me at the whistling. I play piano, and classical is where I started. So I know what it means to use music as a signal that you are safe. Staples picked Vivaldi and Beethoven for a reason. He needed a sound that the people around him already filed under harmless, and those composers came pre approved. An alternative reading comes from the psychologist Claude Steele, who opens *Whistling Vivaldi* (2010) with this exact story. I think Steele is right, and I think his reading strengthens Staples rather than correcting him. ### Conclusion

The practical value of this essay is that it shows a problem from the position most people never occupy. Anyone can understand being afraid of a stranger at night. Staples makes you sit with what it is like to be the stranger, and to know that the other person's fear is the thing most likely to hurt you. For readers who have lived it, the essay names something familiar and gives it a shape. For readers who have not, it is a short, calm, almost unanswerable account of a cost they have never had to pay. The limit of the essay is that Staples stops at what one man does to stay safe, and he never asks the people doing the fearing to change anything. That is not a flaw in his argument so much as an honest admission about who actually has to adapt. Almost forty years later, that is still the part worth arguing about.
