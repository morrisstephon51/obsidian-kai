# Courts & Jurisdiction — Terms to Know

> [!summary] The unit in one line
> Before a court can decide anything, it has to have **power** — power over the *type of case* (subject-matter jurisdiction) **and** power over the *parties* (personal jurisdiction). Everything on this list is either a kind of power, a way of getting it, or a rule about which of several qualified courts should actually hear the case.

> [!tip] The order the concepts stack in
> 1. Does **any** court have authority over this kind of dispute? → subject-matter jurisdiction
> 2. State or federal? → federal question / diversity / exclusive / concurrent
> 3. Can this court reach **this defendant**? → personal jurisdiction, long-arm, service of process
> 4. Of the courts that qualify, **which one** should hear it? → venue, forum, forum non conveniens
> 5. **Whose law** does it apply once it gets there? → conflict-of-law, choice-of-law

---

## 1. Courts and the People in Them

**Doctrine of judicial immunity**
Judges cannot be sued personally for money damages for acts performed in their judicial capacity — even if the ruling was wrong, or made maliciously or corruptly. The point is independence: a judge who could be sued by every losing party would decide cases defensively. It is **not** unlimited — it does not cover acts taken in the *clear absence of all jurisdiction*, and it does not cover administrative or non-judicial acts (like hiring and firing staff). The remedy for a bad decision is an **appeal**, not a lawsuit against the judge.

**Magistrate**
A judicial officer with **limited authority** who handles matters below the level of a full judge. In the federal system, U.S. magistrate judges are appointed by the district judges and handle preliminary work — issuing warrants, setting bail, ruling on discovery disputes and pretrial motions — and may try misdemeanors, or even civil cases, **with the parties' consent**. In many state systems, "magistrate" refers to the judge of a minor or local court.

**En banc proceeding**
French for "on the bench." Normally a federal appeal is heard by a **panel of three** judges. In an en banc proceeding, **all** the judges of that appellate court sit together to hear the case. It is rare and reserved for two situations: the case is exceptionally important, or panels of that same court have issued conflicting decisions that need to be reconciled.

---

## 2. The Parties

**Plaintiff**
The party who **starts** the lawsuit by filing a complaint. Alleges that they were harmed and asks the court for a remedy. Carries the burden of proof.

**Defendant**
The party the suit is **brought against**. Responds by filing an answer, and may raise defenses or counterclaims. In a criminal case, the defendant is the person accused of the crime.

---

## 3. Jurisdiction: The Core Idea

**Jurisdiction**
From Latin *juris* ("law") + *dictio* ("to speak") — literally *the power to speak the law*. The authority of a court to hear a case and issue a binding decision. A court needs **two** kinds at once:

| | What it covers | Can it be waived? |
|---|---|---|
| **Subject-matter jurisdiction** | The *type of case* | **No.** Can be raised anytime, even on appeal. A judgment without it is void. |
| **Personal jurisdiction** | The *parties or property* | **Yes.** Waived if the defendant doesn't object early. |

**Subject-matter jurisdiction**
The court's authority over the **kind of dispute** in front of it — bankruptcy, probate, felonies, small claims, and so on. Because it comes from a constitution or statute, the parties cannot create it by agreeing to it, and cannot destroy it by ignoring it.

**Original jurisdiction**
The power to hear a case **first**, at its beginning. These are **trial courts**: they take evidence, hear witnesses, and find facts. (The U.S. Supreme Court has original jurisdiction in a narrow set of cases — those affecting ambassadors and public ministers, and those in which a state is a party.)

**Appellate jurisdiction**
The power to **review** a lower court's decision. Appellate courts take **no new evidence and no new witnesses** — they work from the trial record and the briefs, and they review mainly questions of **law**, giving deference to the trial court's findings of fact.
*(This term appears twice on the course list — it's one definition.)*

**General jurisdiction**
A court that can hear **almost any kind of case**, except those specifically assigned somewhere else. These are the workhorse trial courts — state circuit, superior, or district courts, and the federal district courts.

**Limited or special jurisdiction**
A court restricted to a **narrow class** of case or a **dollar ceiling** — probate, family, traffic, small claims, bankruptcy court, U.S. Tax Court.

**Trial de novo**
Latin for "anew." A **completely new trial**, conducted as if the first proceeding never happened. Typically used when appealing from a court that is **not a court of record** (small claims, magistrate's court), where there's no transcript for an appellate court to review — so instead of reviewing, a higher trial court just tries the whole case over.

## 4. When Can a Case Go to Federal Court?

Federal courts have **limited** subject-matter jurisdiction. There are two main doors in.

**Federal question**
Door #1. The case **arises under** the U.S. Constitution, a federal treaty, or a federal statute. There is **no minimum dollar amount** for a federal question case.

**Diversity-of-citizenship**
The *condition* that the parties are citizens of **different states** (or a state citizen versus a foreign citizen or country). Requires **complete diversity**: no plaintiff may share a state of citizenship with any defendant. A corporation is a citizen of both its state of incorporation and the state of its principal place of business.

**Diversity jurisdiction**
Door #2. The federal court's *authority* to hear such a case. Requires **both**:
1. Complete diversity of citizenship, **and**
2. An amount in controversy **over $75,000**

The historical reason for the door: give an out-of-state party a **neutral forum**, free of local bias. Note the twist — a federal court sitting in diversity applies **state substantive law**.

**Amount in controversy**
The dollar value the plaintiff claims is at stake. For diversity jurisdiction it must **exceed $75,000**, exclusive of interest and costs. ($75,000.00 exactly is not enough — it must be *more than*.)

## 5. Getting Power Over the Defendant

**Personal jurisdiction**
The court's power over the **parties** (or over property) before it. Sometimes called *jurisdiction over persons and property*. Three forms: in personam, in rem, and quasi in rem.

**In personam jurisdiction**
Power over the **person** of the defendant, so the judgment binds them personally and can reach their assets anywhere. A court gets it when the defendant is:
- **served while physically present** in the state,
- **domiciled** in the state,
- **consents** (including by a contract clause), or
- has **minimum contacts** with the state — contacts such that the suit doesn't offend "traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice" (*International Shoe*).

**Summons**
The official court document notifying the defendant that a suit has been filed, when they must respond, and what happens if they don't. Delivered together with a copy of the complaint.

**Service of process**
The **formal delivery** of the summons and complaint to the defendant. Two jobs at once: it satisfies **due process** (you get notice and a chance to be heard), and it is the mechanism by which the court actually **acquires personal jurisdiction**. Defective service can sink an otherwise valid case.

**Personal service**
Handing the summons and complaint **directly to the defendant** (or an authorized agent), usually by a sheriff, marshal, or licensed process server. The gold standard. When it's not possible, courts allow **substituted service** — leaving the papers with a competent adult at the residence, certified mail, or, as a last resort, publication.

**Default judgment**
A judgment entered **for the plaintiff** because the defendant failed to answer or appear within the time allowed. The plaintiff effectively wins by forfeit. This is why service of process matters so much — and why courts will set a default aside if service was improper.

**Long-arm statute**
A **state statute** that lets its courts reach out and exercise personal jurisdiction over a **nonresident** defendant, as long as that defendant has sufficient minimum contacts with the state — for example by transacting business there, committing a tort there, or owning property there. The "arm" of the court reaches across the state line.

**In rem jurisdiction**
Latin for "against the thing." Jurisdiction over **property located within the court's borders**, which lets the court resolve claims to that property even without personal jurisdiction over its owner. The judgment is **limited to the property** — it cannot reach the owner's other assets.
*(Related: **quasi in rem** — seizing a defendant's in-state property to satisfy a claim unrelated to that property, capped at the property's value.)*

## 6. State Court, Federal Court, or Either?

**Exclusive jurisdiction**
**Only one** court system may hear that type of case.
- *Federal only:* bankruptcy, patents, copyrights, federal crimes, most admiralty, suits against the United States.
- *State only:* divorce, adoption, most real property disputes, wills and probate.

**Concurrent jurisdiction**
**Both** state and federal courts have authority, so the plaintiff gets to choose. This covers diversity cases and most federal question cases. If the plaintiff files in state court, the defendant may be able to **remove** the case to federal court.

---

## 7. Which Court, and Whose Law?

**Conflict-of-law**
The **body of rules** a court uses to decide **which jurisdiction's law governs** when a dispute touches more than one state or country. It's a rulebook for resolving the collision, not a rule of substance itself.

**Choice-of-law**
The **actual determination** of which state's or nation's substantive law applies — the output of the conflict-of-law analysis. Also refers to a **choice-of-law clause** in a contract, where the parties agree in advance that (say) Delaware law will govern any dispute.

**Venue**
**Which particular court**, geographically, among those that have jurisdiction, should hear the case — typically the county or district where the defendant resides or where the events occurred. Venue is about **convenience and fairness**, not power. Because of that, it **can be waived or changed** (e.g., moved when pretrial publicity makes a fair trial unlikely).

**Forum**
The **court or place** where a dispute is heard. A **forum-selection clause** is a contract term in which the parties agree ahead of time where any lawsuit must be brought.

**Forum non conveniens**
Latin for "an inconvenient forum." A doctrine that lets a court **with** proper jurisdiction and venue **still dismiss or transfer** a case because another forum is substantially more appropriate — considering where the witnesses and evidence are, whose law applies, and the burden on local courts and jurors.

## Exam Traps (these are the ones that get tested)

> [!warning] Jurisdiction vs. Venue
> **Jurisdiction = power.** **Venue = location.** A court can have jurisdiction and still be the wrong venue. Wrong venue gets the case moved; no jurisdiction gets it thrown out.

> [!warning] Subject-matter vs. Personal jurisdiction
> Subject-matter jurisdiction **can never be waived** and can be raised at any time. Personal jurisdiction **can be waived** — if the defendant shows up and litigates without objecting, they've consented.

> [!warning] Diversity requires BOTH elements
> Complete diversity **and** more than $75,000. Federal question requires **neither** a dollar amount nor diverse parties.

> [!warning] Original vs. Appellate
> Original = fact-finding, witnesses, evidence, **first time**. Appellate = record review, **law**, no new evidence.

> [!warning] Trial de novo is not an appeal
> An appeal reviews what happened. A trial de novo **starts over from scratch**.

## Self-Quiz

> [!question]- 1. What two kinds of jurisdiction must a court have to decide a case?
> Subject-matter jurisdiction (over the type of case) and personal jurisdiction (over the parties or property).

> [!question]- 2. Which type of jurisdiction can be waived, and which cannot?
> Personal jurisdiction can be waived. Subject-matter jurisdiction cannot — it can be challenged at any point, even on appeal.

> [!question]- 3. What are the two requirements for diversity jurisdiction?
> Complete diversity of citizenship (no plaintiff shares a state with any defendant) and an amount in controversy exceeding $75,000.

> [!question]- 4. Does a federal question case have a minimum amount in controversy?
> No.

> [!question]- 5. What does a long-arm statute do?
> Lets a state court exercise personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant who has sufficient minimum contacts with the state.

> [!question]- 6. What is the functional difference between in personam and in rem jurisdiction?
> In personam binds the person, so the judgment can reach their assets generally. In rem is limited to the specific property within the court's borders.

> [!question]- 7. What happens if a defendant is properly served and never responds?
> The court can enter a default judgment for the plaintiff.

> [!question]- 8. Why does judicial immunity exist, and what are its limits?
> To protect judicial independence, so judges aren't sued by every losing party. It does not cover acts in the clear absence of all jurisdiction, or non-judicial/administrative acts.

> [!question]- 9. When does an appellate court sit en banc?
> When the case is exceptionally important, or when panels of that court have issued conflicting decisions. All the court's judges hear it instead of the usual three-judge panel.

> [!question]- 10. Distinguish conflict-of-law from choice-of-law.
> Conflict-of-law is the set of rules used to decide whose law applies. Choice-of-law is the actual determination of which law applies (or a contract clause selecting it in advance).

> [!question]- 11. A court has jurisdiction and proper venue but the witnesses and evidence are all 1,500 miles away. What doctrine applies?
> Forum non conveniens — the court may dismiss or transfer to the more appropriate forum.

> [!question]- 12. Name two areas of exclusive federal jurisdiction and two of exclusive state jurisdiction.
> Federal: bankruptcy, patents, copyrights, federal crimes, suits against the U.S. State: divorce, adoption, most real property disputes, wills and probate.

> [!question]- 13. What is a trial de novo and when is it used?
> A brand-new trial conducted as if the first never occurred, typically on appeal from a court that is not a court of record (small claims, magistrate's court).

> [!question]- 14. What two functions does service of process perform?
> It gives the defendant constitutionally required notice, and it is how the court acquires personal jurisdiction over them.

> [!question]- 15. Under concurrent jurisdiction, who picks the court?
> The plaintiff chooses where to file; the defendant may then be able to remove the case to federal court.
