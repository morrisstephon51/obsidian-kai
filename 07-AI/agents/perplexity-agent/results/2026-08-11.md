For **market research on competitor cost models and pricing strategies**, the most useful approach is to treat them as two linked analyses: first infer how competitors’ costs are structured, then map how they turn those costs into prices and discounts.[1][4][6]

- **Competitor cost models** are usually estimated indirectly from public and industry data, because competitors rarely disclose actual costs.[1][8]
- Common techniques include estimating the competitor’s **variable vs. fixed cost structure**, using company and industry data to infer **category-level costs** like materials, wages, and R&D, and applying **cost-driver frameworks** to connect observable operating drivers to likely cost levels.[1][3][8]
- In practice, analysts also estimate things like **gross margin pool** by backing into cost from normalized selling price, shipping burden, and cost to serve.[4]

- **Pricing strategy research** usually starts by identifying direct competitors, collecting their visible prices, and documenting the full offer rather than the headline price alone.[5][6][20]
- Important pricing elements to capture include **shipping**, **add-ons**, **service fees**, **discount schedules**, **promotions**, and **contract incentives**, because these often change the effective price customers pay.[5][6]
- Common competitor pricing positions are **below**, **at**, or **above** peers, and the business may use tactics such as **price matching**, **penetration pricing**, **premium pricing**, **loss leader pricing**, **price skimming**, or **dynamic pricing** depending on market goals.[6][7][12][14]

A practical market-research workflow is:

1. **Identify the competitor set** most relevant to your target segment.[5][6]
2. **Gather pricing data** from websites, marketplaces, storefronts, proposals, and sales collateral.[5][6]
3. **Normalize the offer** by comparing equivalent package sizes, terms, service levels, and hidden fees.[5][6][20]
4. **Infer cost structure** using public filings, industry benchmarks, staffing, production intensity, logistics burden, and other cost drivers.[1][3][8]
5. **Compare pricing logic to cost logic** to see whether competitors are competing on low cost, premium positioning, or margin expansion.[4][6][7]
6. **Translate findings into action** by choosing your own price position and discount rules based on your costs, margin floor, and customer willingness to pay.[6][10][14]
