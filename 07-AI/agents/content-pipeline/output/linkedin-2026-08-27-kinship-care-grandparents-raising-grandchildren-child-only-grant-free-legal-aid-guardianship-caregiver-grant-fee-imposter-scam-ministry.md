There is a grandmother in that congregation who came in last Sunday with two small children who are not her own but are hers now. Her daughter could not care for them — the reasons are the daughter's and the Lord's and nobody else's business — and when the choice came down to two children being scattered or one grandmother opening her door, she did not hesitate. She is sixty-six. She raises them now: the lunches, the baths, the homework she can barely read the new math of, the long nights. And she is proud, so she does not say much about how tight it has gotten on a fixed income that was never meant to stretch over three people, or how she lay awake this week not knowing how she is supposed to enroll two children in school when she is not their legal parent, or whether she can even take them to a doctor if one of them spikes a fever. She does not know that grandmothers like her — kinship caregivers — are recognized, and that there is real help that follows the children into her home. She does not know there is child-only assistance and medical coverage and food help the children may qualify for that has nothing to do with her income. She does not know that free legal aid will help her get guardianship without charging her a dime. And this week a text came promising a "grandparent grant" she could unlock for a small fee — and to a woman doing all of this alone, it sounded, for one tired second, like the only hand anybody had held out.

You know her. She is not failing, and she is not too old, and she did not raise her own children wrong — she is a woman who opened her home so two children would not be lost, which is the most honorable thing a person can do. Nobody handed her the sheet. Nobody told her that being the relative who is raising them is generally enough to start reaching help, that she does not have to prove she is their parent before anyone will lift a finger. Nobody told her there is child-only cash help, Medicaid, and food help that may follow the children based on the children, not on her. Nobody told her she can generally enroll them in school right now and arrange for a doctor even before the papers are done, because the law does not make children go without school or medicine while the grown-ups sort out custody. Nobody told her there are kinship-navigator programs and legal-aid offices whose whole job is to walk a grandmother through exactly this, at no cost. And nobody told her the one thing that would have protected her most this week: the "grant" she can "unlock" for a fee is not the real help — it is the first person trying to make money off a grandmother who is already giving everything she has.

And that is the part that has to be said plainly, and I will say it again before I am done: a grandmother who took in her grandchildren when no one else could is not a burden and did not fail at anything. Needing help to raise them well is not proof she cannot cope — it is proof she is carrying a load meant for more than one person, and carrying it anyway. Being proud, and not wanting to look like she is struggling, is not a flaw either. It is what happens when a woman has spent her whole life being the one others lean on, and no one ever told her it was her turn to be helped. Not knowing that kinship caregivers are supported, that there is child-only help that follows the children, that she can enroll them and reach a doctor, that free legal aid handles guardianship, and that she never has to pay to get help for the children is not a flaw in her. It is a gap nobody filled — and it is the exact gap the fee-charging guardianship "service," the fake grant, the agency imposter, and the "you have no rights, you're only the grandmother" lie are all standing in.

And there are doors. Real ones. Doors nobody names for a grandmother who has been quietly told, in a hundred small ways, that she is on her own with this because the children are not legally hers.

Start with the first thing she needs to hear: the text about a "grant" she can unlock for a fee is not her only option, and it is not the honest one. The honest first step is not a fee sent to a stranger — it is a free call to a kinship-navigator program or a legal-aid office, because for a lot of caregivers the distance between doing this alone and being helped is one free door they were never told they could knock on.

So here are the doors almost no one names for her plainly. The first is that being the relative who is raising them is generally enough to start: kinship caregivers are recognized, and she generally does not have to first prove she is the legal parent before she can begin reaching supports for the children in her care. The second is that there is child-only help that follows the children: child-only cash assistance, Medicaid, and food help may be available based on the children rather than her income, so the children she is raising may qualify even though she is not their parent. The third is that she can generally enroll them and get them care now: school-enrollment protections and caregiver-authorization tools generally let a relative caregiver enroll a child in school and arrange medical care even without full custody papers, so the children are not left without school or a doctor while the papers are sorted. The fourth is that free legal aid and a kinship navigator stand with her: free legal aid helps a relative get guardianship or the right papers at no cost, and kinship-navigator programs help her find benefits and resources and support, so she does not carry this alone or for a fee. The fifth is that she can keep her own record: her own notes of who lives with her, since when, and what each child needs are worth keeping, and she does not need perfect paperwork or a finished court case to start asking for help. The sixth is that she never has to pay to get help for the children: legal aid, the kinship navigator, and the benefits office do the honest work with nothing down, so she never hands a "guardianship service" a fee or a "grant" outfit gift cards for what free help does, and no honest helper unlocks a grant for a fee. So she is not the only one who was ever told she was on her own with this, and found the door was already open to her.

But hear this warning before she pays one fee or types one thing into one link, because the scams hunt a tired, proud caregiver as hard as anyone — a grandmother this stretched and this determined to manage will move the moment a confident voice says "I can get you a grant" or "I can get you the papers." Hear it plainly, and I will say it again at the end: real help does not work this way. Beware the "guardianship service," the "document specialist," or the notario-style operation charging a large fee to prepare custody or guardianship papers, because free legal aid does that at no cost and does it right. Beware any "grandparent grant" or "caregiver grant" you have to pay a fee or send gift cards to "unlock," "release," or "guarantee," because real help is not unlocked by a fee.
