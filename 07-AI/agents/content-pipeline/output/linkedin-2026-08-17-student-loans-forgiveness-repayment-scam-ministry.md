There is a young woman in that church who was the first person in her whole family to walk across a graduation stage, and she cannot open her student-loan statements anymore, because the number at the bottom feels bigger than the pride everyone felt that day, and she would rather let the envelopes pile up on the counter than admit that the degree everybody celebrated came with a bill she does not know how to pay.

You know her. Twenty-six. The first one. The one the aunties bragged about, the one the pastor pointed to, the one who was supposed to prove it could be done. She did the hard part — she finished — with no family map for any of it, no one at home who had ever filled out a FAFSA or carried a federal loan or could tell her what a "servicer" even was. And now she is back in the south suburbs working an entry-level job at a nonprofit that pays in purpose more than dollars, and a payment has come due that she is sure she cannot make, and she is certain of one thing that is completely wrong: that her only two options are pay it all or fall into default. She does not know that income-driven repayment exists — that a federal payment can be set by what she actually earns and the size of her family, sometimes to something she can breathe under, sometimes even to zero. She does not know that the nonprofit job she took because she believed in it might be the exact kind of public-service work that counts toward forgiveness. She does not know that getting out of default, or never landing there, is free, and that everything about her loans can be handled without paying a soul a dollar.

And someone is about to make it worse. That is the part that has to be said plainly, and I will say it again before I am done: applying for federal student-loan help is always free, the Department of Education does not cold-call to "enroll" you, and no one legitimate ever needs your FSA ID password. She does not know that yet. So the moment she starts searching for a way out, she is going to get a robocall that says she has been "selected for the new forgiveness program" and must act now before it "expires," and she is going to find a company that will "handle your paperwork" and "enroll you" for a fee up front or a charge every month, and she is going to hit a website that asks for her FSA ID login to "unlock" her forgiveness. A scared young borrower who has never had anyone to ask is exactly who those traps are built for. The only thing standing between her and paying for something that was always free — or worse, handing a thief the keys to her federal aid account — is information nobody ever sat down and gave her.

And there are doors. Real ones. Doors nobody names for a twenty-six-year-old who is the first in her family to hold a degree and the first to hold the debt, because who stands up in the fellowship hall and says the thing everyone praised her for is quietly crushing her.

Start with the first thing she needs to hear: taking loans to be the first one through the door was not a mistake, and not knowing the free federal options is not a failure of hers — it is a road nobody in her family had ever driven, so nobody could hand her the map. She is not irresponsible for owing money she borrowed to do exactly what everyone told her to do. She is not ungrateful for being frightened. Not understanding a system built out of acronyms and fine print is not stupidity — it is being the first, without a guide. And a lowered payment is not a loophole or a handout. It is a real, built-in federal option, designed for someone standing exactly where she is standing.

So here are the doors almost no one names for her plainly. The first is the one her fear hides completely: income-driven repayment, federal plans that set the payment on what she earns and her family size — sometimes low, sometimes nothing — applied for free through her servicer and at studentaid.gov, though what she qualifies for and what the payment actually is belong to the Department of Education and the servicer, not to me. Alongside it there is loan forgiveness — Public Service Loan Forgiveness for people in qualifying public-service and nonprofit jobs like the one she took, teacher forgiveness, and others — always free to pursue, with the terms and eligibility decided by the Department of Education. She needs to understand default and how to get out of it or stay out of it — consolidation, loan rehabilitation, deferment, forbearance if she is struggling or already behind — free through her servicer, with the right path decided case by case. She needs to understand the loans themselves — federal versus private, who her servicer even is, the difference between a deferment and a default, and the plain truth that all of this can be managed for free at studentaid.gov, so that a "program enrollment fee" is a warning sign and not a step. There is help to navigate it, and it does not cost her anything: Federal Student Aid at studentaid.gov, her loan servicer, a reputable nonprofit student-loan counselor, and her school's financial aid office. And underneath all of it, for the particular shame of being the one who "made it" and still cannot breathe, there are trusted mentors, her faith, one call to 211, and 988 if the weight ever gets too heavy to hold alone. She is standing in front of a whole row of doors — income-driven repayment, forgiveness, getting out of default, understanding the loans, free help to navigate, and support for the shame — and she is frozen at the counter certain there are none, because nobody ever told her a single one was hers.

But hear this warning before she pays one fee or types her login into one wrong box, because the scams circle the young borrower the second she starts looking for help. Hear it plainly, and I will say it again at the end: applying for federal student-loan help is always free, and no one can guarantee or "unlock" forgiveness for a fee. Beware the call, robocall, text, or ad saying you have been "selected" for a "new forgiveness program" and must act now before it expires — the Department of Education does not cold-call or text you to enroll, and there is no fee to apply. Beware the company that wants money up front or every month to "handle your paperwork," "enroll you," or "sign you up" for income-driven repayment, consolidation, or forgiveness — every one of those is free to apply for yourself through your servicer and studentaid.gov. Beware anyone asking for your FSA ID username and password, your full Social Security number, or a payment to "guarantee," "speed up," or "release" your forgiveness — your FSA ID is your legal signature, and no honest helper will ever need your password. The rule is flat: managing and applying for federal student-loan help is always free, the Department of Education and your servicer never charge to lower your payment or forgive your loans and never cold-call to enroll you, and nobody legitimate needs your login — so anyone charging upfront or monthly, swearing your forgiveness is guaranteed or instant, or reaching for your FSA ID is the danger, not the help.

So she almost fell for it. Not because she is naive — naive is not the word for a young woman who was the first in her family to finish a degree with no one to show her how. Not because she is careless — she carried a load nobody at home had ever carried and got herself across the stage. She almost fell for it because nobody ever walked her to the real doors, and the only voices offering her a way through were the ones built to rob her.

Here is the thing about the woman across town. Same story. First in her family, same degree, same terrifying payment, same certainty that default was the only exit. But a mentor sat down next to her and said: "Listen to me. That forgiveness call is a scam. You never pay a fee to lower a federal payment. Go to studentaid.gov yourself, find out who your servicer is, and ask them about income-driven repayment. Let me sit with you while you do it." So she did. She logged in herself, found her servicer, applied for a plan that set her payment to something she could actually pay, stayed out of default, and kept working the public-service job that might one day count toward forgiveness — and she never paid a soul a dime. She was not smarter than the woman in our pew. She was not more deserving. She had one person who knew the real doors were there and named them before the shame and the scammer won. One young woman got the help that was always free. The other almost paid a "debt relief" company every month for nothing and almost handed a stranger her FSA ID. The whole difference was somebody who said "that's free, the call is a trap, go straight to studentaid.gov and ask about income-driven repayment," and a silence where nobody ever did.

The woman in our pew can be met before she pays that first fee. Not with a lecture about how she should have gone somewhere cheaper, or picked a more practical major, or just paid it off, or never borrowed at all. With a hand on her shoulder and the plain truth that she has doors, that the help is free, and that the payment she is terrified of might be far more manageable than she has any idea.

Because this is where a tool like AI actually meets a young woman like her — quietly, at the kitchen table, late on a night the envelopes finally got too tall to ignore — doing the part that the not-knowing-where-to-start has been blocking. It can be the patient someone that explains what "income-driven repayment," "your servicer," "federal versus private," "PSLF," "consolidation," "rehabilitation," "deferment," "default," and "capitalized interest" actually mean, in plain words, as many times as she needs, and never once make the first person in her family to earn a degree feel foolish for not already knowing the language of a system nobody ever taught her. It can help her figure out who her servicer even is and walk her to studentaid.gov to log in herself. It can help her understand which repayment paths might exist for someone in her situation, so she can ask her servicer the right questions instead of guessing. It can help her read the default notice or the collection letter she has been too scared to open and find what it is actually asking. And it can name the fake "forgiveness" call, the upfront-fee "enrollment" company, and the FSA-ID-phishing site for exactly what they are, before they take one more thing from a woman who already did the hardest part.

Not to be the one who decides anything. Let that be plain. The machine does not enroll her in a plan, and it does not get to determine what she qualifies for, what her payment will be, or whether her job counts toward forgiveness — that belongs to the Department of Education and her servicer, under their rules, not a chatbot's guess. It is not the Department of Education, it is not her servicer, it is not a lawyer, it is not a financial advisor. And it must never, ever be fed her FSA ID password, her full Social Security number, or her bank or account logins — a public AI tool is not the place for a single character of that. It is only the patient someone that takes a cold, acronym-choked, shame-soaked moment and makes it walkable, so that a young woman who was the first to make it out does not hand what little she has to a stranger promising a forgiveness that was already hers to pursue for free.

That is the gap. It was never that the woman across town deserved it more, or was smarter, or borrowed more wisely. Same first-gen degree. Same debt. Same age. Same fear. It is that one woman had a mentor who said "that's free, the call is a scam, go to studentaid.gov and ask about income-driven repayment," and the other was left alone at the counter with a stack of envelopes, a wrong idea that default was her only exit, and a scammer who felt like the only door. Not more worthy. Not more careful. Just never told the doors were hers.

A word of care, plainly, and I will say the important parts twice. This is general navigation, not legal, financial, or tax advice, and not a determination of what you qualify for or what your payment will be. Repayment plans, forgiveness, eligibility, and terms are decided by the Department of Education and your loan servicer under their rules — not by a machine and not by a guess. AI tools can be confidently wrong, so never trust a rule, a payment amount, a deadline, or an eligibility answer one gives you without confirming it at studentaid.gov, with your servicer, and with a reputable nonprofit counselor. And never put your full Social Security number, your FSA ID password, or a bank login into a public AI tool, and never pay an upfront or monthly fee or hand your login to anyone promising to lower your payment or forgive your loans. Hear the heart of it twice, because it is built for someone in exactly this spot: applying for federal student-loan repayment, consolidation, and forgiveness is always free, the Department of Education and your servicer never charge to lower your payment or forgive your loans and never cold-call to "enroll" you, and no one legitimate ever needs your FSA ID password — so if a "call," a "company," or an "ad" wants money up front or every month, swears your forgiveness is guaranteed, or reaches for your login, walk away. Route the real help to real, trusted places: studentaid.gov and your loan servicer for the loans themselves, free; a reputable nonprofit student-loan or credit counselor to advise you; your school's financial aid office for the loans it can see; a trusted mentor and your faith community for the shame and the fear; your church's benevolence fund for a true gap; 988 if the weight gets too heavy; and 211 or 211.org to find local financial counseling and support in one call.

A real first-generation graduate, taught how to make the same tool the loan companies and the "debt relief" outfits already use sit with her at the kitchen table, explain the words nobody explained, help her find her servicer and get to studentaid.gov herself, understand the repayment doors nobody named, read the notice she was afraid to open, and spot the fake "forgiveness" call and the upfront-fee "enrollment" company for exactly what they are — so she can get the help that was always free and not get robbed on the way to it.

The men and women in our churches are not falling into these traps because they were careless or borrowed foolishly. They are falling because they were the first in their families to go, with no map for the debt that came with it, and because nobody ever told them the help is free, the government does not cold-call to enroll them, and there are real doors they have never heard named.
