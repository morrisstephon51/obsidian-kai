There is a man in that church who almost signed over the title to his car to a storefront on the corner, and almost wired money to a stranger who called him, because his transmission died the same week his car payment was already late and he could not see a single other way to keep getting to work.

You know him. Thirty-eight. He works in a warehouse — he is on his feet the whole shift, running a forklift, doing the kind of work that does not happen from a couch and does not happen if you cannot get there. He financed the only car he could afford, because in the south suburbs a car is not a luxury, it is the difference between having a job and not having one. And a couple of weeks ago the transmission went. Twenty-eight hundred dollars to fix, the shop said, money he does not have sitting anywhere. His loan was already a month behind from a slow stretch of hours. Then the letters started — the word "repossession" on the page — and the phone started ringing, a man saying he could "stop the repo today" if he paid a fee right now, and on the same block a storefront with a bright sign promising fast cash if he just signed over the title. So a good, hardworking man, scared of losing the one thing that keeps him employed, reached for a pen and reached for his wallet.

He was about to walk into two traps at once. That is the part that has to be said plainly, and I will say it again before I am done: a title loan that makes you sign over your car for triple-digit interest can take the very car you were trying to save, and a caller demanding a fee to "stop your repossession" is very often a thief who works through fear, not through your account. He did not know either of those things. He thought the title loan was a lifeline, the caller was the lender, and there was no door but the two in front of him. None of that was necessarily true — and the only thing standing between him and a much deeper hole was information nobody handed him with the repo notice.

And there are doors. Real ones. Doors nobody names for a working man the week his car dies, because who wants to say out loud at coffee hour that he cannot cover a car repair.

Start with the first thing he needs to hear: a transmission failing is not a moral failure, and needing a car to get to work is not being bad with money. He did not run that car into the ground on purpose — a machine broke, the way machines break. He is not irresponsible. He is a father who financed the only car he could afford so he could keep working, and then had bad luck in a place built so that no car means no job. That is arithmetic, not character. Wanting to keep his car and pay what he owes is honorable — but there is no version of doing the right thing that requires him to sign over his title at triple-digit interest or hand a fee to a voice on the phone.

So here are the doors almost no one names for him plainly. The first is the one he is most afraid to walk through: call the lender before the repo, not after, and ask about a hardship option, a deferment, or a modified payment — many auto lenders have one, and they would often rather work something out than take the car. Before he ever touches a title loan, a credit union or a bank may offer a real small-dollar loan or a refinance at a real rate, not a triple-digit one. A repossession is not a lightning strike with no rules — there are reinstatement and redemption rights, and rules about how a repo can legally happen and what he might still owe, and those are worth knowing before he panics. He should check whether he already has gap insurance, a warranty, or a service contract that covers some of the repair. Some nonprofits and community programs help working families with an affordable repair or a low-cost car. Legal aid exists for a bad buy-here-pay-here contract, a wrongful repossession, or a repair-shop rip-off. And his own church's benevolence fund, plus one call to 211, can map the local help. He is standing in front of a whole row of doors — a lender hardship plan, a real loan instead of a title loan, his actual repo rights, existing coverage, nonprofit car help, legal aid, and his church and 211 — and he is reaching for a pen to sign his title away, because nobody ever told him a single one of those doors was his to open.

But hear this warning before he signs or pays anyone a dime, because the thieves hunt the people about to lose a car. Hear it plainly, and I will say it again at the end: a real lender works through your own account and your own paperwork — nobody legitimate calls demanding a fee by gift card, prepaid card, wire, or Zelle to "stop your repossession today." That caller is very likely a thief. The title-loan storefront is not a phone scam, but it is a trap — you sign over your car's title for triple-digit interest, and one missed payment lets them take the car, and you can still owe a balance after they sell it. There are robocalls that say "your car's warranty is expiring" that exist only to harvest a card number or sell a worthless "warranty." There are "credit repair" and "we'll fix your auto loan" outfits that charge a big fee upfront and deliver nothing. And there are phishing texts that say "your auto loan is past due, click here to pay" and exist only to steal a card or an account number. The rule is flat: never sign a title loan to fix a car before you have called your lender and a credit union, never pay a caller by gift card or wire to "stop a repo," real help is either free or a real regulated loan, and you make anyone prove it in writing before you pay a cent.

So he almost signed. Not because he is foolish — foolish is not the word for a man who runs a forklift all day and never misses a shift he can get to. Not because he is bad with money — a dead transmission is not a spending spree, it is a thing that happened to a machine. He almost signed because nobody ever walked him to the doors, and the only voices that spoke up about his car were the one built to charge him triple-digit interest and the one built to rob him.

Here is the thing about the man across town. Same story. Same dead transmission, same payment already behind, same repo warning, same title-loan sign on the corner, same "I can stop your repo" caller. But a friend had already sat with him at the kitchen table and said: "Listen. Before you sign anything or pay anyone, call your lender and ask about a hardship or a deferment — they don't want the car back, they want the payments. Call a credit union about a real loan before you even look at that title place. Find out your actual repossession and reinstatement rights so you're not guessing. And if anybody calls wanting a fee by gift card to 'stop the repo,' hang up — that's a thief." So he called his lender, worked out a deferment, got a small credit-union loan for the repair at a rate he could live with, and kept his car and his title. He never signed at the storefront and never wired a dime. He was not smarter than the man in our pew. He did not deserve it more. He had one friend who knew the doors were there and named them before the panic set in. One man kept his car and his paycheck. The other almost lost both to a title loan and a thief on the phone. The whole difference was somebody who said "call your lender, and don't pay that caller a fee," and a silence where nobody ever did.

The man in our pew can be met before he signs. Not with a lecture about how he should have bought a more reliable car. With a hand on the shoulder and the plain truth that he has doors, and that a broken-down car does not make him a failure or a mark for the next person who calls.

Because this is where a tool like AI actually meets a man like him — quietly, at the kitchen table late at night, when the repo notice and the repair quote are spread out in front of him and he cannot make the numbers work — doing the part that the fear and the strange words and the not-knowing-where-to-start have been blocking. It can be the patient someone that explains what "a title loan," "buy-here-pay-here," "reinstatement and redemption rights," "the deficiency balance," "gap insurance," "refinancing," and "a hardship deferment" actually mean, in plain words, as many times as he needs, and never once makes a working man feel like less for asking. It can help him understand what the repossession notice is actually saying and what a hardship request even is. It can help him put the words together — a letter to the lender asking for a deferment, a clear side-by-side of a credit-union loan against the title-loan storefront — so he is not deciding blind or signing in a panic. And it can help him recognize the "stop-the-repo" fee scam, the fake-warranty call, and the title-loan trap for exactly what they are, before they take one more thing from him.

Not to be the one who decides anything. Let that be plain. The machine does not decide what he owes, and it does not get to promise the car will be saved or the payment lowered — that belongs to the lender and to the law, not a chatbot's guess. It does not determine his repossession rights, and it is not the lender, not the loan office, not a lawyer. And it must never, ever be fed his Social Security number, his account number, or a bank or card number — a public AI tool is not the place for a single line of a family's private information. It is only the patient someone that takes a terrifying, word-thick, where-do-I-even-start thing and makes it walkable, so that a father who did everything right does not sign away his car or his savings to a storefront or a thief for no reason at all.

That is the gap. It was never that the man across town deserved it more, or was better with money, or was less proud. Same transmission. Same late payment. Same repo notice. Same calls. It is that one man had a friend who said "call your lender, get a real loan, know your rights, don't pay that caller," and the other was left alone at the table with a repair he could not afford and a phone that would not stop ringing. Not less deserving. Not less faithful. Just never told the doors were his.

A word of care, plainly, and I will say the important parts twice. This is general navigation, not financial or legal advice, not an endorsement of any lender, program, or product, and not a decision about what you owe, whether your car can be saved, or what your repossession rights are. Repossession and lending rules are set by state law and the loan contract — not by a machine and not by a guess. AI tools can be confidently wrong, so never trust an amount, a rule, or a rights answer one gives you without confirming it with the official source — the lender's hardship or loss-mitigation line, a legal-aid attorney, and the Illinois Attorney General or state regulator — and 211. And never put a Social Security number, an account number, or a bank or card number into a public AI tool. Hear the heart of it twice, because it is built for a family in exactly this spot: a real lender works through your own account and nobody legitimate makes you pay a stranger a fee by gift card to "stop a repo" — so make them prove it, and never sign a title loan before you have called your lender and a credit union. Route the real help to real, trusted places: the lender's hardship line for a deferment or a modified payment; a credit union for a real loan before any title place; a legal-aid attorney for a bad contract, a wrongful repo, or a repair rip-off; your own church's benevolence fund for the gap; and 211 or 211.org to find local car and transportation help in one call.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Dolton. Robbins. Chicago Heights. Matteson. South Holland. Not a webinar. A room. Real people. A real working father, taught how to make the same tool the man across town already knew how to use sit with him at the kitchen table, explain the words nobody explained, help him draft the hardship letter and weigh a real loan against a title-loan trap, and name the "stop-the-repo" caller and the title storefront for what they are — so that he can keep his car and his paycheck instead of signing his title away in a panic while a thief waits on the line.

The faithful men in our churches are not losing their cars because they were careless. They are losing them because a machine broke, and nobody ever told them the lender has a hardship door and that a caller is not the same as the truth — and panic is the worst possible time to sign anything.

We are here to sit with them before they pick up the pen.
