There is a woman in that church who opens the utility bill at the kitchen table in the August heat and goes very quiet. Fifty-eight years old. A fixed income that does not stretch the way it used to, a little part-time work when she can get it, and somebody else under her roof who counts on her — a grandchild some seasons, an aging parent others. She reads the bill twice. There is a number she cannot pay, and under it, a date. A shutoff date. And right there at the table, without telling a soul, she starts doing the math of what she can go without so the lights and the fan stay on — the prescription she can stretch another week, the groceries she can thin out, the thing she was going to fix that can wait again. She does not call it a crisis. She calls it managing. And she carries it by herself, because a bill you cannot pay feels like a private failure, not a thing you say out loud in the fellowship hall.

You know her. She is not careless with money — she is the one who pinches every dollar until it begs. She is not lazy — she works when there is work and she never asks anybody for anything. She is certain of one thing, and it is the thing keeping her stuck: that a utility bill is hers and hers alone to carry, that getting behind is something to be ashamed of, that other people manage and she should too. So she does not ask. She does not want to be the woman who could not keep her own lights on. She carries the shutoff notice folded in her purse like a secret, and she carries it alone.

And that past-due bill may not be a thing she has to carry by herself. That is the part that has to be said plainly, and I will say it again before I am done: energy assistance exists, applying for it is free, and the real power company does not call demanding gift cards in the next thirty minutes. She does not know that yet. She does not know there is help with a name — energy assistance, often called LIHEAP — built for exactly a person who is behind on the gas or the electric. She does not know the utility itself often has payment plans, budget billing, and hardship options. She does not know that 211 and a community action agency will help her apply and never send her a bill for it. Nobody sat her down and told her. So she assumes the only choices are pay it somehow or sit in the dark, and she keeps skipping her own necessities to keep the power on.

And there are doors. Real ones. Doors nobody names for a fifty-eight-year-old woman who is too proud, and too ashamed, to say out loud that she cannot pay the light bill.

Start with the first thing she needs to hear: falling behind on a utility bill on a fixed income that got squeezed is not a character flaw, and asking for help to keep the power on is not begging — it is using help that was built for exactly this. Not knowing an entire energy-assistance system exists is not mismanagement. It is the exact gap the "pay us now or we cut you off" callers feed on. And needing free help to apply is not weakness. It is what 211 and the assistance agencies are there for.

So here are the doors almost no one names for her plainly. The first is the one her shame hides completely: a shutoff notice is not the end of the road — energy-assistance programs, commonly called LIHEAP, and utility hardship and deferred-payment plans exist to help people who are behind; the rules and who qualifies are governed by program and state, so confirm the specifics with the assistance agency, 211, or the utility, but do not assume there is no help before anyone has looked. The second is a step she can take today, safely: call the utility directly, using the number printed on her real bill or the utility's official site — not a number a scary caller gave her — and ask about payment plans, budget billing, hardship options, and whether there are any steps or protections around a disconnection. The third costs her nothing: the state or community energy-assistance agency, community action agencies, and 211 help people apply at no cost, and no honest party charges an upfront fee or demands gift cards to "get your bill paid." The fourth is a thing she can start tonight: gather the bill, the shutoff notice, proof of income, and an ID — the kind of paperwork an application asks for — so she is ready. And underneath all of it, for the fear of the house going dark in the heat, there are trusted mentors, family, her church, her faith, a community action agency, one call to 211, and 988 if the weight of it ever gets too heavy to hold. She is standing in front of a whole row of doors — a name for the help, a safe call to her own utility, free hands to apply, a shield against the scam — and she is frozen certain there are none, because nobody ever told her a single one was hers to walk through.

But hear this warning before she pays a stranger a cent or types her Social Security and bank numbers into a website, because the scams hunt a person scared of losing the lights harder than almost anyone. Hear it plainly, and I will say it again at the end: energy assistance is free to apply for, the real power company does not demand gift cards or crypto in the next thirty minutes, and nobody legitimate needs your full SSN and your bank login handed to a stranger to "stop the shutoff." Beware the caller claiming to be the power or gas company — often with a number that looks real on the caller ID — who says the power goes off in minutes unless she pays right now with a prepaid card, gift cards, a payment app, a wire, or crypto; the real utility does not work like that, and that demand alone is the tell. Beware the site or ad promising to pay her bill or "approve" her for a utility grant that is really harvesting her SSN, her bank login, and her account number. Beware the outfit charging an upfront fee to do what LIHEAP and 211 do for free. And beware the caller who says she overpaid or is owed a credit and just needs her card or bank details to "send it back." The rule is flat: real energy assistance is free to apply for, the real power company does not demand instant payment in gift cards or crypto, and no legitimate process needs your full SSN and your bank login handed to a stranger to "stop the shutoff" — so anyone demanding gift cards in thirty minutes, charging an upfront fee to "get your bill paid," or fishing for your personal information is the scam, not the help.

Now one more thing, because it matters and the con hides right next to it: a real notice is not the same as a trick. Her actual shutoff notice, her actual past-due balance, and a real deadline from her real utility are not scams — she must not ignore a genuine notice or a real deadline, and calling the number on the real bill or applying through the real assistance agency or 211 is exactly the right move. Part of the help, a big part, is telling the real bill and the real deadline apart from the imposter call and the fake-assistance site — and that is exactly the kind of thing to bring to 211 or a community action agency, not to guess at alone in a panic.

So she almost fell for it. Not because she is foolish — foolish is not the word for a woman who stretches a fixed check over a whole household. Not because she is careless — she guards her home so hard she nearly paid a "power company" caller four hundred dollars in gift cards to keep the lights on tonight. She almost fell for it because nobody ever walked her to the real doors, and the only voice that reached her first was the one engineered to sound like the power company and built to make her panic before she could think.

Here is the thing about the woman across town. Same story. Same fixed income, same August heat, same shutoff date, same private shame. But a sister from church sat down next to her and said: "Let me sit with you. There is help to keep the power on, applying is free, and if anybody wants a gift card, hang up — that is not the power company." So she called the utility from the number on her real bill, got put on a payment plan, and applied for energy assistance through 211 at no cost. And she kept her lights on and her dignity with them. She was not smarter than the woman in our pew. She was not luckier. She had one person who knew the doors were real and free, and who named them before the panic and the "pay us in gift cards or we cut you off" call won. One woman kept her power on. The other almost paid a scammer to keep lights that were never actually about to go off that minute. The whole difference was somebody who said "the help is real, applying is free, guard your information," and a silence where nobody ever did.

The woman in our pew can be met before she buys those gift cards or types her Social Security number into a fake site. Not with a lecture about how she should have budgeted better, or called sooner, or not gotten behind. With a seat beside her and the plain truth that help to keep the power on exists, that applying for it costs nothing, that her real utility has options she has never been told about, and that a woman on a fixed income is allowed to reach for the help that was built for exactly this.

Because this is where a tool like AI actually meets a woman like her — quietly, at the kitchen table, on the night she finally lets herself admit she cannot pay the bill — doing the part the not-knowing has been blocking. It can be the patient someone that explains what "shutoff notice," "disconnection," "past due," "energy assistance," "LIHEAP," "payment plan," "hardship program," "budget billing," "reconnection," and "utility imposter" actually mean, in plain words, as many times as she needs, and never once make a careful woman feel small for not already knowing the vocabulary of a system nobody ever handed her. It can help her understand what an application gathers, so she is not blindsided. It can help her find the energy-assistance agency, her utility's real hardship line, and 211 that she can reach herself. And it can name the gift-card call, the fake-assistance site, and the upfront-fee con for exactly what they are, before they take from a woman already scared of the dark.

Not to be the one who decides anything. Let that be plain. The machine does not decide whether she qualifies, and it does not promise her a dollar, an approval, or that the power stays on — that belongs to the assistance agencies and the utility, not a chatbot's guess. It is not the power company and it is not the assistance office. And it must never, ever be fed her full Social Security number, her bank login, or her account passwords as things to type into a public tool — that is the exact information the thieves are fishing for. It is only the patient someone that takes an intimidating, jargon-soaked, fear-soaked dead end and makes it walkable, so that a good woman does not spend one more August rationing her own medicine to keep a fan running, or pay a scammer in gift cards for a shutoff that was never really thirty minutes away.

That is the gap. It was never that the woman across town deserved it more, or was smarter, or made better moves. Same age. Same fixed income. Same shutoff date, same August heat. It is that one woman had a sister who said "the help is real, applying is free, guard your information," and the other was left alone with a folded notice in her purse, a house she was scared to see go dark, and a "pay us in gift cards or we cut you off" call that felt more real than any help anyone had ever offered her. Not more worthy. Not more careful. Just never told the door was there.

A word of care, plainly, and I will say the important parts twice. This is general navigation, not legal, financial, or benefits advice, and not a determination of your eligibility. Energy-assistance eligibility, utility rules, disconnection protections, and deadlines are governed by program and state rules and decided by the agencies and the utility — not by a machine and not by a guess. AI tools can be confidently wrong, so never trust an answer one gives you about whether you qualify, a protection, or a deadline without confirming it with the energy-assistance agency, 211, or your utility using the number on your real bill. And never put your full Social Security number or your bank login into a public AI tool, and never pay in gift cards or hand your information to a caller threatening to shut off your power. Hear the heart of it twice, because it is built for someone in exactly this spot: real energy assistance is free to apply for, the real power company does not demand gift cards or crypto in the next thirty minutes, and no legitimate process needs your full SSN and your bank login handed to a stranger to stop a shutoff — so if someone is demanding instant payment in gift cards, charging an upfront fee to get your bill paid, or fishing for your information, hang up. And do not confuse that scam with a real notice — your actual shutoff notice and a real deadline from your real utility are genuine; do not ignore them, and call the number on the real bill or apply through the real agency or 211. Route the real help to real, trusted places: the LIHEAP / energy-assistance agency and 211 to apply at no cost; your utility's official number on your real bill for payment plans and hardship options; family, a mentor, and your church for the weight of doing it alone; and 988 if the fear ever gets too heavy.

A real woman on a fixed income, taught how to make the same tool the scammers already use sit with her at the kitchen table, explain the words nobody explained, help her get ready to apply, find the energy-assistance agency and her utility's real hardship line and 211, and spot the gift-card call and the fake-assistance site for exactly what they are — so she can keep the power on and not get robbed on the way there.

The women and men in our churches are not stuck because they did not try hard enough. They are stuck because a past-due bill can feel like a private shame, because nobody ever told them help to keep the power on exists and applying is free, and because nobody ever told them the scary call demanding a gift card is not the power company.
