There is a woman at that church who owns her home now, and every young family that finds that out asks her the same question.

Nobody expected it of her. She rented her whole life. She raised her kids in apartments, moved when the rent jumped, painted over other people's nail holes, and never once let herself picture a house with her name on the deed, because she was sure it was not for someone like her. Not enough saved. Credit that was not perfect. She had heard you needed twenty percent down and a relative who could hand it to you, and she had neither, so she stopped thinking about it the way you stop thinking about weather on another planet. Then five years ago, at fifty-three, somebody at a workshop mentioned a free housing counselor and a program that helped with the down payment, and she almost did not go, and she went, and eighteen months later she closed on her own house. Now a young couple at church, or a single mother who thinks she will rent until she dies, pulls her aside after service and asks, quiet, like it is a secret: how did you do it?

And then the hard part starts. Because getting yourself through that door once is one thing. Holding the whole homeownership maze in your head for every family who is certain the door is bolted shut is another, and nobody hands a working woman a map to it.

Because you do not need twenty percent down — that is the myth that stops more families than any bank ever did, and there are loans that ask for as little as three percent and some that ask for nothing, and the family repeating the twenty-percent number to each other never finds that out. Because the state has real money set aside to help with the down payment and the closing costs — thousands of dollars, sometimes forgiven if you stay — and most buyers who would qualify have never heard the program's name. Because there is a counselor whose help is completely free, approved by HUD, whose whole job is to sit with you, look at your real numbers, get your credit ready, and match you to the programs — and families pay strangers for that or, worse, never get it at all. Because the credit score that feels like a locked gate is often lower than what these loans actually require, and a thin file or no file can be built with a few deliberate months, and the woman who assumes she is disqualified never even asks. Because the homebuyer-education class everyone treats as a hoop is often the very thing that unlocks the assistance money. Because once you own, the county gives you an exemption that lowers the tax bill, and seniors get a bigger one, and families leave that sitting there unclaimed for years. Because if you already own and you have fallen behind, a counselor can get on the phone with your mortgage company and work it out before it becomes a foreclosure — but only if you call early instead of hiding from the letters. Because the "rent-to-own" and the "contract for deed" that feel like a shortcut to owning are, more often than anyone admits, a way to take your money for years and leave you with nothing. Because the flyer that says we buy houses, cash, any condition is sometimes a neighbor helping and sometimes a company stripping an elder's equity for a fraction of what it is worth. Because lending and appraisal discrimination are not history — a house can be valued lower because of who owns it or where it sits — and you are allowed to challenge it. And because underneath all of it is that quiet belief that owning is for other people, when the truth is the door was built to open and somebody just made sure nobody told you where the handle was.

So the woman tries to hold all of it. In her head. From what she went through herself five years ago, when the programs had different names and the numbers were different. She tells a young couple the wrong down-payment figure and they walk away sure they cannot afford it. She forgets to mention the free counselor and a family pays a fee they never owed. She hears about a new assistance program and cannot remember if it is still open or who to call. She is one woman, holding the door for other families out of her own memory of a system that renames its programs and moves its money faster than anyone working a job and raising kids can keep track of.

So the money the state set aside to help a family buy stays in the account nobody applied to. The young couple keeps renting and keeps writing a check every month that builds someone else's wealth, sure that owning was never for them. The credit gets no better because nobody said it could. The homeowner exemption goes unclaimed and the tax bill runs higher than it had to. The family that fell behind waits too long, too ashamed to call, until the counselor who could have saved the house is calling too late. And the elder signs the cash offer because the flyer got to her before the truth did.

Here is the thing about a family with resources: the door was always held open for them. They have an agent who knows every program. They have a loan officer who calls them back. They have someone whose whole job is to prep the credit, catch the assistance money, read the fine print, and walk them from the first question to the closing table. Owning is not a maze they have to solve alone, because there was always a hand on the small of their back guiding them through it. That family is not smarter than the woman in the pew who did it by herself at fifty-three. The door was just never locked for them the way it was locked for her.

She can have that hand too. For free. On the same phone she almost talked herself out of using five years ago.

She can find out the real down payment for a real loan and stop families from disqualifying themselves over a number that was never true. She can point a young couple to the free HUD-approved counselor instead of a stranger with a fee. She can learn which down-payment programs are open right now and who to call. She can help someone see that their credit is closer to ready than they feared, and lay out the few months of moves that get it there. She can flag the homeowner exemption so the tax bill drops. She can tell the family that fell behind to call the counselor today, not next month, and warn them off the upfront-fee "rescue" that would only take what little they have left. She can help someone read a contract-for-deed and see the trap before they sign it. She can have the whole maze — the myth-free down payment, the assistance, the counselor, the credit steps, the class, the exemption, the foreclosure help — broken into an order of operations she can start this week, and then walk each family straight to a HUD-approved counselor, the state housing authority, an approved lender, and the county assessor who carry it the rest of the way.

Not to replace what she does. The question whispered after service is hers. The reason a young mother trusts her enough to admit she has given up on owning is hers, and no machine will ever have painted over another landlord's nail holes for thirty years and then stood in its own kitchen holding its own keys. It only takes the maze off her shoulders so she can do the part only she can do, which is to look a tired renter in the eye and say the thing she needed somebody to say to her: this is not just for other people, this door has your name on it too, and we are going to find the handle together.

That is the gap. It was never that the families in our pews want it less or deserve it less than the family for whom the door was always open. It is that that family had someone paid to walk them through, and ours got one faithful woman holding the door with the memory of how she got through it once, five years ago, when everything was named something else.

A word of care, plainly: a HUD-approved housing counselor is always free, and down-payment assistance is real and free to find — so anyone charging you an upfront fee to "save your house," to guarantee a loan modification, or to "get you approved" is a red flag, because that help exists for nothing. Be slow with anything that lets you "own" without a mortgage in your name — rent-to-own and contract-for-deed can take your money for years and leave you with no house — and read every line, or better, let a counselor read it with you before you sign. If a "we buy houses" offer shows up for an elder's paid-off home, get a second set of eyes before anyone signs anything. And if you believe a loan was denied, priced, or appraised unfairly because of who you are or where you live, that is not something you have to accept — you can ask the lender for a reconsideration of the value and you can file a fair-housing complaint. This is general navigation, not financial, mortgage, legal, or real-estate advice — helping someone learn that a program exists is not the same as being their loan officer or housing counselor — so always route the actual programs, the current rules, and the loan itself to a HUD-approved housing counseling agency (find one through HUD's Find a Housing Counselor tool or the HUD line 1-800-569-4287), to the Illinois Housing Development Authority at ihda.org and an IHDA-participating lender, and to the Cook County Assessor at cookcountyassessor.com for the homeowner exemptions. For lending or appraisal discrimination, a local fair-housing center or the HUD fair-housing line at 1-800-669-9777 can help, and 2-1-1 can point to local resources.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities and the people navigating resources beside them in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Matteson. Dolton. Not a webinar. A room. Real problems. A real family that stopped believing owning was for other people, a real down-payment program that got claimed instead of expiring unused, a real house with a real family's name on the deed.

The people holding the door open for other families by hand are not behind. They were just never handed the tools the resourced never even have to reach for.

We are here to hand them over.
