There is a woman in that church who cuts her own pills in half. Thirty-nine years old, a mother, and she lost the health insurance that came with the job she lost, and now she stretches one prescription across two months and quietly skips her own checkups so there is a little more room to keep the kids seen — and she has told no one, because she is ashamed to say out loud that she is a grown working woman with no coverage and no idea how to get any.

You know her. She did not choose to be uninsured. A job ended, and the insurance ended with it, the way it does — no ceremony, just a letter and a last day and suddenly every ache is a math problem. She is certain of two things, and both of them are hurting her. The first is that real coverage is simply unaffordable, a number she cannot look at without her stomach dropping. The second is that whatever help exists is for other people, not for someone like her — too much paperwork, too many rules, some door that was never meant to open for a mother between jobs in the south suburbs. So she does not look. She prays nobody spikes a fever on a weekend, rations what she has, and carries the whole thing alone.

And she may be walking past coverage she could actually have. That is the part that has to be said plainly, and I will say it again before I am done: legitimate help enrolling in Medicaid or a marketplace plan is free, no one charges a fee to sign you up, and no honest health plan is sold by a high-pressure phone call demanding a card number on the spot. She does not know that yet. She does not know her children may qualify for free or low-cost coverage right now. She does not know her own plan through the marketplace might cost a fraction of the number in her head once a subsidy is figured in. She does not know that losing job-based insurance usually opens a special window to enroll even outside the once-a-year season — a window with a clock on it. Nobody sat her down and told her. So she assumes the door is locked and never tries the handle.

And there are doors. Real ones. Doors nobody names for a thirty-nine-year-old mother who is too proud to say she is scared and too tired to fight a system she assumes was built to say no.

Start with the first thing she needs to hear: losing your insurance because you lost a job does not make you irresponsible, and rationing your own medicine so your kids stay covered is not a failing — it is a mother doing the most responsible, most self-sacrificing thing she knows how to do with the little she was left. Not knowing whether you qualify for Medicaid or a subsidy is not stupidity; it is the exact confusion a complicated system counts on, the same confusion the scammers count on too. And needing help to enroll is not weakness. It is using the free help that already exists and was built for exactly this moment.

So here are the doors almost no one names for her plainly. The first is the one her fear hides completely: she and especially her children may qualify for free or low-cost coverage through Medicaid or a children's health program based on income and household size, and that eligibility can be checked for free — governed by state and federal rules, so confirm it with the state agency or a navigator, but do not assume the answer is no. The second is the one that shrinks the terrifying number: a plan through the health insurance marketplace — healthcare.gov or her state's own site — may cost far less than she expects because of premium tax credits and subsidies tied to income, which is why it is worth an actual look instead of a guess in the dark. The third is the one with a clock on it: losing job-based coverage is usually a life event that opens a special enrollment window even outside the yearly open-enrollment period, but those windows have time limits, so it is worth checking now rather than waiting. The fourth is the one that costs her nothing to use: free, in-person and phone help exists to enroll — marketplace navigators and certified assisters, community health centers, and the official marketplace call line — none of whom charge her a dime to apply. The fifth is the one for right now, before any paperwork clears: community health centers and sliding-scale clinics can see her and her kids on income-based fees while coverage is being sorted, so care does not have to wait on a form. And underneath all of it, for the shame of being a mother scared of the next fever, there are trusted mentors, her faith, one call to 211, and 988 if the fear ever gets too heavy to hold alone. She is standing in front of a whole row of doors — Medicaid for the kids, a subsidized plan for herself, a special window open right now, free help to walk through it, and care in the meantime — and she is frozen certain there are none, because nobody ever told her a single one was hers.

But hear this warning before she buys a single thing off a phone call or an ad, because the scams hunt the uninsured and the frightened harder than almost anyone. Hear it plainly, and I will say it again at the end: real enrollment help is free, and nobody legitimate charges you a fee to sign up for coverage. Beware the slick caller or the too-good ad selling "affordable health coverage" that turns out to be a limited-benefit or discount plan that pays almost nothing the day she is actually sick — real marketplace coverage is not sold by a high-pressure robocall. Beware anyone charging an "enrollment," "processing," or "activation" fee to sign her up for Medicaid or a marketplace plan — official help is free, and Medicaid does not charge a sign-up fee. Beware the caller or text pressing for her full Social Security number, her bank account, or a card to "confirm" or "release" her benefits — real enrollment happens on the official site or with a certified navigator, not by handing your Social and your bank to a stranger who called you. The rule is flat: legitimate help enrolling is free, no one charges a fee to sign you up, and no honest health plan is sold by a high-pressure call demanding a card number or your full SSN on the spot — so anyone charging an enrollment fee, pushing a "limited-benefit" plan by phone, or demanding your SSN and a payment to "activate" coverage is the danger, not the doorway.

Now one more thing, because it matters and the scam hides right next to it: a real official notice is not the same as a scam call. If the state Medicaid agency or the marketplace sends her a real letter asking her to verify her income or renew her coverage, that is genuine paperwork with a real deadline, and ignoring it can cost her the very coverage she needs — so she must not treat every envelope like a robocall. Part of the help, a big part, is simply telling the official letter apart from the sham pitch — and that is exactly the kind of thing to bring to a certified navigator, not to guess at alone.

So she almost fell for it. Not because she is careless — careless is not the word for a mother splitting her own pills so her children stay seen. Not because she is naive — she is careful with every dollar, careful enough that a caller promising cheap coverage nearly got a card number out of her before payday. She almost fell for it because nobody ever walked her to the real doors, and the only voice offering her coverage was the one built to sell her nothing.

Here is the thing about the woman across town. Same story. Same lost job, same insurance gone with it, same split pills, same certainty that coverage was for other people. But a friend from church sat down next to her and said: "Before you buy anything off a phone call, go to the real marketplace site and let a free navigator check whether you and the kids qualify for Medicaid or a subsidy. Don't pay anybody a fee to enroll — that part is free." So she did. She found out the children qualified for free coverage, and her own plan through the marketplace cost a fraction of the number that had been keeping her up at night once the subsidy was figured in. She enrolled with a navigator who never charged her a cent, and she filled her prescription that month — the whole thing, not half of it. She was not smarter than the woman in our pew. She was not luckier. She had one person who knew the doors were real and free, and who named them before the fear and the fake ad won. One mother got her family covered. The other almost paid an "enrollment fee" for a junk plan that would have covered nothing. The whole difference was somebody who said "let a free navigator check, and don't pay to enroll," and a silence where nobody ever did.

The woman in our pew can be met before she pays that first fake fee. Not with a lecture about how she should have kept a job with benefits, or signed up sooner, or figured out the marketplace by now. With a hand on her shoulder and the plain truth that coverage might be within reach, that her kids may already qualify, and that the help to find out is free.

Because this is where a tool like AI actually meets a woman like her — quietly, at the kitchen table, after the kids are down, on a night she finally has the nerve to look — doing the part that the not-knowing has been blocking. It can be the patient someone that explains what "Medicaid," "the Marketplace," "premium," "deductible," "subsidy," "special enrollment period," "the coverage gap," and "limited-benefit plan" actually mean, in plain words, as many times as she needs, and never once make a tired working mother feel foolish for not already knowing the vocabulary of a system designed to be confusing. It can help her figure out where to even start, and find the official sites and a local navigator she can reach herself. It can help her gather what she will need to apply so she is not scrambling. It can help her read a plan letter or a notice she has been too anxious to open and find what it is actually asking. And it can name the fake-plan pitch, the "enrollment fee," and the SSN-phishing text for exactly what they are, before they take one more dollar or one more night's sleep from a mother who is already doing everything she can.

Not to be the one who decides anything. Let that be plain. The machine does not enroll her, and it does not get to decide what she or her kids qualify for or which plan she should pick — that belongs to the marketplace, the state Medicaid agency, and a certified navigator or licensed agent, not a chatbot's guess. It is not a doctor, it is not an insurance agent, it is not a navigator. And it must never, ever be fed her full Social Security number, her bank login, or her account numbers — a public AI tool is not the place for a single digit of that. It is only the patient someone that takes a frightening, shame-soaked, jargon-soaked moment and makes it walkable, so that a good mother who lost a job does not go one more month splitting pills, or hand a card number to a stranger selling coverage that isn't real.

That is the gap. It was never that the woman across town deserved it more, or was smarter, or managed better. Same age. Same lost job. Same fear for the same kids. It is that one woman had a friend who said "let a free navigator check, don't pay to enroll," and the other was left alone with half a bottle of pills, a number that terrified her, and a fake ad that felt like the only door. Not more worthy. Not more careful. Just never told the doors were hers.

A word of care, plainly, and I will say the important parts twice. This is general navigation, not medical, insurance, or legal advice, and not a determination of what you or your family qualify for or which plan is right for you. Eligibility, subsidies, and enrollment are governed by state and federal rules and decided by the marketplace, the state Medicaid agency, and a certified navigator or licensed agent — not by a machine and not by a guess. AI tools can be confidently wrong, so never trust an eligibility answer, a cost, a subsidy amount, or a deadline one gives you without confirming it with the official marketplace at healthcare.gov (or your state's site), the state Medicaid agency, and a free certified navigator. And never put your full Social Security number, a bank login, or account numbers into a public AI tool, and never pay a fee or a card number to a caller to "enroll" or "activate" coverage. Hear the heart of it twice, because it is built for someone in exactly this spot: legitimate help enrolling in Medicaid or a marketplace plan is free, no one charges a fee to sign you up, and no honest health plan is sold by a high-pressure robocall demanding a card number or your full SSN — so if a "plan" is charging you to enroll, pushing a limited-benefit plan by phone, or demanding your SSN and payment to activate, hang up. And do not confuse that scam with a real official notice — if the Medicaid agency or the marketplace sends you a genuine letter to verify income or renew, do not ignore it; answer it and get to a navigator fast. Route the real help to real, trusted places: the official marketplace at healthcare.gov or your state's site to look and enroll; the state Medicaid agency for you and the kids; a free marketplace navigator or certified assister to walk you through it; a community health center or sliding-scale clinic for care in the meantime; a trusted mentor and your faith community for the shame and the fear; your church's benevolence fund for a true gap; 988 if the weight gets too heavy; and 211 or 211.org to find local health, enrollment, and clinic help in one call.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Dolton. Robbins. Chicago Heights. Matteson. South Holland. Not a webinar. A room. Real people. A real uninsured mother, taught how to make the same tool the plan-sellers and the scammers already use sit with her at the kitchen table, explain the words nobody explained, help her find the official site and a free navigator, gather what she needs to apply, read the letter she was afraid to open, and spot the fake-plan pitch and the enrollment-fee scam for exactly what they are — so she can find out what she and her kids actually qualify for and not get sold nothing on the way to it.

The men and women in our churches are not going uninsured because they were careless or refused to take care of their families. They are going uninsured because a job ended and the coverage ended with it, and because nobody ever told them free or low-cost coverage might be within reach, the help to enroll is free, and no honest plan is ever sold by a caller charging a fee.

We are here to sit with them before they pay that fee for a plan that isn't real.
