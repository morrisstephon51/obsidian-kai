**Resources:**
- **Illinois Helpline for Opioids & Other Substances** — **1-833-2FINDHELP (1-833-234-6343)** / **HelplineIL.org** — free, confidential, 24/7; a real person connects you to open treatment and medication that takes your coverage. **No fee. No "deposit." No middleman.**
- **Free naloxone (Narcan)** — free **by mail** from Illinois (search **NaloxoneForAll** / the IDPH Drug Overdose Prevention Program), free at **many pharmacies with no prescription**, and free at local harm-reduction programs. **Narcan is genuinely free — nobody real sells it to you.**
- **Illinois "Good Samaritan" overdose law** — if you **call 911 for an overdose**, the law gives the caller *and* the person overdosing **limited protection from arrest/charges for small-amount drug possession.** Calling saves a life. **The fear of arrest is the lie that kills.**
- **Medicaid covers substance-use treatment** — detox, residential/inpatient, outpatient, and **medication for opioid use disorder (MAT — buprenorphine/Suboxone, methadone, naltrexone/Vivitrol).** MAT is **real medicine, not "trading one drug for another."** Your care is billed to Medicaid — **not to a debit card or Cash App.**
- **988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline** — **call or text 988** — free, 24/7, for a crisis right now.
- **SAMHSA National Helpline** — **1-800-662-4357** — free, confidential, 24/7 national treatment referral; and **FindTreatment.gov** to find licensed programs.
- **A local FQHC / community health center or CCBHC** — sliding scale, takes Medicaid, and many can **start MAT** — find one at **findahealthcenter.hrsa.gov**
- **2-1-1** — free, 24/7 referral to local treatment, recovery homes, and support.
- **FTC Fraud Reporting** — reportfraud.ftc.gov / **1-877-382-4357** — report "free rehab / fly-you-out" call centers, fake "bed-hold" deposits, and Narcan-for-a-fee sites.
- **IdentityTheft.gov** — **1-877-438-4338** — if you handed over a **Medicaid ID, Social Security number, or a card.**
- **Illinois Attorney General — Consumer Fraud / Health Care Bureau** — **1-800-386-5438**

**Scam spine:** A family in the narrow window where someone they love has finally said *yes* is one of the most targeted, most desperate buyers on the internet — and the fraud industry that feeds on addiction is built precisely to strike in that window. **(1) The "free luxury rehab — we'll fly you out today" body-brokering call center:** a slick voice (often bought from your own late-night search for "rehab near me") promises a same-day bed at a faraway "campus" and a free flight, then (a) harvests the person's **Medicaid or insurance ID** to bill fraudulent charges against, (b) demands a **"refundable bed-hold deposit"** by Cash App, Zelle, or gift card, and (c) ships a sick person to an out-of-state flophouse where relapse is *the business model* — because a person who relapses can be readmitted and billed again. **(2) The sober-living "deposit / bed-hold fee" scam:** *"pay $600 tonight to hold the last room"* — for a house that is a bed in name only, or does not exist. **(3) The "pay the intake fee now or lose the bed" imposter:** urgency plus an untraceable payment — gift card, Cash App, wire — is the whole tell. **(4) Narcan-for-a-fee / fake overdose kits:** selling, for $40 or $45, the exact thing the state mails to your door for free. **(5) The fake "recovery coach / peer-specialist certification" upfront fee:** preying on people *in* recovery who want to give back, charging for a "credential" that isn't one. **(6) The imposter "treatment navigator":** a call or text demanding a **Social Security or Medicaid number to "verify eligibility"** before "releasing" a bed. Every one of them does the same evil thing: it turns the single most fragile, most hopeful moment in a family's life — the moment someone reaches for a door — into the moment they get robbed of money, of an identity, of a Medicaid number that funds someone else's fraud, or worst of all, of the *window itself,* because by the time the deposit clears and the "flight" never comes, the *yes* is gone. **No real program charges a family to hold a bed, sells free Narcan, or needs a bank card to admit a dying person. The instant the help asks you to pay to save your child, the help is the danger.**

**The $1,200 "Bed-Hold Deposit," the Window of Yes, and the Mother Who Buried Her Son and Learned the Map.**

Terrell Hayes said the word on a Sunday, in a back pew, in a suit that no longer fit, three weeks after his sister brought him back from the dead with a nasal spray she got for free and had almost thrown away.

He didn't say it to the pastor. He said it to his mother, low, during the last hymn, not looking at her: *"I can't do this no more, Mama. I want to stop. For real this time. Help me before I change my mind."*

Gloria Hayes had been praying that exact sentence back to God for four years. And the second she heard it, every cell in her body understood one thing with animal clarity: *the window is open and it will not stay open.* She had lived through the mornings when the midnight *yes* was gone by breakfast. She was not going to lose this one to a waiting room.

So while the benediction was still going, she was in the vestibule with her phone, typing with shaking thumbs: *rehab open today near me.*

---

The phone rang back in four minutes.

The voice was kind. The voice was calm. The voice said its name was a recovery-campus admissions coordinator and that it could *hear how much you love your son, ma'am,* and that it had *one bed left* at a beautiful facility, and that they could have Terrell on a flight *tonight* and into treatment by morning.

"We work with all insurance," the voice said. "He's on Medicaid? Perfect, we take that. I just need his Medicaid ID number to verify his benefits — and to lock in that last bed before it's gone, we take a refundable twelve-hundred-dollar bed-hold deposit. Cash App is fastest. Can you do that in the next few minutes? I'd hate for someone else to get this bed while we're talking."

Gloria had $1,200. She had exactly $1,200, because it was the money she had been quietly setting aside for this exact day for two years. Her son was thirty feet away in a pew saying yes for the first time in years. And a kind voice was telling her the yes had a clock on it and the clock was *right now.*

Her thumb was on the app. A text buzzed in under the call — *"Sober living, private room, $600 move-in to hold it tonight, act fast."*

That is the moment Mother Loretta Gaines came across the aisle.

---

She did not lunge for the phone. Loretta Gaines had learned, in six years of doing this, that you do not snatch anything out of the hand of a terrified mother. You get beside her.

"Sister Gloria." Quiet. A hand on her shoulder. "Who's on the phone, baby?"

"They have a bed. For Terrell. Today. There's only one left, I have to send the deposit *right now*—"

Loretta looked at the screen. She saw the words *bed-hold deposit.* She saw *Cash App.* She saw *Medicaid ID to verify.* And she saw, underneath, the second text about the $600 room.

She had seen every one of these before. She had *almost sent one herself,* six years and one dead son ago.

"Gloria. Look at me." She waited until the woman's eyes came up off the phone. "I am going to say something and I need you to hear the order of it, because I know that clock in your chest is screaming. Terrell saying yes is real. That's the miracle. *That* clock is real." She put her hand flat over the phone screen, gently, the way you'd cover a candle. "But that voice on the phone is not the help. That voice is a *robbery* that learned the sound of a mother's hope. And the real door — the free one, the one they're standing in front of so you can't see it — I can walk you to it before your son gets his coat."

"But they said—"

"They said *pay to hold a bed.* Baby." Loretta's voice cracked, just once, and steadied. "No real place makes you pay a deposit by Cash App to save a dying man. No real place needs his Medicaid number read out over the phone in a church hallway. They want that number so they can *bill* it — that's their whole business. And that 'free flight'? Half of those flights land at a flophouse two thousand miles from his daughter, where they *want* him to relapse, because a relapse is another bill. I have gone and *found* people in those places, Gloria. I brought a boy back from one in Florida in a Greyhound. Put the twelve hundred down."

Gloria put the phone down.

**Here is the map Mother Loretta laid out at the folding table in the fellowship hall — the one she wishes to God somebody had laid out for her before there was a grave with Andre's name on it:**

**1. Treatment is covered. Medicaid pays for it. The "twenty thousand dollars" is a wall that isn't real.**

"Every version of this I hear starts the same way," Loretta said. "*Rehab's thirty grand, we don't have it, so what's the point.* Baby, that wall is *painted on.* Illinois Medicaid covers substance-use treatment — detox, a residential bed, outpatient, and the medication. Terrell's care gets billed to Medicaid. Which means the coverage was there the whole time. They just got to you before the truth did."

Terrell, at the end of the table, spoke for the first time. "So I don't gotta pay."

"You don't gotta pay, and your mama don't gotta empty a jar she's been filling since you were twenty-seven."

**2. You can start today. One free call. And it's a *real* one.**

Loretta wrote a number on a napkin in block letters. "Eighteen-thirty-three. Two. Find. Help. 1-833-2FINDHELP. That's the Illinois Helpline. It answers day or night, it's free, and a *real* person picks up and finds you a real bed or a real clinic that takes his Medicaid — sometimes same day, sometimes it's a spot in a program near enough that his daughter can still see him. She underlined it twice. "This is the number you keep in your phone *before* the next hard night. Because there will be hard nights. And the answer to a hard night is never the fastest voice on the phone. It's *this* number, that you already trust."

**3. The medicine is medicine. Don't let anybody — including the voice in his own head — call it weakness.**

"There's medication for this. Suboxone, methadone, the Vivitrol shot." Loretta said it plainly. "The street'll tell you that's just swapping one drug for another. **4. Narcan is free. It goes in every bag in this family. Today.**

Loretta reached into her purse and set two boxes on the table — the same kind Nia had used on the bathroom floor. "The state *mails* this to your house for free. Free. Carrying it is not doubting him. It's *covering* him."

**5. If it happens again — and pray it doesn't — you call 911. Every time. The law protects the caller.**

This was the one Loretta had to sit down to say.

"The day Terrell went down, somebody in that room said *don't call, we'll get locked up.*" She let that sit. "Gloria, that sentence is the sentence that killed my Andre. There were people around my son and they were *scared,* and by the time scared lost to love it was too late. So hear me: Illinois has a Good Samaritan law. You call 911 for an overdose, the law *protects* the person who called and the person who's down from getting charged for a little bit of possession. They wrote that law *because* fear was killing people. So there is no more *don't call.* In this family, forever, we *call.* A living son can go to treatment. We can't do a single thing for the other kind."

**6. And everything with a deposit, a gift card, a Cash App, or a Medicaid number over the phone — that's the scam. Full stop.**

Loretta tapped the phone, still face-down on the table. "Write the rule down so it's bigger than any smooth voice: *nobody real charges you to hold a bed. Nobody real sells you free Narcan. Nobody real needs his Medicaid number read out to admit him. And nobody real puts a clock on your grief to rush your money out of you.* The rushing *is* the tell. Real help will still be real help in an hour. Robbery is the only thing that can't wait."

And in a purse in the third pew of a Missionary Baptist church in Crestwood, next to the peppermints, there are still two boxes of free naloxone and a laminated card, carried by a mother who turned the worst thing that can happen to a parent into a map — so that the next Gloria, in the next window of yes, gets walked to the free door instead of robbed at the fake one.

**For deacons, recovery / "Second Chance" ministries, benevolence teams, and every church mother carrying somebody back from the edge:**

The family in your congregation with a son or daughter in active addiction is often the quietest family in the building — because shame keeps them silent until there is a crisis or a casket. When their loved one finally says *yes,* that yes has a shelf life measured in *hours,* and in those hours a desperate parent will type "rehab today" into a phone and hand their hope to whoever calls back fastest. What calls back fastest is very often a body-brokering call center that wants a **Cash App "bed-hold deposit"** and a **Medicaid ID to bill against,** a **sober-home "$600 to hold the room" scam,** or a site selling **free Narcan for $45.**

Your recovery ministry, your deacon board, your prayer warriors, and your church mothers are frequently the *first* — sometimes the *only* — people who know the crisis is happening at all. You will be in the room before the treatment center is. What you know in that room can be the difference between a free door and a robbery, between a phone call and a funeral.
