# Post #337

---

The call from his daughter came at 2:47 in the morning.

Darnell is 64. Retired CTA bus driver — thirty-one years on the 63rd Street route, which means thirty-one years of knowing exactly which corner someone boards with their head down and which corner they're heading somewhere that means something. He retired two years ago with a pension he was proud of, plans to refinish his basement, and a garage full of tools he finally had time to use.

He has not touched the garage in four months.

His daughter Tamika — she's 34 — has been fighting a methamphetamine addiction for seven years. She was doing better. Then she wasn't. The call in February was from the Cook County Jail. The children — Marcus, 11, and Jaylen, 9 — were home alone when she was arrested. The neighbor who checked on them called the DCFS hotline, and DCFS called Darnell.

He said yes before the worker finished the sentence.

Marcus and Jaylen have been at his house in Englewood since February. His wife Deborah has rearranged everything: the bedroom schedule, the grocery list, the after-school pickup, the parent-teacher conferences. They enrolled the boys in the neighborhood school without knowing what they were legally allowed to do. They are using Darnell's pension and Deborah's home health aide income — she's 61, still working — to cover everything.

They have received nothing from the state. They did not know there was anything to receive.

---

**What Darnell and Deborah did not know:**

Illinois has a legal and financial support infrastructure specifically designed for relatives doing exactly what Darnell and Deborah are doing. Almost none of it is well publicized. None of it arrived with the DCFS worker who helped place the boys.

**Illinois Kinship Care Program (IDHS):** When a child cannot live with their parent and a relative steps in — without formal DCFS foster care licensing — the state has a Kinship Care program that provides a monthly cash payment to help offset the cost of caring for the child. It is funded through Illinois TANF money and administered through IDHS. The payment is not enormous, but it is not nothing — and the family does not need to be involved in a formal foster care case to qualify. The child must be living with the relative because the parent is unable to care for them. Darnell and Deborah qualify. They do not need to be licensed foster parents. They need to apply at their local IDHS Family Community Resource Center or through ABE at abe.illinois.gov, or call 800-843-6154.

**Illinois DCFS Guardianship Assistance Program (GAP):** For relatives who become licensed relative foster caregivers and then take permanent legal guardianship of a child from the foster care system, the state provides ongoing monthly financial support — often called a GAP subsidy — along with Medicaid for the child, until the child turns 18 (or 21 if the young person meets certain conditions). If Tamika's case remains open and DCFS has any formal custody of the children, Darnell and Deborah may be asked to become licensed relative caregivers. That licensing process includes background checks and a home study but also comes with support services and financial assistance that informal arrangements do not provide.

**Medicaid for the children:** Marcus and Jaylen almost certainly qualify for Illinois Medicaid (All Kids) as children in a relative's care. Darnell can apply for them through ABE at abe.illinois.gov or by calling 800-843-6154. Children's Medicaid is entirely separate from whether Darnell and Deborah have health insurance. The boys' medical and dental care should be fully covered while they are in his household. This should have been set up in February. It was not.

**School enrollment rights:** Under Illinois School Code, a grandparent or relative who has actual physical care of a school-age child — even without legal guardianship — has the right to enroll that child in school. Most Chicago Public Schools and suburban Cook County districts will accept an affidavit of residency from the relative caregiver. If a school is refusing to enroll Marcus or Jaylen without a court order, that refusal may be legally improper. Legal aid organizations can intervene directly with the district.

**Legal guardianship of a minor:** If Darnell wants the legal authority to make medical, educational, and long-term decisions for his grandsons — and the protection that comes from having that authority in writing and in a court order — he can petition the Cook County Circuit Court, Probate Division, for appointment as guardian of a minor. The process is manageable. The Cook County Circuit Court Self-Help Center at the Richard J. Daley Center (69 W. Washington, Chicago) has the forms. For families with limited income, **Legal Aid Chicago (LAF)** at 312-341-1070 and **Cabrini Green Legal Aid** at 312-738-2452 provide free guardianship assistance to eligible clients. **CARPLS** at 312-738-9200 provides free civil legal advice over the phone and can help Darnell understand whether filing for guardianship now is the right step or whether to wait on the outcome of Tamika's DCFS case.

**Illinois Kinship Navigator Program:** Illinois has a Kinship Navigator program — a federally funded resource that connects grandparents and relative caregivers to the full range of support programs available to them: DCFS involvement, licensing options, court processes, financial assistance, and community support. Statewide navigation is available through the **Illinois Kinship Support Collaborative** and through regional Area Agencies on Aging.

**AgeOptions (Area Agency on Aging, suburban Cook County):** 708-383-0258. AgeOptions runs programming specifically for grandparents and older relatives raising grandchildren — support groups, connections to legal resources, navigation of state programs, and respite options. For families in Chicago proper, the Chicago Department of Family and Support Services (DFSS) at 312-744-5000 connects grandparents to similar resources.

**DCFS Placement / Relative Caregiver contact:** If Tamika's case is still open and DCFS has formal involvement, the assigned caseworker is a primary contact. If contact has lapsed or Darnell is not receiving updates on the case status, he can call the DCFS Placement line at 312-814-6800. He can also request a formal meeting with the DCFS supervisor to clarify what the children's legal status is and what support he is entitled to as their relative caregiver.

**The warning before you pay $1,500 to anyone:**

Informal kinship arrangements — grandparents and relatives stepping in without going through the courts or DCFS — are the largest category of child welfare arrangements in Illinois. They are also the least served by the state's formal system. The combination of urgent circumstances, an unfamiliar legal process, and families who will do anything to protect the children creates a predictable opening.

**The fake "legal guardianship service":** A company — sometimes presenting itself as a legal firm, sometimes as a "family advocacy service" — approaches relatives in Darnell's situation and offers to "file for guardianship" for a flat fee: $800, $1,200, $1,500. It may have a professional-looking website, a paralegal who answers the phone, and testimonials.

Here is the problem. In Illinois, guardianship of a minor is a court process. A non-attorney cannot legally represent you in Cook County Circuit Court. A company that charges you to "file your paperwork" is either helping you fill out forms that are freely available at the courthouse self-help center — or it is filing something that a free legal aid attorney would have caught a problem with before it was submitted. The filing fees at the Probate Division are modest and waivable for low-income families. The forms are free. The self-help center is free. Legal Aid is free for eligible families. The $1,500 fee is not buying anything you cannot already access without paying it.

**The fake "DCFS navigation consultant":** A person — sometimes a former case aide, sometimes someone with no formal credentials — presents themselves as a specialist who can "get you through DCFS" and help you become a licensed relative foster caregiver faster, with better outcomes, for a monthly fee. DCFS has a formal licensing process for relative caregivers. It assigns licensing workers to assist families through it. It is not something a fee-charging consultant can accelerate or improve in ways a DCFS licensing worker does not already provide for free. If someone is billing you $200 a month for "case navigation," they are charging for something your assigned worker does at no cost.

**The fake "kinship benefits consultant":** Someone tells Darnell there is a "state grant" or "federal kinship fund" he can access for a "processing fee" or "application assistance fee." There is no such fee. The Illinois Kinship Care Program, the DCFS GAP subsidy, and Medicaid all have state application processes that charge nothing and require no intermediary. Anyone charging a fee to apply is misrepresenting the process.

**The predatory kinship "coaching" platform:** An app or subscription service charges $19.99 or $39.99 a month for "personalized guidance" on kinship care benefits, case navigation, and legal resources. The Illinois Kinship Navigator program — free — provides this personalized navigation. The Area Agency on Aging — free — provides this support. CARPLS — free — provides legal guidance over the phone. The subscription is billing you for access to a process the state already built without a payment requirement.

**Beware anyone who contacts you first.** The families most likely to be targeted are the ones who mentioned their situation at church, in a Facebook group, in a neighborhood conversation. Legitimate services — Legal Aid, DCFS, the Kinship Navigator, AgeOptions — do not initiate outreach to specific families with offers of help in exchange for a fee. If someone reaches out to you after you shared your situation, and then quotes you a price, that is the transaction you should not make.
