There is a man in that church who sits near the back with his kids and always drops something in the plate even when you can tell it is a hard month. He is thirty-eight. He works — steady, the kind of steady that means he is tired in a way sleep does not fix — and this summer got away from him. The hours got cut for a stretch. The car needed the kind of repair you cannot say no to. A bill from the doctor ate the little cushion he had built. And the whole time the sun was punishing, so he ran the air for the kids because you do not let your children swelter, and the electric bill climbed, and the gas bill sat behind it, and a few days ago a notice showed up — the red kind, the kind with a date on it. And he did something quiet that nobody around him can see. He folded that notice in half and put it in a drawer and decided he was going to handle it alone. He is not going to ask anybody for help. Because in his mind, a man who provides does not let the lights go out on his children, and asking would be the same as admitting he already has.

You know him. He is not careless and he is not a freeloader — he is a working father who hit a rough patch, which is the single most ordinary thing that happens in a working household, and it caught up with him the way it catches up with millions of good people every single year. And the thing stopping him is not the rules. It is a story he was handed a long time ago about what a man is allowed to need. So he will not call the electric company, because he is half-convinced that calling attention to the past-due amount will only make them shut it off faster. He will not apply for energy assistance, because he is sure he makes too much to qualify and too proud to find out. He does not know that the utility itself has a payment plan he can simply ask for. He does not know that energy assistance is real, and free to apply for, and built for exactly the working family that had a bad summer. Nobody told him. So he assumes the only options are pay it all at once or sit in the dark, and that empty space between the two is exactly where the predators are waiting.

And that is the part that has to be said plainly, and I will say it again before I am done: a working father behind on the light bill did not fail his kids, the help is real and free to ask for, his own utility will work with him if he calls, and no honest helper demands he pay a shut-off in the next thirty minutes with a gift card or charges him an upfront fee for a free energy grant. Applying for energy assistance is free through the real county agency. The gas and electric companies have payment plans and hardship programs you can call and request. There are protections that keep certain households connected in certain conditions and seasons. He does not know any of that, because nobody sat a hard-working man down and told him that the safety net under his family's heat and lights is real and was partly paid for out of his own bills and his own taxes the whole time. And that not-knowing is the door the scammers are standing in.

And there are doors. Real ones. Doors nobody names for a father who has already quietly told himself no and folded the notice into a drawer.

Start with the first thing he needs to hear: a man who worked all summer and still fell behind after his hours got cut and the car broke and the doctor's bill landed did not come up short as a father. Falling behind on a utility bill is not a moral failure and it is not a verdict on his worth — it is one of the most common things that happens in a working home, and the help exists precisely for the working family that hit the rough patch. He likely already pays into the very funds that support that help, through the fees on his own bill and the taxes out of his own check. Not knowing that the assistance is free and that his own utility will work with him is not proof he manages money badly; it is only proof that no one ever handed him the truth. The voice in his head that says "a real man never asks, and asking means you already failed them" is not telling him the truth. It is the barrier itself, wearing a father's pride like a costume. Asking for a payment plan he is entitled to, or applying for help he qualifies for, is not weakness. It is keeping the heat on for his kids, which is the entire job.

So here are the doors almost no one names for him plainly. The first is the one he is most afraid of and needs the most: he calls the utility and asks for a payment plan. The electric and gas companies have deferred-payment arrangements, budget or level billing that spreads the cost out evenly, and hardship programs — and calling to ask does not make them shut it off faster, it is how a household stays connected. He confirms his options directly with the real utility, from the number on a real bill, not from a caller who reached him first. The second is the help he was sure he did not qualify for: energy assistance, LIHEAP, is free to apply for through the real county community-action agency — in suburban Cook County that intake runs through CEDA — and it can help with the bill and sometimes with getting service turned back on. He applies through the official program, not a link that lands in his texts. The third is that he does not have to fill out a single form alone: the community action agency, his own church, and the township office have people whose job is to help him gather his documents and complete the application at no cost, and free help is not lesser help. The fourth is knowing his protections: there are rules that shield households in certain conditions and certain seasons from disconnection, and he can ask the utility and the agency what applies to him instead of assuming the worst. And underneath all of it, for the shame of being the one who fell behind, there is his family, his church, a deacon, a neighbor who kept the lights on through a hard winter and will tell him exactly who to call — so he is not sitting in a warm house getting warmer in August, certain he is the only father this ever happened to. He is standing in front of a whole row of doors, and he is sure they are all charity a real man refuses, when most of them are simply his own utility and his own community doing the ordinary work of keeping a family connected.

But hear this warning before he or anyone in his house pays a cent to a caller or a stranger at the door, because the scams hunt a family with a past-due notice harder than almost anyone, precisely because the fear is loud and it is immediate — a man staring at a shut-off date will do things fast that he would never do slow. Hear it plainly, and I will say it again at the end: the real utility does not work this way. Beware the phone call that says it is the power company and his service will be cut in the next thirty minutes unless he pays right now with a prepaid card, gift cards, Zelle, or crypto — the real utility does not give thirty-minute ultimatums and does not take gift cards, and that call is a thief with a script. Beware the "government energy grant" outfit that wants an upfront fee, or his bank login and his Social Security number, to "release" money that is free to apply for. Beware the stranger in a vest at the door claiming to be a utility inspector who needs to come inside or needs cash, and the smooth pitch to "switch your supplier and save" that signs him onto a higher rate right there on the porch. And beware the text that says he is owed a refund or must "verify" his account, which only wants his utility-account password and his bank information. The rule is flat: the real utility does not demand instant payment by gift card or crypto, does not threaten to cut the power in the next half hour if he pays this second, and does not send someone to the door to collect cash; honest help does not charge an upfront fee for a free energy grant and never needs his account password or his bank login — so anyone doing those things is the scam, not the help.

And hear this, too, because it matters as much as the warning: the real electric and gas companies, the real county energy-assistance agency, the real LIHEAP program, the real Salvation Army and community-action energy funds, and the real township offices are not scams. The payment plans are real and they work. The assistance is real and it is meant for a family like his. The people who help him apply are real and they do it for nothing. The caution is about the imposter callers, the upfront-fee "grant" outfits, the door scammers, and the account phishers — not about the real utility or the real people paid to help him for free. Part of the help is learning to tell the community-action worker who helps at no cost apart from the caller who wants a gift-card number in the next thirty minutes.
