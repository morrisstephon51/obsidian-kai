# Post #397 — WHOLLY NEW DOMAIN: *THE BLANK LINE — THE TWENTY-YEAR-OLD THE STATE RAISED IN NINE HOUSES, THE BIRTHDAY THAT IS ALSO A DEADLINE, AND THE ONLY DOOR IN THIS ENTIRE SERIES THAT LETS YOU COME BACK* — HOW A YOUNG WOMAN WHO HAS NEVER ONCE HAD A NAME TO WRITE ON AN EMERGENCY-CONTACT LINE PAID $189 TO A WEBSITE FOR MONEY SHE ALREADY QUALIFIED FOR, WENT TWO YEARS WITHOUT HEALTH COVERAGE SHE ALREADY HAD, AND WAS NEVER TOLD THAT THE LAW BUILT HER A ROOM SHE IS STILL ALLOWED TO WALK BACK INTO — BECAUSE **YOU CAN MISS A NOTICE, BUT YOU CANNOT MISS A BIRTHDAY**

**Centered on:** ZARIAH DRAYTON, 20, Morgan Park, on Chicago's Far South Side — she was removed from her mother's apartment at eleven years old and she came out the other side of it at eighteen with a black trash bag, a manila folder, twelve dollars on a bus card, and no forwarding address for her own childhood. Nine placements. Four high schools. Six caseworkers, and she can only picture three of their faces. She has a younger sister, **NYLA, now 13**, who was placed separately in the second year and whom she has not seen since a supervised visit in a McDonald's on a Tuesday. Zariah works overnight stock at a grocery on Western, thirty-four hours most weeks, and sleeps in the finished half of a basement belonging to **MISS KIMBROUGH**, whose daughter Jaliyah went to one of the four high schools with her, for four hundred dollars a month in cash and no lease. She has never been arrested. She has never missed a shift. She has filled out, by her own count, somewhere over forty forms in two years — job applications, clinic intake, a phone contract, an apartment application, a gym she never went to — and every single one of them has a line on it that says **EMERGENCY CONTACT**, and she has left that line blank every time but once, when she wrote *n/a*, and the woman behind the glass slid it back and said, *"No, honey — the person we call."* And Zariah Drayton said, *"I know."* She turns **twenty-one on March 9th**, which she thinks of as a birthday, and which is in fact the expiration date on three doors nobody has ever read to her out loud.

**Faith anchor:** DEACON WENDELL MULDROW, 71, of Mount Carmel Missionary Baptist Church in Morgan Park — for **thirty-four years he has been the man with the camera.** Not the livestream booth; there are younger men who run the cameras and the slides and push the service out to Facebook, and God bless them. Wendell Muldrow is the one who shot **film.** Baptisms, Easter speeches, the Men's Day breakfast, every graduating senior on the church steps in a robe since 1992, the ninety-fifth birthdays, the repasts. He developed and printed for the first nineteen years of it in a converted half-bath off his kitchen, and he has, stacked in his basement on steel shelving he built himself, **somewhere over eleven thousand negatives in labeled shoeboxes.** Nobody in that congregation has a photograph of their own child at seven that he did not take. And the wound he carries is not a loss, and it is not a debt, and it has no dollar figure on it at all: in 1994 and 1995 a boy named **THURMON** came to Mount Carmel with a foster family, two years, every program, and Wendell photographed him at least a dozen times — and then Thurmon stopped coming, and nobody called, and nobody asked, and it has been thirty-one years. *"I have got that boy in a choir robe in December of '95 and I cannot tell you his last name,"* he says. *"Eleven thousand negatives in my basement. I documented that child and I did not know him. I took his picture and I never took his name. That right there is the whole sin, and it is mine."*

*(What follows is general navigation for education — not legal advice, not medical advice, not financial advice, and not a promise about anybody's case, eligibility, benefit, or money. Child-welfare rules, ages, deadlines, eligibility conditions, scholarship programs, and funding amounts are set by state and federal law and by agency rule; they differ by state and they change, sometimes in the middle of a year. Nothing in this post is your rule or your date. Verify every single thing here for your own case, in writing, with the agency that actually holds it.)*

**Resources — the real doors, free or low-cost, no fee to a stranger, ever:**

- **THE DOOR NOBODY KNOWS EXISTS: IN ILLINOIS, YOU MAY BE ABLE TO ASK TO COME BACK.** Care in this state does not have to stop the day you turn eighteen, and — this is the part almost no young person is ever told plainly — **leaving is not always permanent.** A young person who signed themselves out at eighteen may be able to ask to return to care before the cutoff age, with housing, a caseworker, and a stipend attached. I am not promising you it will be granted, because I cannot; the age, the conditions, and the process are DCFS rule and they change. **But the ask is free, the ask is yours to make, and nobody is going to come find you and offer.** Call the Illinois DCFS Advocacy Office for Children and Families at **1-800-232-3798**, say the words *"I aged out and I want to know if I can come back into care,"* and write down the name of whoever answers.
- **YOUR BIRTHDAY IS A DEADLINE. WRITE BOTH OF THEM ON THE WALL — 21 AND 26.** Some of these doors close on your twenty-first birthday and some of them stay open until twenty-six, and no envelope is coming to tell you which is which. Put the two dates on the wall over your bed in marker. **You can miss a notice. You cannot miss a birthday.**
- **GET YOUR OWN PAPER, AND GET YOUR OWN FILE.** Certified birth certificate, Social Security card, state ID, immunization and medical records, and every school transcript from all four schools. You are also generally entitled to ask for **your own case record.** It is your life, written down by other people, and you are allowed to read it. Ask your former caseworker, the regional office, or the Advocacy Office above how to request it in writing.
- **MEDICAID UNTIL AGE 26, REGARDLESS OF WHAT YOU EARN.** This is the single most valuable thing most former foster youth are walking around not using. Federal law opened a Medicaid category for young adults who were in foster care at the qualifying age, and **it is not income-tested the way ordinary Medicaid is** — you can be working full time and still qualify. It does not turn on automatically for everybody, and the rules about which state and which age are technical. Apply through **ABE.Illinois.gov**, or call your county's Family Community Resource Center, and **say the words "I was in foster care"** out loud, because that sentence is the whole eligibility.
- **EDUCATION MONEY THAT IS NOT A LOAN — AND YOU ARE AN INDEPENDENT STUDENT.** Fill out the **FAFSA at studentaid.gov — it is free, the word *free* is the first word in its name** — and when it asks whether you were in foster care, answer yes, because that answer generally makes you an **independent student**, which means **no parent information and no parent signature.** That single question is why thousands of former foster youth wrongly believe they cannot file. Then ask about the federal **Education and Training Voucher (ETV)** program for former foster youth, the **DCFS Scholarship Program**, and **tuition-and-fee waivers at Illinois public universities and community colleges** for youth and former youth in care. Amounts, deadlines, and eligibility are limited and competitive and they change every year. **Applying costs nothing. Ask early, ask in the fall, ask twice.**
- **START-UP AND HOUSING HELP EXISTS AND IT IS CALLED SOMETHING NOBODY WOULD GUESS.** Federal **Chafee / Youth in Transition** dollars and Illinois youth-housing assistance can go toward a security deposit, a first month, a bed, a stove, work clothes, a laptop. It is administered through DCFS and its partner agencies, it is not unlimited, and you generally have to be connected to somebody to access it. That is exactly why door #1 on this list is the first one.
- **PULL YOUR OWN CREDIT — FREE — AND THEN FREEZE IT.** Get all three reports at **AnnualCreditReport.com** (free, the real site, no card). Young people who grew up in care are robbed of their Social Security numbers at far higher rates than anybody else, usually by an adult who had their paperwork. **Freezing your credit is free at all three bureaus, and you can unfreeze it for free.** If you find something that is not yours, **IdentityTheft.gov** gives you a free report and a recovery plan.
- **YOU ARE ALLOWED TO LOOK FOR YOUR SIBLINGS.** Ask DCFS, the Advocacy Office, or your former caseworker about sibling contact and sibling information. If a brother or sister is still in care, there are people whose actual job is this. Nobody is going to volunteer it.
- **FREE LEGAL HELP, AND IT IS REAL:** **illinoislegalaid.org** for plain-English answers and forms; **CARPLS at 312-738-9200**; **Legal Aid Chicago**; and the **Chicago Coalition for the Homeless Law Project**, which does specifically this. Free legal help is also badly oversubscribed. Call early and call more than one.
- **IF TONIGHT IS THE PROBLEM: 2-1-1** (call 211 or 211.org) for shelter, food, and cash assistance in your county. For young people specifically in Chicago, **The Night Ministry** and **Covenant House Illinois** exist, and the **National Runaway Safeline is 1-800-786-2929.** If somebody is offering you a place to stay in exchange for anything at all, the **National Human Trafficking Hotline is 1-888-373-7888.** And if you are in crisis, in danger, or just at the end of it: **call or text 988.**
- **THE LAST DOOR IS NOT A PROGRAM. IT IS A NAME ON A LINE.** Ask one adult — one — to be your emergency contact, and make them give you the number, and put it in your phone under what they actually are. And if you are reading this and you are somebody's deacon, somebody's usher, somebody's church mother, somebody's supervisor: **be the name.** It costs you nothing and it is the only item on this entire list that no state agency can issue.

Let me start with the sentence that is the whole post. It was said by a twenty-year-old woman in a fellowship hall in Morgan Park, sitting in a folding chair with a clipboard on her knees, and she said it flatly, the way you say a fact about the weather:

**"I've never had anybody to put on the line. Not once. I'm twenty years old and I have filled out forty forms and I have never in my life been able to answer the question 'who should we call.'"**

Hold onto that. Everything after it is mechanics, and the mechanics are the easy part.

## I. Nine houses

Zariah Drayton was eleven the night she was removed.

I am not going to tell you why, and I want to be direct about the reason. **This post does not require anybody to be a villain.** Some of you reading this have a daughter in care right now and you are not a monster, you are sick, or you were nineteen, or you were beaten yourself, or the county came on the worst week of a bad year. Some of you are the aunt who couldn't take one more. Some of you are the mother who is now clean four years and cannot get anyone to say the word *reunification* to her.

What happened to Zariah's mother is her mother's post, and I have written pieces of it — recovery, housing, benefits, the courts.

**This one is about the child, twenty years old now, standing on the other side of all of it.**

Nine placements in seven years. Some of them were fine. One of them was genuinely good, a woman on 111th who made her do homework at the kitchen table and who Zariah still thinks about, and who moved to Georgia. Four high schools, which is why she has credits in three transcripts and a diploma from a school where nobody knew her long enough to write a recommendation. Six caseworkers, and she can picture three.

And here is the specific thing I need you to understand before we talk about a single dollar or a single program, because everything else in this post is downstream of it:

**Nine times in seven years, Zariah Drayton learned that needing something is what makes people send you away.**

That is not what anybody intended to teach her. Placements end for a hundred reasons and most of them are not about the child. But a child does not receive a policy. A child receives a pattern. And the pattern she received, nine times, was: *I got hard, and then I got moved.*

So when she turned eighteen, she signed herself out.

Not because anybody pushed her. Because staying felt like being watched, and because leaving was, as she puts it, **"the first choice I ever made that was actually mine."**

She walked out with a trash bag, a manila folder, and twelve dollars on a bus card.

Nobody read her a list.

## II. The line at the bottom of every form

Every post in this series has had a piece of paper at the center of it.

A storage lease signed in a parking lot. A rent-to-own contract with the total on page two. A notice of default. A washed check. A denial letter. And the instruction, in post after post, has always been some version of the same thing: **find your paper, read these four lines, and take somebody with you.**

Last week I wrote about a man who had no paper at all — no contract, no statement, no clause, because he was being billed for the crime of loving somebody he had no legal relationship to. That post was about **standing.** He wasn't a party to anything.

This one is worse in a quieter way, and I want to name the difference exactly, because they are not the same post:

**He had no contract. She has no person.**

Two hundred and ninety-one times I have written some version of *bring somebody with you.* Bring your daughter to the bank. Bring your cousin to the hearing. Take a friend to the clinic. Ask your deacon to sit in the room. It is, honestly, the most reliable advice in this entire series, because a second adult in a room changes what gets said to you.

Zariah Drayton has nobody to bring.

Not because she is unlovable and not because she is difficult. She is neither. She is a twenty-year-old who works overnights and pays her four hundred dollars in cash on the first and has never once been late. She has nobody to bring because **an emergency contact is not a person you find, it is a person you accumulate** — over years, in one place, among people who watched you grow — and she was moved nine times, on purpose, by a system that was trying to keep her safe.

So there is a line at the bottom of every form in America that says EMERGENCY CONTACT, and for Zariah Drayton it functions as a small, quiet, perfectly legal notification, forty times over, that she is by herself.

She once filled in *n/a.*

The woman behind the glass slid it back across the counter and said, not unkindly, *"No, honey. The person we call."*

And Zariah said, *"I know."*

## III. The birthday that is a deadline

Here is the structural fact that makes this domain different from every other one I have written about, and I want to put it plainly because it is worth more than any phone number on the list above.

**Every deadline in this series, until now, was issued by an institution and mailed to an address.**

Thirty days to answer a summons. A redemption date on a lien. The recertification window. The notice of hearing. The auction on a Saturday. Every single one of them was a date some entity chose, wrote down, put in an envelope, and — this is the part that made those posts possible — **could get wrong.** Half of what I write is about the notice that went to the old address, the letter that looked like junk, the deadline the company hoped you'd miss.

Zariah's deadline is her twenty-first birthday.

Nobody mails it. Nobody can send it to the wrong address. No clerk mis-keys it. It cannot be extended, appealed, waived, tolled, or negotiated, and there is no supervisor to ask for. It arrives on **March 9th** whether or not one human being has ever explained to her what it costs.

And it arrives on the one day of the year that is supposed to be *hers.*

That is the cruelty of the design, and I don't think anybody built it on purpose. Doors have to close somewhere and legislatures pick ages. But the practical result is that a young woman who has been taught by nine houses not to ask for anything is handed a countdown clock that **only runs down if she stays quiet** — and the only way to stop it is the single behavior her entire childhood punished.

So write both dates on the wall. **Twenty-one, and twenty-six.** Some doors close at one, some at the other, and no envelope is coming.

## IV. The door that opens backward

Now the part I have genuinely never been able to write before, in three hundred posts.

Every domain I cover is a one-way ratchet. The storage unit sells and it is sold. The check clears and the money is gone. The $14,218 does not come back. The deadline runs and the record sticks and the best I can honestly offer is *here is how to survive what already happened and never let it happen again.*

**In this domain, the law built a door that opens backward.**

In Illinois, care does not have to stop at eighteen. And a young person who left — who signed out at eighteen the way Zariah did, who walked because staying felt like surveillance — **may be able to ask to come back.** Not as a favor. As a thing the statute and the rules contemplate, with housing and a caseworker and a stipend attached, up to a cutoff age.

I am hedging that paragraph hard on purpose and I want you to see me do it. **I am not promising Zariah Drayton anything.** The age, the conditions, the process, and the paperwork are DCFS rule; they differ from state to state; they have changed before and they will change again; and there are young people who ask and are told no. If you take one sentence from this post to a young person tonight, do not take *"you can go back."*

Take this one: **"You are allowed to ask, the ask is free, and nobody is ever going to come find you and offer."**

Because that is the whole thing. Nobody is going to knock. There is no entity on earth whose job it is to drive to a basement in Morgan Park and tell a twenty-year-old overnight stocker that a room still exists with her name on it. The system that discharged her is not staffed to chase her, and the vendors who *will* find her at one in the morning — we are getting to them in section VI — are not selling a room.

I have spent a year writing about notices that never arrived and rights that went unclaimed. This is the first time I get to write about a door that is still standing open, right now, today, for somebody with five months and eleven days left.

Call **1-800-232-3798.** Say: *"I aged out and I want to know if I can come back into care."*

Write down who answers.

## V. Twelve dollars, four thousand one hundred dollars, and a wrist

Let me put the arithmetic of this on the table, because the numbers in this domain are strange and they do not behave like the numbers in any other post I've written.

**What she left with:** a trash bag, a folder, twelve dollars on a bus card, and no health insurance she knew about.

**What was in the folder:** her Social Security card, a photocopy of a birth certificate that two agencies would later refuse because it was a photocopy, one immunization record from the fourth of her four high schools, and a discharge packet she has never read all the way through. No transcripts from the other three schools. No medical history. No photographs.

**What she did not know she had:** Medicaid. She was eligible the entire time, at any income, because of the foster-care category — and she went **two years and two months** without using it because she believed, reasonably, that a person working thirty-four hours a week at a grocery store does not qualify for anything.

**What that cost:** in February she fell on ice at four in the morning carrying a case of milk, and her wrist has never been right since. She did not go. When she finally went, in May, it was an emergency room, and the bill is **$1,380**, and it is in collections, and her wrist is still not right, and she had coverage the entire time.

**What somebody else did with her name:** she pulled her credit for the first time in her life at that folding table, on a laptop, in nine minutes, for free. There is a utility account in her name opened when she was **twelve years old** with a balance of **$4,100**, and a repossession from a car she has never seen.

Sit with the twelve-year-old part. That is not a stranger who phished her. Twelve-year-olds do not have wallets. Somebody who had her paperwork in their hand — an adult in a house she was placed in — opened an account in the name of a child the state had given them to look after, and let it go to collections, and walked away, and it has been sitting on her file for eight years growing quietly while she was in the ninth grade.

I have written about child identity theft before. **That post was about a mother who could still freeze it in time.** She could catch it at nine and it would never touch her son's adult life.

This is the other end of that same pipe. This is what it looks like at twenty, when the freeze never happened, when the credit report was never pulled, when the person who did it may also be the closest thing you had to a mother for eighteen months in 2018 — and you have to decide, at twenty years old, with a $1,380 hospital bill and a wrist that clicks, whether to file a report naming her.

Nobody has a procedure for that. I don't have one either. What I have is: **pull the report, freeze the file, and go to legal aid before you decide anything.** You are allowed to take the year to decide. You are not allowed to not know.

**And the sum of it: the state of Illinois spent what it spent raising Zariah Drayton for seven years — and discharged her owing $1,380 to a hospital for a wrist she hurt at four in the morning, carrying milk, with insurance she'd had the whole time and nobody ever said out loud.**

## VI. The six things hunting this young woman — and the first two are perfectly legal

**1. The discharge itself.** Nobody breaks a law here. A young person turns eighteen, signs a form, and receives a packet. The packet is real and it is accurate and it is nineteen pages long and it is written for adults who read agency prose, and it is handed to a teenager on the worst-organized week of her life, in a building she is leaving. There is no exit interview where an adult sits across from you and says *here are your three dates and here are the two things you have that you don't know you have.* **The harm is not a con. It is a handoff performed at exactly the moment the person is least able to receive it.**

**2. The only landlord who will take her.** Also legal. She has no rental history, no cosigner, no credit (or worse, a file with somebody else's $4,100 on it), and no emergency contact. That combination does not get you a lease from a management company; it gets you a room, week to week, cash, no paper, from whoever doesn't ask. She got lucky: Miss Kimbrough's basement is warm and Miss Kimbrough is decent. **But "I got lucky in who took me in" is not a housing plan, and the market for people with nobody to vouch for them is run almost entirely by people who benefit from you having nobody to vouch for you.**

**3. The $189.** At 1:40 in the morning in February, she searched *"grants for people who aged out of foster care."* The top result was clean, official-looking, and full of photographs of young people in graduation caps: a **"Foster Youth Independence Award,"** $3,500, with a **$189 application processing fee, "refundable if not awarded."** She paid it with a debit card. It was the deposit money. It was forty-seven percent of a month's rent. There was no award, there is no refund, the "status portal" has said *under review* since March, and the phone number rings to a voicemail that is full. **Real money for young people who were in care is keyed to a case that already exists.** Which brings me to the test.

**THE TEST, and it is the most portable thing in this post: IF THEY CANNOT SAY YOUR CASE NUMBER, THEY DO NOT WORK ON YOUR CASE.**

Anybody with actual standing in your life as a former youth in care — the agency, the advocacy office, a caseworker, a scholarship program that serves this population — is operating off a case that the state already opened, years ago, with a number on it. A stranger selling you access to your own history cannot say it. They will ask *you* for it. **Real doors reference the file. Fake doors charge you for a folder.**

**4. The person who offers you a place to stay and calls you family inside a week.** I am going to say this carefully, because I am not interested in scaring a young woman away from kindness, and most people who offer a couch are exactly what they appear to be. But: **every exploiter uses the same opening, and the reason it works is not that the young person is stupid.** It works because a person who has had nine houses has no baseline. If you have never had love that was already proven, you have nothing to measure a fast, intense, generous offer against. So here is the only rule I trust: **real help does not require you to owe anybody.** If housing comes bundled with a debt, a shift, a favor, a phone they bought you, or a rule about who you can call — that is not housing. **1-888-373-7888.** **The Night Ministry. Covenant House. 988.** And no judgment on the other end of any of those numbers, ever, including for things you already did to stay warm.

**5. The second wave, which wears the first wave's uniform.** After the $189, she started getting texts. *"Your Foster Youth Independence Award is approved — verify your identity to release funds."* *"DCFS benefit update — action required."* Somebody who takes $189 from a twenty-year-old sells the fact that she is a twenty-year-old who will pay $189. **The people who come second always know something true about you, and they know it because the people who came first sold it.** No real agency texts you a link to release money. No real anything needs a gift card, a Cash App, a bank login, or your Social Security number typed into a page you reached from a text.

**6. And the quiet thief is a sentence: "I don't want to be a burden."**

This is the one that costs the most and the one nobody can subpoena.

I have written this section six times now and the sentence is different every time. For the man with the storage unit it was *"I'll get it out when I get on my feet"* — private shame. For the families with somebody inside it was *"we don't put our business in the street"* — a community agreement we taught each other and called dignity.

Hers is worse than either, because hers was **taught to her by adults, on purpose, nine times, as a survival skill.**

*I don't want to be a burden* is not modesty in a young person who came up in care. It is a strategy. It is the thing she figured out at twelve, that the quiet kid stays, and the kid with needs gets a phone call made about her. She is not being humble. **She is running the only program that ever worked.**

And it is the exact behavior that closes every door on the list above, because **every single one of those doors opens by asking.**

Structural insight, and it is the same one at the bottom of every post in this series: **all six of these need one condition, and it is that she doesn't say anything out loud to anybody who could help.** Five of them are priced on her silence. The sixth one *is* her silence.

## VII. What this post will not tell you

Seven honest things, because you deserve them and because I have never once ended one of these with a clean bow.

**One. I did not promise you can go back.** I said you can ask. There are young people who ask and are told no, and there are cutoffs that have already passed for some of you reading this. Ask anyway. An unasked question is a guaranteed no.

**Two. The education money is real and it is limited.** ETV, the DCFS scholarship, the tuition waivers — every one of them has an application, a deadline, a cap, and more qualified young people than dollars. **Applying is free. Receiving is not guaranteed.** Anybody who guarantees you an award is selling something, and the guarantee is the tell.

**Three. Nothing on that list gives her back the nine houses.** There is no program, no voucher, no stipend, no scholarship, and no church table that returns a childhood. I can get a twenty-year-old health coverage and a transcript and possibly a room. I cannot get her the years, and I am not going to pretend the list is a fix. **It is a floor. It is not restitution.**

**Four. She has done things she is not proud of.** So has almost everybody who came out of this the way she did, and some of it was to eat and some of it was to stay somewhere and some of it was just being nineteen. **This post requires nobody to be blameless.** If you are a church and you want to serve these young people, you need to decide right now, before the first one walks in, that your help is not conditional on a clean story — because if it is, you will get clean stories, and they will be fiction, and you will have built one more place where the truth gets a person moved. And for the young person reading this who has just quietly decided they are the exception — the one whose history is too far gone to walk into a church with — **988**, tonight, before you decide that about yourself alone in a room.

**Five. Free legal help is real and it is drowning.** CARPLS and Legal Aid Chicago and the Coalition are genuinely excellent and genuinely oversubscribed. Call more than one. Call early in the day.

**Six. The $4,100 may not fully come off, and the person who did it may be somebody she loves.** Identity-theft recovery on an eight-year-old account, opened by an adult who had lawful custody of her documents, is not a form you file on a Tuesday. It can be done. It is slow. And the hardest part of it is not procedural.

**Seven, and this is the dignity reframe, so read it twice: Zariah Drayton is not a young woman who ran out of chances. She is a child who ran out of adults.** Everything you are tempted to read as instability in her file — nine addresses, four schools, a gap, a job she left — is not a character trait. It is a *record of other people's decisions,* written in her name, in the third person, by strangers who were paid to observe her. She has a childhood. She just doesn't own the copy.

## VIII. Three frames in a shoebox marked 2009

The table was Wendell Muldrow's idea, and it was a strange one, and the trustees approved it mostly because he is seventy-one and nobody says no to him.

Second Sunday, the fellowship hall at Mount Carmel. One long folding table. On it: a borrowed flatbed scanner, an extension cord, a laptop open to AnnualCreditReport.com, a stack of index cards, two shoeboxes of negatives, and Wendell's camera on a strap. Poster board taped to the wall behind him, block letters, black marker:

**WHO IS YOUR EMERGENCY CONTACT? AND WHOSE ARE YOU?**

He called it **THE BLANK LINE.**

Fourteen people sat down over two hours.

**Three of them had been in foster care.** One was Zariah, who came because Miss Kimbrough made her. One was a nineteen-year-old named Jaliyah who had aged out in June and did not know the word *Chafee* existed. And one was a sixty-one-year-old woman who has been a member of Mount Carmel for thirty-four years, who has sung in that choir and served on that usher board and buried a husband out of that sanctuary — and who had never once, in thirty-four years, said out loud in that building that she was in care in 1979. She said it at a folding table to a man with a camera. Then she sat down in a metal chair and cried for a while, and three women who had known her for three decades stood around her, and not one of them had known.

**Nine adults signed the sheet** agreeing to be an emergency contact for a young person who didn't have one. Wendell would not let them just sign. He made every one of them **write their phone number on an index card in their own handwriting and hand the card to the person directly,** because, he said: *"A promise you can't dial is a feeling. I'm not collecting feelings today."*

**Zariah Drayton found three things in ninety minutes.** She found out she'd had Medicaid for two years and two months. She found the $4,100 account from when she was twelve, and froze all three of her credit files at that table, free, in about twenty minutes. And she found out — from a woman at the church who used to work in the schools and knew exactly which office to call — that there is a way to ask about her sister.

And then Wendell Muldrow did the thing that is the reason this post exists.

He had asked her, early on, what year she was born and where her people were from, the way old men do. And something caught. So while she was on the laptop he went down to his own basement, three blocks over, and came back forty minutes later out of breath with a shoebox with **2009** written on the end of it in grease pencil.

Zariah's grandmother had gone to Mount Carmel. Briefly, years ago, before everything — two, maybe three years, and then she passed and the family scattered and nobody there had put the name together with the young woman in the folding chair.

**Easter 2009.** Wendell had shot the whole program, the way he shot every program for thirty-four years.

And on that strip of negatives, in three consecutive frames, was a **four-year-old girl in a yellow dress and white shoes, holding an old woman's hand at the bottom of the church steps.**

Zariah Drayton is twenty years old and she had never seen her own face at four.

There is no photograph of her at five, or seven, or nine, or at any point in the nine houses. There are case notes. There are assessments. There are dated entries describing her affect. **Her entire childhood exists in the third person, written by strangers, in a binder — and the only image of her as a small child on this earth was in a shoebox in a deacon's basement, because thirty-four years ago a man with a Nikon made it his business to stand at the bottom of the steps on Easter and photograph everybody's grandbaby.**

He scanned it right there and printed it at the Walgreens on 111th and put it in a frame from the dollar store.

Then he took a new one. Her, that morning, in the fellowship hall, in her work fleece, not ready, hair tied up, laughing at something Jaliyah said. He printed that one too.

*"Now you got two,"* he said. *"One when you were four and one from today. Everything in between, somebody else wrote down. These two, we took."*

He has started something in the back of his log book, where for thirty-four years he has recorded the date, the event, and the roll of film. A new list, and it is not of photographs. It is **names, and the phone numbers of the people who agreed to be called for them.** Nine so far.

*"Eleven thousand negatives,"* he says. *"And a boy in a choir robe in 1995 whose name I never got. I have spent thirty-four years documenting this church. I am going to spend whatever I have left **knowing** it. That is a different job and I should have figured that out in 1995."*

Zariah Drayton has written a date on the wall of a basement in Morgan Park in black marker. **March 9.** Under it, a phone number: 1-800-232-3798. Under that, in smaller letters, nine words she has been practicing out loud because they are physically hard for her to say:

**"I aged out and I want to come back."**

And on the emergency-contact line of the next form she fills out, for the first time in her life, there is going to be a name on it — Wendell Muldrow's, in her handwriting, off an index card in her wallet, with the number he wrote himself.

*"It's not that I didn't have anybody who cared about me,"* she said. *"It's that I never had anybody I was allowed to be a problem to."*

## IX. Where The Plug AI actually fits

I built The Plug AI to be a kitchen-table decoder. In this domain that means it can sit with a young person and:

- **Translate the nineteen-page discharge packet** and the DCFS letters into plain English — what is a date, what is a right, what is just a form.
- **Help her say the nine words.** Practice the call. What to say, what to write down, whose name to get, and what to do when the first person she reaches says something vague.
- **Draft the records request** for the case file, the transcripts from all four high schools, the immunization record, and the certified birth certificate — and explain why a photocopy keeps getting rejected.
- **Walk the FAFSA foster-care question** so she checks the box that makes her an independent student and stops believing she needs a parent signature she cannot get.
- **Tell a real door from a fee in thirty seconds** — run the test: can they say the case number, or are they asking you for a card?
- **Write the text to a sister** she has not seen in seven years, which is the hardest ninety words anybody will write this month.
- **Write the nine sentences that ask a pastor for a table** in the fellowship hall, which is how every one of these ends anyway.

**And what it does not do, which matters more.** The Plug AI is **augmentation, not replacement.** It does not give legal advice, medical advice, or financial advice. It does not contact DCFS. It does not file anything, apply for anything, or appear anywhere on your behalf. It cannot tell you whether you will be allowed back into care, whether you will get a scholarship, or where your sister is — the agency answers that and it answers it for free. **It is augmentation, not replacement,** and the humans on that resource list are the ones who actually hold the doors.

It will never ask a young person for a Social Security number, a bank login, a card, or a gift card. It does not charge former foster youth. **And if anything calling itself The Plug AI ever asks a young person who was in the system to pay a fee for help — that is not us, and that request is itself the tell.**

The technology is not the point. The technology is a flashlight.

**The point is that there is a line at the bottom of every form in this country that says EMERGENCY CONTACT, and there are young people in your congregation right now who have never had a name to write on it — and you are not a program, you are not a caseworker, you have no budget and no authority and no training, and you can fix that one this Sunday with an index card and a pen.**

A twenty-year-old in Morgan Park is going to be twenty-one on March 9th.

Somebody get connected to her before the calendar does.

**The plug is in.**
