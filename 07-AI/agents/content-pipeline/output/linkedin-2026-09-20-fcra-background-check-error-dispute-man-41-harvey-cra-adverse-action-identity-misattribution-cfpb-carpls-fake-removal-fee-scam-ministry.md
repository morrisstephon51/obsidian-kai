**Scam spine:** "Background record removal," "background check clearance," and "criminal record deletion" services advertise on Facebook, neighborhood apps, and text blasts targeting job seekers who receive adverse action notices. They charge $300–$800 upfront to "remove" negative records — work that is either impossible (no private company can delete an accurate, current conviction — only a court-ordered expungement can), free (if the record is inaccurate, already expunged, or belongs to someone else, you dispute it directly with the CRA at no cost under the FCRA), or redundant (sending the same dispute letter you can submit yourself in fifteen minutes). What these services actually do: fill out the same free dispute form any consumer can submit online, sometimes with errors, and charge you for the privilege. Some take the money and send nothing at all.

**Key legal facts:**
- **FCRA (15 U.S.C. § 1681 et seq.):** federal law governing how consumer reporting agencies — including background check companies such as Checkr, HireRight, Sterling, First Advantage, Accurate Background, and others — must collect, use, and report consumer information, including criminal history
- **Adverse action sequence (mandatory, not optional):** when an employer takes adverse action (rescinds offer, declines to hire, demotes, terminates) based wholly or partly on a consumer report, federal law requires: (1) pre-adverse action notice — a copy of the report and written FCRA rights summary BEFORE the employer acts; (2) a reasonable time for the consumer to review and respond (courts generally recognize at least five business days); (3) final adverse action notice — naming the CRA, stating the CRA did not make the decision and cannot explain it, and notifying the consumer of dispute rights
- **Free report upon adverse action:** the consumer is entitled to a free copy of the report from the named CRA within 60 days of adverse action; consumers may also request their file from major employment CRAs at any time
- **Dispute rights:** any inaccurate, incomplete, or unverifiable information may be disputed directly with the CRA in writing or online — for free; the CRA must investigate within 30 days (45 days when the consumer submits additional information), correct or delete inaccurate or unverifiable entries, and notify all employment recipients of the corrected report in the past two years
- **Identity misattribution / "mixed file":** the most common class of background check error — CRA attributes another person's conviction to the consumer based on partial name match, similar date of birth, or address proximity; recognized as an FCRA violation when the CRA fails to use reasonable procedures to assure maximum possible accuracy; disproportionately affects people with common names
- **Illinois expungement and CRA compliance:** 20 ILCS 2630/10 prohibits dissemination of expunged or sealed records; private CRAs are independently required to purge expunged records but compliance is inconsistent; an FCRA dispute is the enforceable mechanism to compel a CRA to remove a record the consumer has already expunged through the courts
- **FCRA civil remedies:** actual damages; statutory damages of $100–$1,000 per willful violation; punitive damages; attorney's fees and costs; real employment lawyers take FCRA misidentification cases on contingency — they do not charge upfront fees
- **CFPB enforcement authority:** the CFPB supervises large CRAs and has civil penalty authority; a CFPB complaint formally logged against a CRA carries enforcement weight that a complaint lodged through a paid "removal service" does not

**Distinct from:**
- Expungement / record sealing (multiple prior posts) — those are court processes (through OSAD, the circuit clerk, or petition) that legally clear a conviction from the official court record; FCRA disputes are administrative challenges to private background check companies about data they are inaccurately reporting, regardless of what the underlying court record says
- Credit report errors — credit reports (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion) are also governed by FCRA but are maintained for creditworthiness purposes; criminal background check CRAs are a distinct industry with different dispute portals, data sources, and legal standards
- Wage theft / wrongful termination (prior posts) — FCRA violations concern the integrity of information used in pre-employment screening; wage theft and wrongful termination arise after a hire

**Here is what Marcus didn't know — and what tens of thousands of workers in Cook County don't know:**

**1. The employer violated federal law before they ever called you.**

The Fair Credit Reporting Act does not just govern what the background check contains. It governs the entire process an employer must follow before using that report to harm you.

When an employer takes adverse action based on a consumer report — including rescinding a conditional job offer — federal law requires a specific sequence that the employer cannot skip:

**Before acting:** the employer must send you a copy of the background check and a written summary of your rights under FCRA. You must receive this before the decision is final. The purpose is to give you time to correct errors before the offer disappears.

**After giving you time to respond:** only then may the employer finalize the adverse decision, and even then they must send you a formal adverse action notice identifying the CRA by name, address, and phone number, stating that the CRA did not make the decision and cannot explain it, and telling you about your right to dispute inaccurate information.

Marcus received none of this. The coordinator called and told him over the phone the offer was gone. No report. No rights summary. No notice. No time.

That is a separate, standalone FCRA violation by the employer — before we even get to the wrong records.

**2. You are entitled to a free copy of that background check. Get it first.**

Upon adverse action, you have the right to request a free copy of your report from the CRA within sixty days. If the employer failed to send a formal adverse action notice (as Marcus's employer did), you can call the HR department and ask directly which company ran the background check.

Once you have the CRA's name, go directly to their consumer portal. Every major employment background check company has one. It costs nothing. You do not need a lawyer to access your own report.

Get the report. Read every line. Write down exactly what is wrong: wrong name attached to a conviction, wrong jurisdiction, wrong charge, wrong disposition, charge that has been expunged.

**3. File a dispute with the CRA directly. Free. Thirty days for them to investigate.**

Under the FCRA, you have the right to dispute any inaccurate, incomplete, or unverifiable information directly with the CRA — in writing, online, or by mail — at no cost.

When you file, the CRA must:
- Notify the entity that furnished the information — the court, the state criminal records repository, the county clerk's office
- Conduct a reasonable investigation into the accuracy of the disputed entry
- Correct or delete inaccurate, incomplete, or unverifiable information
- Notify you of the outcome within thirty days

If the investigation confirms the error — if the St. Louis County conviction cannot be verified as belonging to Marcus — the CRA must delete it, correct the report, and notify everyone who received the wrong report in the past two years for employment purposes.

This is not a courtesy. It is a federal obligation. Filing the dispute yourself, through the CRA's free consumer portal, triggers the same legal requirement that filing through a $450 Facebook service would. The legal clock and the legal obligations are identical. The difference is whether you're $450 lighter when you start.

**4. Identity misattribution is the most common background check error. There is a name for what happened to Marcus.**

What happened to Marcus is called a "mixed file" — the background check company attributed another person's criminal records to him, likely based on a partial name match, similar date of birth, or overlapping geographic identifiers.

This is not a glitch. It is a systemic failure in how many CRAs build their databases. They aggregate data from county clerks, state repositories, court records, and arrest databases, then match to consumer profiles using imperfect criteria. When the matching is loose — a shared last name, a similar birthday — records from someone else end up in your file.

Misidentification is more common than people know. It disproportionately affects people with common names, people who share surnames with relatives, and people whose personal identifiers overlap with someone else in the same county or state database. The CFPB has published guidance on it. The courts have recognized it as an FCRA violation when CRAs fail to use procedures designed to ensure maximum possible accuracy.

Marcus has never lived in Missouri. The robbery conviction is attributable to someone with his name or a similar profile in a Missouri database. That record is unverifiable as applied to him — which means, once he disputes it, the CRA is legally required to delete it from his file.

**5. File a CFPB complaint the same day you file the CRA dispute.**

Consumer Financial Protection Bureau: **consumerfinance.gov/complaint** / **1-855-411-2372**

A CFPB complaint routes to the CRA's compliance team, which is required to send a formal response within fifteen business days. The complaint creates a paper trail that exists independent of the CRA's internal process.

If the CRA does not investigate properly or refuses to correct a clear error, that CFPB complaint record supports a civil lawsuit under the FCRA. Real attorneys take FCRA misidentification cases on contingency — they take a percentage of what you recover, which may include actual damages, statutory damages up to $1,000 per willful violation, punitive damages, and attorney's fees. They do not charge $450 before looking at your case. They evaluate it at no cost and tell you whether it is worth pursuing.

The $450 Facebook service cannot initiate CFPB enforcement. It cannot file a civil claim. It cannot get you damages. It can only do what you are already doing — for free — right now.

**6. Your 2018 expungement should have removed your Cook County case from every legitimate background check. Here is why it may still be appearing.**

Illinois law (20 ILCS 2630/10) prohibits the dissemination of expunged or sealed records. That means no criminal justice agency in Illinois may release records of your expunged conviction.

But private background check companies are not criminal justice agencies. They maintain their own databases, compiled from historical bulk downloads, data brokers, and court records scraped before your expungement was processed. When a court expunges your record, it does not automatically update every private CRA database that previously contained your information.

This is a known gap. The obligation is on the CRA to purge expunged records — and on you to dispute when they fail to do so.

If your Cook County battery appears on any background check report after your 2018 expungement, file an FCRA dispute citing the Illinois expungement statute and providing your expungement order. The CRA must delete it. If they refuse, that is a willful FCRA violation.
