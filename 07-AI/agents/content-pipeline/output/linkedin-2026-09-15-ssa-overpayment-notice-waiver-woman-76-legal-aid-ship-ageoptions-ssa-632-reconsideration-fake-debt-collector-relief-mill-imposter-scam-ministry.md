There is a woman who has started showing up in the church office on the first Monday of every month, and what she is watching for is this: a woman walking in with a white envelope and a scared look.

She is seventy-six years old. She put in thirty-five years as a union custodian for a school district in Harvey — the woman who made sure every building was clean when nobody was looking, who managed the boiler rooms through the winters, who stripped and waxed floors every summer in an empty building that smelled like cleaning solution and chalk and time. She retired at sixty-five and has been collecting Social Security for eleven years. Last January, she opened a letter.

It was from the Social Security Administration. The letter said she had been overpaid. The amount: fourteen thousand, two hundred dollars. The letter said SSA intended to withhold her full monthly benefit — her only source of income — beginning the following month, until the debt was recovered in full. It gave her a deadline. The deadline was thirty days away.

She sat with that letter for two days before she brought it to the women's ministry meeting.

She did not send a check. She did not call the number on the search result she found when she typed "SSA overpayment" into her phone. She did not take the call three days later from a man who said he was an SSA "repayment specialist" and that she could settle her debt at a reduced amount by calling him back. She brought the letter to the room where other women knew other people who had been through things, and someone in that room knew a SHIP counselor — an Illinois State Health Insurance Assistance Program counselor, the free service that helps seniors make sense of Social Security and Medicare options at no cost. She made the call before the deadline. The counselor walked her through what the letter actually said — and more importantly, through what the letter did not say.

What the letter did not say: she had the right to request a waiver of repayment entirely.

What the letter did not say: the waiver is free, filed directly with SSA, on a form called Form SSA-632, and the standard is this — if the overpayment was not your fault, and if repaying it would cause you financial hardship, SSA has the authority to waive the debt. Both parts must apply. But for a woman living on Social Security alone, who did not intentionally receive money she was not entitled to, who did not know she was being overpaid because SSA sent no notice during the time the overpayment was occurring — the two-part standard may very well be met, and the only way to find out is to ask.

What the letter did not say: if she submitted a waiver request before the response deadline — or within sixty days with good cause — SSA was generally required to continue paying her full monthly benefit while the waiver was being reviewed. The protection only attaches if she asks. If she does nothing, SSA moves forward. The silence reads as consent to the debt, and her check shrinks or stops.

She filed the waiver. She got help from Legal Aid Chicago to frame her hardship argument — the kind of help that existed for free and that she never knew to ask for. Three months later, she was still getting her full check. The waiver process was ongoing. But she was still in her apartment, still paying her bills, still going to church on Sundays, still intact. Because she did not do what the system expected her to do, which was panic and pay.

And now she stands watch at the church office on the first Monday of every month.

Because here is what she knows that she didn't know in January. SSA sends out a staggering number of overpayment notices every year. Many of them are caused by SSA's own administrative delays, reporting timing mismatches, bureaucratic errors in calculating income or work status — errors that a recipient living her ordinary life has no way to prevent or even detect while they are happening. She is not rare. She is not an edge case. She is one of a very large number of older Americans on fixed incomes who will open an envelope one day and find a notice demanding money they don't have back from a system they already paid into for decades. Most of them will not know they have options. Most of them will not know the deadline is real and what it means to meet it or miss it. Some of them will send checks they cannot afford. Some of them will answer a phone call from a man claiming to be a specialist. Some of them will lose a month's benefits before anyone explains the waiver.

She is in the first Monday of every month because she doesn't want any of those women to be the women she didn't reach in time.

Start with the door itself — the one the letter doesn't name clearly, the one almost no one tells a seventy-six-year-old woman receiving her first overpayment notice. Before she sends a dollar to anyone, before she calls any number from a search result, before she takes any meeting with anyone who mentions a fee: there is **SSA at 1-800-772-1213** — the number on the back of her Medicare card; the call to make; the words to say: *"I received an overpayment notice and I want to ask about requesting a waiver."* There is her **local Social Security office** (ssa.gov/locator — she can schedule or walk in and bring the letter). There is an **Illinois SHIP counselor** — the State Health Insurance Assistance Program, free, trained to help seniors navigate SSA and Medicare questions at no charge — at **1-800-548-9034**. There is **AgeOptions**, the Area Agency on Aging for suburban Cook County, and the **Illinois Department on Aging Senior HelpLine at 1-800-252-8966**, where a person answers. There is free legal help — **CARPLS, Legal Aid Chicago, and Prairie State Legal Services** — staffed by attorneys who handle SSA overpayment cases, who know the current rules and the current waiver standards, and who represent her at no cost. And there is **2-1-1**, the free call that opens the rest.

A note on the waiver process — stated plainly, with the hedge it deserves, because these rules have shifted in recent years and the only source she should trust for her specific situation is SSA and a legal aid attorney, not a post. In general: Form SSA-632 is the Request for Waiver of Overpayment Recovery. To qualify for a waiver, a person must show two things: (1) the overpayment was not her fault — meaning she did not misrepresent her situation or cause the error — and (2) repaying the amount would cause financial hardship, meaning she cannot meet ordinary living expenses if SSA takes back the money. Both parts must be present. She does not need a lawyer to file Form SSA-632, though having one helps considerably, and a legal aid attorney is free. The form asks her to describe her income, expenses, and the circumstances of the overpayment. SSA reviews it. If SSA denies the waiver, she has the right to a hearing. The waiver process is free, direct, and entirely between her and SSA — there is no role for any private "negotiator" or "specialist" who charges a fee.

A separate option: **Form SSA-561**, the Request for Reconsideration, which is the path if she disagrees with the overpayment amount itself or disputes the determination that she was overpaid at all. A waiver and a reconsideration can sometimes both apply, in different directions — the SHIP counselor or legal aid attorney can help her understand which to file first, or whether to file both. The deadline question — and the rule that submitting a response within the notice window generally holds her benefits in place while the review is pending — is real, time-sensitive, and controlled by current SSA policy, which she must verify directly.

And now the warning — said plainly, and I will say the flat rule again before I am done. Because the moment a woman on a fixed income discovers she has a $14,000 government debt, there is a predatory infrastructure built to reach her before anyone helpful does. There is the **fake "SSA debt collector"** who calls — voice calm, tone official, title impressive — saying she can settle her debt at a reduced amount if she calls back today, or pay the remainder by gift card, wire transfer, or prepaid debit card to avoid "enforcement action." The real SSA communicates by mail. The real SSA never calls to demand immediate payment. The real SSA never accepts gift cards under any circumstances. If that call comes, she hangs up and calls 1-800-772-1213 herself, on her own, to ask if there is actually a debt in her file.

There is the **"overpayment settlement" or "SSA debt reduction" service** that appears in search results and sometimes in church bulletins and community mailers — the firm that charges her hundreds of dollars to "negotiate" with SSA on her behalf and "reduce" what she owes. Form SSA-632 is free. Legal aid is free. SSA does not require or recognize private intermediaries in its waiver process, and any money she pays a "negotiator" before she has spoken directly with SSA or legal aid is money she cannot get back. There is the **fake SSA website** — a search-result link that looks official, asks for her name, Social Security number, and bank account information to "check her case status," and sends that information to a criminal who is not SSA. She navigates to SSA only through ssa.gov, typed directly, never a search link. There is the **false-urgency threat** — "pay the full amount today or federal agents will come to your address" — which is not how SSA overpayment works; there are no arrests for administrative overpayment notices, and the deadline in the letter is a response deadline, not a payment deadline. And there is the **false-hopelessness lie** — that the letter is final, that once SSA sends it there are no options, that the only question is how to pay — which is entirely untrue and designed to get her to stop asking questions.

The flat rule, said twice: **SSA's waiver process is free, and any person or service charging a fee to "negotiate," "settle," or "reduce" an SSA overpayment is not helping her — they are taking from her.** The real SSA communicates by mail, never by phone demanding immediate payment, and never through gift cards. She confirms everything through 1-800-772-1213 or her local SSA office — never through a search result, a phone call she did not initiate, or a business card from someone who knocked on her door.
