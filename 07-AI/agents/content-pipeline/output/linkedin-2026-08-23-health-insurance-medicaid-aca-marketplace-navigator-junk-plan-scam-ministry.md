There is a woman in that congregation who takes care of everyone but herself. She is forty. She works — she cleans houses, or she does hair, or she watches other people's babies, or she strings together part-time shifts somewhere in the south suburbs — and not one of those jobs ever came with health insurance. So she does not have any. She skips her own checkups. Maybe she rations a blood-pressure pill down to half, or she has felt something she will not get looked at, and every time the thought comes up she pushes it back down with the same words: "I'll go when I have the money." She has never once tried to get covered, because she is certain it costs hundreds of dollars a month she does not have, or certain she makes too much for Medicaid but too little for anything real, or she just does not know the door exists at all. And this week her phone rang with a warm, friendly voice — "a comprehensive health plan, limited enrollment, only ninety-nine a month" — and for a second it sounded like the thing she has been going without for years.

You know her. She is not careless and she does not take her health for granted — she is a woman who spends every day holding other people's homes and children together, and who goes without her own care so the rent gets paid and the lights stay on. No one ever handed her a job with benefits and a nice packet explaining her options. No one sat her down and told her that the coverage she assumes is for other people was actually built for working people right at her income, or that there is free help to sign up, or that the scary number in her head is probably not the number she would really pay. That is not a character flaw. That is what it looks like when a hard-working woman has been left to guess at a system nobody ever explained to her. And that guessing — that not-knowing, that quiet certainty that it is not for her — is the exact gap the scammers are standing in.

And that is the part that has to be said plainly, and I will say it again before I am done: a woman who works every day and still has no insurance did not fail, and she is not irresponsible. She was never offered coverage, and no one ever told her the help was there. Health coverage for working people is real, the financial help is real, and getting enrolled is free — through healthcare.gov, through her state's Marketplace, through her state's Medicaid, and through a certified Navigator who sits with her at no charge. And no honest helper sells her a miracle plan by pressure robocall, charges her a fee just to apply, or threatens to cut her off today unless she hands over her number on a link. She does not know any of that, because nobody ever told a woman who takes care of everyone else that there was free help to take care of herself. And that not-knowing is the door the con artists are leaning in.

And there are doors. Real ones. Doors nobody names for a proud, tired woman who has already quietly decided that coverage is a luxury for other people.

Start with the first thing she needs to hear: going without insurance while she works this hard is not proof she does not care about her health. It is proof the system never came looking for her. And the number she is dreading — the hundreds of dollars a month she cannot spare — is very likely not the number she would actually pay, because she has never had anyone run it with the real help figured in. A woman at her income, with her household, may qualify for coverage that costs far less than she fears, and possibly for free care she assumes is off-limits. Never having applied is not a verdict on whether she deserves it. It is only proof that no one ever told her the day was open to her at all.

So here are the doors almost no one names for her plainly. The first is that Medicaid may cover her, free, based on what she actually earns — not what she assumes about herself — and she may qualify and simply not know it, and applying at her state Medicaid office does not cost a dollar. The second is the Marketplace with real financial help: at healthcare.gov or her state's Marketplace, premium tax credits and cost-sharing reductions can bring a real plan far below the price in her head, sometimes close to nothing, depending on her income and who is in her home. The third — and hear this one, because it is the one that changes everything for a woman with no time and no answers — is that the help to enroll is free: a certified Navigator, an assister, a community health worker, someone trained to sit down with her, compare the real plans, and fill out the application with her, at no charge, ever. The fourth is that a life change like losing a job or a household change can open a special enrollment window, so she may not have to wait for a certain season to sign up. The fifth is that she does not have to wait on paperwork to be seen at all: a community health center will care for her on a sliding scale right now while she sorts the coverage out. And underneath all of it, for the shame of being the one who takes care of everyone and has nothing for herself, there is her family, her church, and another working woman who was just as sure she "made too much to qualify," sat down with a Navigator, and walked out covered — so she is not sitting alone certain she is the only one going without. She is standing in front of a whole row of doors, sure they are all locked or priced out of reach, when most of them are the coverage that was built for exactly a working woman like her.

But hear this warning before she gives one dollar or types her number into one link, because the scams hunt an uninsured, worried woman harder than almost anyone — a person who has gone without coverage for years, who is one bad diagnosis away from disaster, will move fast when a friendly voice finally offers her the thing she has been afraid to hope for. Hear it plainly, and I will say it again at the end: real coverage help does not work this way. Beware the warm robocall or the slick pitch for a "comprehensive plan, limited enrollment, only ninety-nine a month" — that is very often a discount card, a junk plan with almost no real coverage, or a "health-sharing" arrangement that is not insurance at all and can simply refuse to pay the day she actually gets sick. Beware anyone who charges her a fee to "sign her up" for Medicaid or the Marketplace, because enrolling is free through healthcare.gov, her state Marketplace, her state Medicaid, and certified Navigators, and no honest helper charges her to apply. And beware the text or the call or the look-alike website that says she must "re-verify your benefits now or lose coverage today" and wants her Social Security number, her Medicaid ID, or her bank information through a link — the real Medicaid and the real Marketplace do not threaten her into handing those over. The rule is flat: real health-coverage help — checking Medicaid, comparing plans, enrolling — is free through the official sites and a certified Navigator; no honest helper charges her up front, sells her a too-good-to-be-true plan by pressure call, threatens to cut her off today, or needs her number read aloud — so anyone doing those things is the scam, not the help.

And hear this, too, because it matters as much as the warning: the real Medicaid program, the real ACA Marketplace, healthcare.gov, the real premium tax credits, and the real certified Navigators are not scams. The coverage is real, the financial help is real, and enrolling is free. A trained Navigator who sits with her for an hour and asks nothing for it is not a trick — it is exactly how the honest help is supposed to work. The caution is about the "$99 miracle plan" robocall, the fee-to-enroll hustle, and the "re-verify or lose it" phishers — not about the real Marketplace, the real Medicaid, or the real people whose actual job is to get a working woman covered for free. Part of the help is learning to tell the free, trained helper apart from the warm voice that wants her money and her number.

Here is where the AI fits, and where it does not — and this is the second time I am saying the part that keeps her safe, on purpose. A tool like the ones we teach at The Plug AI is good for the quiet, patient work: explaining what "Medicaid," "the Marketplace," "premium tax credit," "cost-sharing," "Navigator," and "special enrollment" actually mean, in plain words, at the kitchen table after the kids are down, without ever making a tired woman feel small for not knowing what no one taught her; helping her gather what she needs — roughly what she earns, who is in her household, what she is looking for — and understand what to expect and what to ask; helping her find the real healthcare.gov, her real state Medicaid office, and a real certified Navigator so she reaches the true thing herself. That is real help, and it is a great deal of it. But the AI does not tell her she qualifies or what she will pay, and it does not promise her a plan or a price, because it cannot, and because a machine can be confidently wrong. This is general help finding your footing, not medical or legal or financial or insurance advice, and not a determination of whether she qualifies or what she will owe. Eligibility, the plans, the prices, and what is covered are decided by Medicaid, the Marketplace, and the insurers — not by an app and not by a guess. She verifies everything the tool helps her understand at the official healthcare.gov, at her state Marketplace or Medicaid office, or with a certified Navigator. And she never puts her Social Security number, her Medicaid ID, or her bank login into a public AI tool, and she never reads those to a robocaller or types them into a link — because the honest help does not work that way.

So if you are reading this and you are her — if you work every day and still have no insurance, if you skip your own care so everyone else's needs get met, if you have already decided coverage is a luxury that is not for you, and now a friendly voice on the phone is promising you the world for almost nothing — hear the whole of it, one more time, because you do not have to keep going without and you do not have to pay a stranger to get covered. You did not fail because no one ever handed you benefits. The coverage was built for working people exactly like you, and the number you are dreading is probably not the number you would really pay. Getting help to enroll is free. Go to the real healthcare.gov. Check the real Medicaid. Ask for a certified Navigator by name and let them sit with you.
