Helping someone find these doors is **not** the same as treating them, so route the real work to the real doors: **1-800-GAMBLER (1-800-426-2537)** and **ncpgambling.org** for the helpline, **IDHS/SUPR** for funded treatment, the **Illinois Gaming Board** for self-exclusion, **gamblersanonymous.org** and **gam-anon.org** for the free rooms, **nfcc.org** for the debt, **2-1-1** for a local person, **988** if it is dark right now, and the **FTC (1-877-382-4357), the Illinois Attorney General (1-800-386-5438), IC3 (ic3.gov),** and the **Illinois Gaming Board** to report the fakes.

At the Second Wind breakfast, they did it in order, and it took longer than a story would like. Sister Vernita said it out loud in the parking lot on a Saturday in October — *"I been at them machines, Elder"* — and then cried in the front seat of a Ford F-150 for eleven minutes while a seventy-four-year-old man said nothing at all and handed her napkins from the glove box. They called **1-800-GAMBLER** on speaker from that truck. She hung up the first time. They called again the next Saturday and she got through, and the woman on the phone was so matter-of-fact about the whole thing — *"okay, ma'am, let's find you an appointment"* — that Vernita said later the ordinariness of it was what undid her, because she had been carrying it like a monstrous thing and this stranger handled it like a flat tire. The assessment at the licensed provider cost her **nothing** — Illinois-funded, no bill, no insurance argument. Two weeks after that, Elder Waddell drove her to enroll in the **Illinois Gaming Board's self-exclusion program,** and she signed her own name with a shaky hand, and she says she cried in the car again afterward, but differently. She started a **Gamblers Anonymous** meeting on Tuesday nights two towns over, in a church basement where nobody knew her, which is exactly why she could talk. She called Rochelle in Killeen on a Sunday and told the whole truth including the furnace lie, and she will tell you that twenty minutes was harder than the rest of it combined, and that Rochelle cried and then got quiet and then got **angry at the lounge,** and then found **Gam-Anon** for herself and goes. A **free nonprofit credit counselor** put the $4,800 on a real plan built from her real pension and her real Social Security, on paper, with an end date on it — and Vernita did **not** pay the $499 "case-opening fee," did **not** renew the $199 system, did **not** wire the $890 to release the $47,500, deleted the app with *Palms* in the name, and reported the fake-winnings text to the FTC. She asked her credit union to **block gambling transactions** on her debit card and the young man there did it in four minutes and charged her nothing. And her mother's wedding ring came off the pawn ticket in March — the men's ministry did not pay it off for her, because Elder Waddell said that ring had to be hers, and so she paid it herself out of eleven weeks of set-aside, and that is why it counts.

Here is the honest part, because this ministry does not sell miracles. **She had a slip.** In March, at a truck stop off the interstate where nobody's list was ever going to stop her, on the worst anniversary of the year. She lost sixty dollars, which does not sound like anything and felt like the sky falling. The difference — the entire difference, the whole reason she is eleven months in instead of back at the beginning — is that she told her sponsor **the next morning** instead of hiding it for a year. Elder Waddell says that's not a failure, that's the skill: *"Cleve's slip lasted nine years because he never told nobody. Hers lasted one night."* Sister Vernita Pettigrew is back on the dinner-sale count committee, and when the pastor asked her to come back she told him why she had left, all of it, sitting in his office with her pocketbook in her lap, and he listened and then asked her if she would be willing to say some of it out loud one Sunday. She said she'd think about it. She thought about it for five weeks. Then she stood up at New Hope Tabernacle on a Sunday in August, sixty-six years old and shaking, and said, *"Y'all know me. I counted your money for nineteen years. I want to tell you where I been going, and I want to tell you what's free."* And the thing she remembers is not the silence, and it's not the amens. It is that **six women** found her afterward. Six. In the vestibule, one at a time, each one certain she was the only one in the building. New Hope started a second table that fall — Thursday mornings in the fellowship hall, coffee and Vernita's cornbread, and they call it **Second Wind Sisters** — and she is the one who sits at the end of it now, and when a woman comes in unable to look up, Sister Vernita Pettigrew slides her chair over and does exactly what was done for her: she goes first.
