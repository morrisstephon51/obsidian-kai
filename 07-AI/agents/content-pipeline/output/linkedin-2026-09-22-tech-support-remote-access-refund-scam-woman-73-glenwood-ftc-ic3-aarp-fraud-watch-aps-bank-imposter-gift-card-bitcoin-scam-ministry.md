**Resources:**
- **Your bank or card issuer — the number on the BACK of your card (or your paper statement)** — the single most important door. Call your *real* bank on a number *you* find, never a number a caller or a pop-up gives you. Ask for the fraud department; freeze the card, flag the accounts, and reverse anything you can. Fast calls sometimes stop a transfer that's still pending.
- **FTC — reportfraud.ftc.gov / (877) 382-4357** — report tech-support scams, gift-card demands, and imposter fraud. reportfraud.ftc.gov also gives you a plain-English recovery checklist.
- **FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) — ic3.gov** — where to report internet fraud and any money sent by wire, gift card, Bitcoin/crypto, or a cash courier; fast reports help investigators and can occasionally aid recovery.
- **AARP Fraud Watch Network Helpline — (877) 908-3360** — free, and you do **not** have to be a member or a senior. Trained specialists will talk you (or your worried family) through what happened and what to do next, without judgment.
- **Illinois Attorney General — Consumer Fraud Bureau** — illinoisattorneygeneral.gov / **(800) 386-5438** (Chicago), (800) 243-0618 (Springfield), (800) 964-3013 (Carbondale) — takes complaints about tech-support and imposter scams and can mediate.
- **Illinois Adult Protective Services (Illinois Department on Aging) — (866) 800-1409** (24-hour) — for financial exploitation of an adult 60+ or an adult with a disability; free, confidential, and a crime, not a shame.
- **Gift-card issuer fraud lines — call the card company right away** (Apple/iTunes, Google Play, Amazon, Target, Walmart, Visa/Mastercard gift). If you already bought or read off card numbers, report it immediately — money on an unspent card can *sometimes* be frozen.
- **Your device maker, the real way** — Microsoft (microsoft.com) and Apple (apple.com) **never** cold-call you and never pop a phone number onto your screen. To actually clear a scary pop-up: close the browser or hold the power button to restart — do not call the number.
- **CARPLS Legal Aid** — carpls.org / **(312) 738-9200** — Cook County's free legal-advice line if money was taken or an account was drained.
- **988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline** — call or text **988** — because the shame and panic after one of these can hit hard, and no amount of money is worth your peace; you are not alone and it was not your fault.
- **2-1-1 (United Way)** — dial **2-1-1** for a trusted local tech helper, senior services, and a real person to sit with you.

**Scam spine:** A careful person alone at a computer — a widow paying her bills, a retiree checking a balance, anyone who finally learned the machine and is a little afraid of it — is exactly who the tech-support crews are built for, because the whole con runs on manufactured panic and a friendly voice that arrives the instant the fear does. The chain is always the same. First, the **scary pop-up or the cold call**: a full-screen red "alert" with a blaring alarm and a frozen mouse, or a phone call, an email, or a text claiming to be *Microsoft, Apple, Norton/McAfee, Amazon, your internet provider, or your bank* — saying your computer is infected, your account was breached, or your "subscription auto-renewed," and giving you a number to call *right now.* The pop-up itself is the entire virus; there is nothing wrong with the machine. Second, the **remote-access hook**: the calm "technician" has you install a "security tool" — **AnyDesk, TeamViewer, or another screen-sharing app** — so he can "remove the virus" or "protect your account," and now a stranger is driving your computer, flashing scary black command windows to prove a "hack," and quietly opening your online banking. Third, the **manufactured emergency and the transfer**: he "discovers" foreign hackers pulling money from your account and connects you to the "bank fraud department" or a "government agent" who tells you to move your money to a **"safe account"** to protect it — which is his account. Fourth, the **refund / overpayment trick**: to "compensate" you, the "refund department" issues a refund — then "accidentally" sends far too much (you *see* $30,000 land, because he simply moved *your own savings into your own checking* while you watched), and now he is panicking that he'll be *fired* and *you* must send back the "extra" or "the transaction reverses on your whole family." Fifth, the **untraceable payoff**: because "for compliance" he can't take a card or a check, you must repay in **gift cards, a Bitcoin ATM, a wire, or cash handed to a "courier"** who comes to your door — and he coaches you to **stay on the phone** and to **lie to the store clerk** ("say it's for your grandkids; the store is compromised too") so no one can stop you. Sixth, the **second wave**: weeks later a "recovery agent," "refund service," or even a fake "FTC" or "police" caller phones the same victim promising to *get the money back* — for a fee — because a proven target gets sold and worked again. Under all of it is the identity harvest: while he "helped," he copied passwords, opened files, and installed a way back in. Eloise saw $30,000 that was never real, believed a polite young man was about to lose his job over her, and was standing at a drugstore counter with gift cards in her cart and a stranger in her ear — one swipe from handing her savings to a voice she would never hear again.

**Key facts and truths:**

- **Real companies never cold-call you, and a pop-up is never the fix.** Microsoft, Apple, your bank, and the government do not call, email, text, or throw a phone number onto your screen to warn you about a virus or a breach. A "CALL THIS NUMBER NOW" alert with an alarm sound *is* the scam — the number is the thief. Close the browser or restart the computer; don't call.

- **Never let a stranger take remote control of your device.** No legitimate technician, bank, or agency needs to *drive your computer* to "verify," "protect," or "activate" anything. The moment someone asks you to install AnyDesk, TeamViewer, or any "security tool" so they can see your screen, hang up — that step exists only to open your accounts and your files.

- **No real bank or agency ever tells you to move your money to a "safe account," and a "refund" is bait.** Your money is safest right where it is. A caller who says hackers are in your account and you must transfer funds to protect them is stealing them. And the "we refunded you too much, send back the difference" story is a trick — the "extra" is your *own* money moved between your *own* accounts to fool you.

- **No one honest is ever paid in gift cards, Bitcoin, a wire to a stranger, or cash to a courier.** Real refunds, real bills, and real fraud departments never work this way. A demand for gift cards, a trip to a Bitcoin ATM, or a "courier" coming to collect cash is a thief every single time — and being told to *hide it from the store clerk* is the loudest alarm of all.

- **Hang up, verify on a number you find yourself, and there are free doors that don't judge you.** Disconnect, then call your bank on the number on the back of your card, and report to the FTC (reportfraud.ftc.gov), IC3 (ic3.gov), and the AARP Fraud Watch Helpline (877-908-3360). Adult Protective Services (866-800-1409) is a free 24-hour door for anyone 60+. Fast action can sometimes freeze a card or reverse a pending transfer — and being targeted by a professional is not a character flaw.

**The Alarm That Wasn't.**

Eloise had the water bill open and the checkbook register beside the laptop when the screen went red.

The sound was the worst part — a whooping alarm, loud in the quiet kitchen, the kind of noise that means *something is on fire.* The mouse wouldn't move. White words stacked up over her banking page, which she could still see glowing behind them: *YOUR COMPUTER HAS BEEN LOCKED. DO NOT SHUT DOWN OR YOU MAY LOSE YOUR FILES AND BANKING INFORMATION.* And under it, a phone number, and the word *Microsoft.*

Her heart went sideways. Her whole life was on that machine now — the bank, the pictures of the baby, Nathaniel's letters she'd let her granddaughter scan. *Do not shut down.* So she didn't. She picked up her cell, and she called the number on the screen.

A man answered on the first ring. Pleasant, unhurried. "Microsoft Security, this is Raymond, my badge number is MS-4471. I'm so glad you called before it spread. Ma'am, are you at the computer now? Don't touch anything — you did the right thing not shutting it down."

He was kind. That was the thing she'd tell Loretta later, the thing she couldn't get past. He was so kind, and she was so scared, and he sounded like a person whose whole job was to make the scary thing stop.

---

He had her type a short web address to install "the security tool so I can see the threat and remove it." A little program opened. And then the cursor on her screen began to move on its own.

"That's just me now, ma'am, don't be alarmed — I'm in the system helping you."

Black windows flew open, green text scrolling too fast to read. "You see this? These are the intrusions. Someone in another country is inside your online banking right now, pulling funds. I'm going to connect you to our banking-fraud partner to secure your accounts before they empty them. Stay with me. Do not hang up — if you hang up, the connection breaks and they finish the job."

A second voice came on, a "fraud specialist." Then a story that moved so fast she couldn't get a hand on it: her accounts were compromised, they'd stop the thieves, and because her computer's security had "failed her," she was owed a refund of three hundred dollars for the expired protection.

"I'm issuing that refund to your checking now, ma'am. Please open your account and confirm you see it."

She looked. The checking balance had jumped — not by three hundred. By *thirty thousand.*

"Oh — oh no." Raymond's voice cracked. "Ma'am, I'm so sorry, I — I typed it wrong, I put thirty thousand instead of three hundred. Ma'am, please, that's not your money, that came out of the refund account, they will *fire* me, I have two little girls — you have to help me send back the difference or it comes out of *my* check, please, I've been so careful with you—"

And Eloise Hargrove, who had never in her life kept a dime that wasn't hers, who once drove back to a store over forty cents in wrong change, felt the hook go all the way in. *A nice young man is about to lose his job because he helped me.*

"What do I do," she said. "How do I send it back?"

---

He couldn't take a card, he said. Couldn't take a check. "Compliance." The only way that "clears the reversal" was gift cards — Apple, Target — bought in cash, and read to him over the phone, and then a courier would come for anything left.

"Ma'am, I need you to drive to a store. Keep me on the line the whole way — if we disconnect, the thirty thousand locks and it's a federal problem for both of us. And ma'am, this is important: **don't tell the cashier what the cards are for.** The store's payment system may be part of the breach. If they ask, say it's gifts for your grandchildren. Can you do that for me? You're doing so well."

She drove to the drugstore on Halsted with the phone hot against her ear and thirty thousand dollars of fear sitting in her chest. She put five hundred-dollar Apple cards in her cart because that's where he told her to start. She was at the customer-service counter, cards on the counter, reaching for her cash, when the young cashier — a girl maybe nineteen — looked at the stack, looked at Eloise's face, and said, quiet, "Ma'am… is somebody on the phone telling you to buy these?"

And something in Eloise's chest snapped awake.

She stepped back from the counter. She didn't hang up — she was too scared to hang up — but she pressed the phone to her coat to muffle it, and with her other hand she called the one person she knew had worked in a bank.

Sister Loretta answered on the second ring.

"Eloise?"

"Loretta." Her voice came out small. "There's a man from Microsoft — my computer, there were hackers, and now there's thirty thousand dollars in my checking that isn't mine and he's going to lose his job and I have to send it back in gift cards and he told me not to tell the girl—"

"Eloise." Thirty years of the fraud desk came into Loretta's voice, flat and certain and calm. "**Hang up on that man. Right now. Do not buy one card. Set the phone down on that counter and walk three feet away from it.** Is your money still in your bank?"

"Your money is fine. It has *been* fine this whole time. Walk away from the phone, baby. I'm getting in the car. Tell me where you are, and while I drive I'm going to tell you exactly what that man did, because I sat on the other side of this thousands of times and it is the oldest con there is wearing a brand-new coat."

Eloise put the phone down on the counter, the tiny voice still going *ma'am? ma'am? are you there?*, and she stepped back, and she breathed, and she waited for Loretta.

"First thing." Loretta held up a finger. "That red screen with the alarm? That *was* the virus. That is all it was — a webpage built to blare a siren, freeze your mouse, and put a phone number in front of you before your good sense could catch up. Microsoft did not know your name. She turned in the seat.

"And the gift cards. And the Bitcoin machine they'd have sent you to next. And the *courier.* And 'don't tell the cashier.' Every one of those is the same word in a different language: **untraceable.** A real refund goes back the way it came. A real bank moves nothing and asks you to buy nothing. Nobody honest on this earth was ever, one time, paid in Apple cards read off the back over the phone. Eloise put her hand over her mouth. "I gave him my computer."

"Then here's what we do, and we start right now, sitting in this car." Loretta was already dialing. "We call your bank — not any number he gave you, the number on the *back of your card* — and we tell the real fraud desk what happened and we freeze that card and watch those accounts. They did.

The bank confirmed it in four minutes: nothing had left her accounts. The "thirty thousand" was her own savings, sitting in her own checking, moved by a stranger's hand — the bank slid it back and put a fraud hold and a new card on the account before Eloise finished her coffee. At the house they powered the laptop all the way down. From Loretta's phone they changed the banking password and turned on the text alerts. That Saturday, at the Tech Table, one of the youth usher boys wiped the laptop clean and showed Eloise, twice, patiently, how to close a screaming pop-up without ever touching the number: *just turn it off, Miss Hargrove; hold the button; it can't follow you.*

She filed the reports herself, at the fellowship-hall table, with Loretta reading her the questions — reportfraud.ftc.gov, then ic3.gov — and she called the AARP helpline just to say it out loud to a stranger who told her, kindly, that she had done everything right in the end.

Three weeks later a man called claiming to be "with the FTC recovery unit," saying he could get her thirty thousand back for a small processing fee. She hung up before he finished the sentence, and she called Loretta laughing, a little, for the first time since the red screen.

**Here is what is true — and what a tech-support crew is betting you'll be too scared to remember:**

**1. A pop-up or a cold call is never how real help arrives.**

Microsoft, Apple, your bank, Amazon, and the government do not call, text, email, or splash a phone number and an alarm across your screen. That "CRITICAL ALERT — CALL NOW" is the entire scam. Close the browser or restart the machine. The number on the screen is the thief.

**2. Never give a stranger remote control of your device.**

No legitimate technician or bank needs to drive your computer to "fix," "verify," or "protect" it. "Install this so I can see your screen" — AnyDesk, TeamViewer, any of them — is the whole trap. Hang up before you click.

**3. No real bank moves your money to a "safe account," and a "refund" that's too big is bait.**

Your money is safest where it is. "Transfer it to protect it" is theft. And "we refunded you too much, send back the difference in gift cards" is a lie built on your own honesty — the "extra" is your own money, moved to fool you.

**4. Gift cards, Bitcoin ATMs, wires, and cash couriers are the tell — every time.**

Real refunds and real bills never run through gift cards read over the phone or a Bitcoin machine at the gas station. And "don't tell the cashier why" is not privacy — it is a thief keeping you from the one person standing right there who could stop it.

**5. Hang up, verify on a number you find, and use the free doors.**

Disconnect. Call your bank on the number on the back of your card. Report to the FTC (reportfraud.ftc.gov), IC3 (ic3.gov), and AARP Fraud Watch (877-908-3360). For anyone 60+, Adult Protective Services (866-800-1409) is a free, 24-hour, no-shame door. Move fast — a frozen card or a caught transfer can save everything.

**And hold this next to all of it:** real tech help is real, and needing it is not weakness. A grandchild who sets up your laptop, a library class, a church Tech Table, a genuine technician you found and called yourself — that is exactly how it's supposed to work, and a scary pop-up is not proof you did anything wrong. The caution is about the *unsolicited* alarm, the *cold* call, the stranger who wants to drive your screen, the "safe account," the too-big "refund," and the gift-card payoff — never about learning the machine, never about asking for help, and never about the free public doors, which are where a scam like this actually gets stopped.
