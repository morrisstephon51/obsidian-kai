There is a woman at that church who makes the whole building possible for the family everyone else quietly braces for. You know the family. The one that comes in a little late, a little loud, and half the room stiffens before it decides to be kind.

She is the one who built the buddy. So the boy who rocks and hums through the whole service has someone sitting right beside him who is glad to be there, and his mother, for one hour a week, gets to close her eyes and worship instead of managing. She is the one who carried a grown son up the front steps for two years because the ramp was talked about and never built. She learned enough sign to greet the deaf usher's whole family by name. She is the one who sat with a mother the week her daughter turned twenty-two and aged out of every program at once, and did not say a word, just stayed.

And then the hard part starts. Because loving a child with a disability and actually getting that child what the law already says they are owed are two different things, and the distance between them is a maze of claims and codes and waitlists nobody hands you a map to.

Because the SSI check that would help is almost always denied the first time, and there is a way to appeal it, a reconsideration, then a hearing, but the window is sixty days and it closes while an exhausted mother decides the fight is not worth losing again. Because at eighteen that same SSI gets re-decided under adult rules, and a young man who qualified his whole childhood gets a letter, gets dropped, and the family never learns he can reapply the same month. Because there is a Medicaid waiver that pays for the in-home help and the day program, but the waitlist runs for years, which means you have to get your child's name on the list long before you need it, and in this state that list is called PUNS, and no one at any hospital or school ever said the word to a mother who has needed it since the day she got the diagnosis. Because the school wrote an IEP that quietly gives half of what the child needs, and the parent has the legal right to demand a full evaluation in writing and bring an advocate into that room, and nobody told her she was allowed. Because at eighteen a family gets pushed toward full guardianship, signing away every right their child has, when supported decision-making might have fit better, and no one laid the choice out. Because the grandmother has been saving birthday money in a jar, two thousand dollars now, not knowing that same jar can disqualify her grandson from the benefits keeping him alive, when an ABLE account exists precisely so a family can save without losing everything.

So she tries to hold all of it. In her head. From the last three families she walked through it, half-remembered. She tells a mother the SSI was denied so that is the answer, not knowing sixty days is all they had to say no it is not. She never mentions PUNS because no one ever mentioned it to her. She is one faithful woman, working from memory of a system that changes its own rules faster than any one person can keep them.

So the door stays shut. The appeal window closes and the check that was owed never comes. The waiver waitlist that should have started at age three starts at age nineteen, and the family waits seven more years for help they needed all along. The IEP stays thin. The birthday jar cuts off the benefits. And a child the whole church has loved since the nursery slides from included to overlooked, quietly, one unspoken deadline at a time.

Here is what a family with money and a good hospital gets.

They get a care coordinator. A social worker who files the SSI before discharge, who signs the child onto the waitlist in the first month, who brings a special-needs attorney to the IEP table and lays the guardianship choice out years ahead. That family is not more devoted than the mother in the fourth pew. They were just handed a navigator, and she was handed a diagnosis and a waiting room and told to figure out the rest.

She can have that help too. For free. On the same phone a mother called her from.

She can put the denial letter in and get a plain read on why the SSI came back no and exactly what an appeal needs and by when. She can learn that PUNS exists, that the family should have signed years ago, and that signing this week still matters. She can find out that the age-18 redetermination is coming and that a young adult can reapply. She can learn the words a parent has the right to write to a school, and that an advocate is allowed in the room. She can learn that guardianship is not the only door at eighteen, and that an ABLE account can hold the birthday jar safely. She can get the whole maze — the SSI appeal, the waiver waitlist, the school evaluation, the eighteenth-birthday fork, the asset trap underneath all of it — broken into an order of operations she can run this week, and then walk it in to the free federally funded parent center and the state disability resource line that will sit with the family the rest of the way.

Not to replace what she does. The buddy sitting beside the boy is hers. The son carried up the steps is hers. The silent hour beside the mother whose daughter just aged out is hers, and no machine will ever learn a family's names in sign or be glad, actually glad, to sit next to the child the room braced for. It just takes the maze off her shoulders so she can do the part only she can do, which is to look a worn-out mother in the eye and say your child belongs here, all the way, and I am not going anywhere, until the mother believes it.

That is the gap. It was never that the families in our pews deserve a harder road to what the law already owes their children than the family that got a care coordinator on the way out of the hospital. It is that that family got a navigator, and ours got a diagnosis and one faithful woman working from memory.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities and the people navigating resources beside them in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Matteson. Dolton. Not a webinar. A room. Real problems. A real appeal, a real waitlist finally joined, a real child who finally gets what was theirs all along.

The people carrying our families with disabilities up the steps by hand are not behind. They were just never handed the tools the funded programs take for granted.

We are here to hand them over.

The plug is in.
