There is a woman in that church who never missed a Sunday and never missed a shift. She is forty-seven. She worked the same job for years — showed up early, stayed late, trained the new people, never once made it about herself — and a few weeks ago the company made cuts, and her name was on the list. Not because she did anything wrong. Because a spreadsheet somewhere needed a smaller number. And since that day she has done something quiet that nobody around her can see. She decided she is not going to file for unemployment. She tells herself it is a handout. She tells herself it is for other people, the kind of people she was raised not to be. She tells herself she probably would not qualify anyway. So she sits with the mail piling up and the pride holding the door shut, and she does not make the call, because she believes asking would make her less — when the truth is she is not asking for anything. She is trying to collect on insurance she already bought.

You know her. She is not lazy and she is not looking for a free ride — she is a worker who got cut, which is the single most ordinary thing that happens in a working life, and it happened to her through no fault of her own. And the thing stopping her is not the rules. It is a lie she was handed a long time ago about what unemployment even is. Because unemployment insurance is exactly that — insurance. She paid into it. Her employer paid into it, out of the work she did, out of every check she earned for years. It is not somebody else's money she is begging for. It is her own premium, coming back to her at the exact moment the program was built for: a good worker laid off when the company cut. And filing the claim is free on the official state site, and free people exist whose whole job is to help her do it. She does not know that. Nobody told her. So she assumes it is charity, and that assumption is exactly the empty space the predators move into.

And that is the part that has to be said plainly, and I will say it again before I am done: a laid-off worker did not fail because the company cut her job, the benefit is money she already paid for, real people will help her claim it for free, and no honest helper charges her a fee to file a free claim or "guarantees" approval for a price. Filing is free on the official state unemployment site. Help filing it is free — the state workforce office, the American Job Center, legal aid if she gets denied. If she is turned down, she has the right to appeal. She does not know any of that, because nobody sat down and told a steady woman who trained half that floor that the safety net had her name woven into it the whole time. And that not-knowing is the door the scammers are standing in.

And there are doors. Real ones. Doors nobody names for a laid-off worker who has already quietly told herself no.

Start with the first thing she needs to hear: a woman who showed up for years and got cut in a company decision did not come up short as a person. A layoff through no fault of her own is not a moral failure and it is not a verdict on her worth — it is the exact circumstance unemployment insurance was made for. She and her employer paid the premiums out of her own labor. Not knowing the claim is free and the help is free is not proof she is lazy or behind; it is only proof that no one ever handed her the truth about what she was paying for all those years. The voice in her head that says "unemployment is a handout, and it's not for people like me" is not telling her the truth. It is the barrier itself, wearing the truth's clothes. Claiming insurance she bought with years of her own work is not begging and it is not a handout. It is collecting what is already owed. The shame that says she should just tough it out is not dignity. It is the lever.

So here are the doors almost no one names for her plainly. The first is the key to all of it: the unemployment claim is free to file on the official state site, the money is insurance she and her employer already paid into, and a layoff when the company cuts is what it exists for — so she files, and she files soon, because timing can matter. She confirms the current steps and the deadlines through the official state site, not a link that lands in her texts. The second is the help she did not know was free: the state workforce office and the American Job Center are staffed with people whose job is to help her file, help her certify each week, and help her when something goes sideways, and free legal aid can step in if she is denied — free help is not lesser help. The third is the appeal: a denial is not the end of the road, she has a right to appeal within a short window the state sets, and she does not have to fight that alone. The fourth is the road forward: that same workforce system does not just hand her a check, it offers free job-search help and free retraining for the next thing, so the layoff becomes a bridge and not a cliff. And underneath all of it, for the shame of being the one who got cut, there is her family, her church, a mentor, and the others laid off from the same plant or the same store who filed and stood back up and will tell her exactly where to start — so she is not sitting alone at a kitchen table certain she is the only one this happened to. She is standing in front of a whole row of doors, and she is sure they are all charity she is too proud to take, when every one of them was paid for with her own work.

But hear this warning before she or anyone in her house pays a dime to "help" with the claim or hands over one more password, because the scams hunt the newly laid off harder than almost anyone, precisely because the fear is fresh, the money is short, and the process is confusing enough to make a look-alike website believable. Hear it plainly, and I will say it again at the end: the claim and the real help are free. Beware the imposter "unemployment benefits" website that charges a fee to file a claim that costs nothing on the official state site. Beware the text, email, or call that says she must "reactivate" or "verify" her account and needs her Social Security number, her state-account password, and her bank or benefit-card login to do it — that is not the state, that is the theft, and benefit fraud rings send those by the thousands. Beware the outfit that promises to "get her benefits approved, guaranteed" for a fee — no one can guarantee that, and the state decides it for free. And beware the fake "you were overpaid, pay it back here" notice or the skimmer on the benefit card that drains the money after it is loaded. The rule is flat: honest help does not charge a fee to file the free claim, does not guarantee approval for a price, does not ask her to pay to receive a benefit she earned, and never needs her state-account password or her bank login — so anyone doing those things is the scam, not the help.

And hear this, too, because it matters as much as the warning: the official state unemployment site, the real workforce office and American Job Center, and legitimate legal aid are not scams. The benefit is real and it is hers. The free help is real and it is staffed by people whose whole job is to get a worker like her through the claim and back on her feet. The appeal is real and it works. The caution is about the imposter sites, the fee-to-file outfits, the "guaranteed approval" services, and the account phishers — not about the real earned benefit or the real people paid to help her for nothing. Part of the help is learning to tell the workforce counselor who helps for free apart from the look-alike site that only wants a fee and a family's passwords.
