# Post #335

---

The doctor used the word hospice, and the whole room changed.

Raymond is 69. Retired CTA bus driver — 34 years, Jeffery Express, the 14, the 28 — out since 2022, knees and a heart that the doctors had been watching for three years. Congestive heart failure, stage three. The cardiologist said it plainly at the appointment last Thursday: the heart was not improving the way they had hoped, and it was time to talk about what Raymond wanted the next chapter to look like. She said the word hospice. She said it gently and meant it honestly.

His wife Loretta has not slept right since. Their daughter Keisha sat across the kitchen table from her father Friday morning and watched him stir his coffee in circles without drinking it. He did not say much. He was thinking about his grandchildren, the way you do when a doctor says what was unsayable. He was thinking about whether choosing comfort meant he had stopped believing God could still move.

Two days later, a company called. Professional sounding. They said they were "palliative care coordinators" and that they could help the family "navigate hospice eligibility and benefits," and that the first consultation — the one that would "map the care options" before Raymond made any decisions — was $350. They said they had helped hundreds of families just like theirs.

His niece Deja — Loretta's sister's daughter, the one in health education at a community health center in Pullman — was there that Sunday at dinner when Loretta mentioned it. She put her fork down.

---

**What Deja looked up that night:**

Raymond is on Medicare. If his cardiologist certifies that, given the normal course of his illness, he has a prognosis of six months or less — the standard clinical threshold for hospice eligibility — then Medicare Part A covers hospice care essentially in full. Not partially. Not with a big deductible. In full, with no cost for the core services.

What Medicare-certified hospice covers:

**Nursing visits** — registered nurses and licensed practical nurses who come to the home (or wherever Raymond lives — a facility, a relative's house) to monitor his condition, manage symptoms, and adjust his care. Covered at $0.

**Aide and homemaker services** — personal care help with bathing, dressing, personal hygiene. Covered.

**Medical equipment and supplies** — a hospital bed if he needs one at home, a wheelchair, oxygen, wound care supplies. Covered.

**Medications for the hospice diagnosis** — drugs that manage pain, breathlessness, anxiety, and the symptoms of his heart failure. Covered. (A small copay may apply for outpatient prescriptions — almost always $5 or less for each drug.)

**Social worker visits** — to help the family with planning, paperwork, difficult conversations, and community resources. Covered.

**Chaplain and spiritual care** — a hospice chaplain who can sit with Raymond, with Loretta, with the grandchildren, in the space where a diagnosis like this lives. Covered. At no cost.

**Grief and bereavement counseling for the family** — for up to a year after a death. Covered.

The coordination of all of that? It is done by the hospice's own care team — a registered nurse who is assigned as the primary coordinator, a social worker, an aide, a chaplain. There is no separate "navigator" to hire. There is no $350 consultation to "map the options." The hospice provides it. It is part of what Medicare is already paying for.

The company that called Loretta is not illegal. It is also not needed.

---

**The thing almost no one says plainly:**

Choosing hospice is not giving up. That sentence has to be said directly, and then said again, because the fear attached to the word lives in this community with particular weight — in our churches, in our families, in the way we were raised to fight and hold on and pray and not let go. Choosing hospice does not mean Raymond has decided to stop living. It means he and his family and his doctor have decided together that his goal is to live as fully and as comfortably as possible, with the people he loves, in a setting that honors his dignity. Patients can revoke hospice at any time — any time — and return to full curative treatment. Raymond keeps his own cardiologist as his attending physician. He can still go to the doctor. He can still go to church. Hospice is not the end of his care. It is a different kind of care.

And the research — the actual clinical research — shows that in many cases, people on hospice live longer than people with the same diagnosis who are not, because the quality of care and the quality of life both improve. That is not a comfortable sentence to read if you grew up believing that hospice meant the doctors were done with you. But it is true, and Raymond and Loretta deserve to hear it.

**The real free resources — the ones that do not cost $350:**

The first call is free. **SHIP — the State Health Insurance Assistance Program** — provides free, one-on-one Medicare counseling from trained volunteers who are not paid by any insurance company or any care provider. A SHIP counselor can sit with Raymond and Loretta and explain exactly what the Medicare hospice benefit covers, what the six-month-prognosis standard means, what questions to ask any hospice provider, and what the family's rights are during the process. In Illinois, SHIP is run through the Department on Aging. 800-548-9034.

**Medicare's Care Compare tool** lets Raymond search for Medicare-certified hospice providers in his zip code, compare quality ratings, and read inspections. medicare.gov/care-compare.

**The National Hospice and Palliative Care Organization (NHPCO)** has a helpline — 800-658-8898 — and a CaringInfo resource at caringinfo.org that speaks plainly to patients and families about what hospice is, what it is not, what questions to ask, and how to begin. It is free. It is designed for families in exactly this moment.

**The Illinois Department of Public Health** certifies hospice agencies in Illinois and maintains a searchable list. A Medicare-certified and state-licensed hospice agency is the standard to look for. idph.illinois.gov.

**Legal Aid** can help with advance directives, a health care power of attorney, and understanding Raymond's rights as a hospice patient — and for seniors, this help is often free or low-cost.

And **AgeOptions**, the Area Agency on Aging for suburban Cook County, and the City of Chicago's Department of Family and Support Services for Chicago residents, can connect the family to in-home support services, caregiver respite, and community resources that work alongside a hospice team. They do not charge a fee to help a family navigate.

---

**Hear the warning before you send $350 to anyone:**

Scams orbit the hardest moments — and a family in the days after a doctor says "hospice" is one of the hardest moments there is. Know this clearly, and I will say it again before the end: a real hospice navigator is your hospice team's registered nurse, social worker, and chaplain. They are provided by the hospice. They are covered by Medicare. You do not hire a "coordinator" on top of them.

**Beware any company or individual charging a fee to "help you navigate hospice eligibility" or "map your care options" or "coordinate your Medicare hospice benefit."** The hospice team does that. It is already paid for. If someone is charging you for it separately, they are charging you for something you already have.

**Beware the "end-of-life care grant" or "hospice benefit unlock."** No government program pays a fee-charging company to release care money to a dying patient. Medicare is the benefit. The hospice is how you access it. No grant is sitting unclaimed that a $200 payment "unlocks."

**Beware any company that threatens you with a bill after your loved one is transferred to a different hospice or leaves hospice voluntarily.** Patients have the right to change hospice providers or to revoke hospice at any time. If someone is calling with a collections threat after a voluntary revocation, document it and call the Illinois Attorney General's Healthcare Bureau: 800-243-0618.

**Beware impersonators calling as Medicare, Social Security, or "hospice benefit services."** A real agency does not call unsolicited to tell you your loved one has "unactivated hospice credits" or ask for his Medicare number over the phone to "enroll him" in anything. That is identity theft for billing fraud.

**And beware any hospice provider** — a real one, with a license — that enrolls Raymond without a proper physician certification of a terminal prognosis, or that discourages him from getting a second opinion, or that promises things the benefit does not cover. Hospice fraud does happen, and it is perpetrated by unscrupulous providers — not by families who accept care they were offered.

The rule is flat: Medicare covers hospice. The hospice covers the coordination. SHIP counselors explain it for free. You do not need a coordinator, a navigator, or a $350 consultation to access a benefit you already have. Anyone who charges you to "unlock," "coordinate," or "release" your Medicare hospice benefit is taking money from a family in the worst week of their life and giving them nothing real in return.
