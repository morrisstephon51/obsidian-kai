There is a man in that church who worked thirty years at the same plant, and last month they closed the doors and handed him a box for his locker, and now he sits at his kitchen table every morning convinced he is too old to start over and too far behind to be worth hiring — and he is so ashamed that he has not told half the people in the pews next to him that the job is gone.

You know him. Fifty-eight. He is the man who never missed a shift, who trained the young guys, whose hands know a machine better than most people know their own kitchen. He gave three decades to that floor. He was going to retire from there. And then a decision he had no part in, made in a room he was never in, closed the plant — and now he is a "displaced worker," a phrase nobody ever taught him would one day be him. He does not know that the unemployment insurance he paid into out of every single check for thirty years is his to file. He thinks it is charity, or a handout, or something for other people, and his pride will not let him reach for it. He does not know there is a place a few miles away that will retrain him for free, help him rewrite what he knows into something a hiring computer can read, and coach him through interviews he has not sat for since Reagan was president. He thinks the online applications that go into a void, and the "we saw your resume" texts that come in at midnight, are the only doors left. And one of those texts is about to try to rob him.

He is about to walk into something that could cost him more than the job already did. That is the part that has to be said plainly, and I will say it again before I am done: a real employer never asks you to pay to get a job, never mails you a check to deposit and wire part of it back, and never needs your bank login or your full Social Security number before there is a real offer on the table. He does not know that yet. He thinks the confident "recruiter" who moved the conversation to a chat app and offered him work-from-home money that sounds too good is his break — because a proud man who has been told no for three weeks is exactly who a scam is built for. The only thing standing between him and a fake check that will bounce and leave him owing the bank is information nobody ever handed him.

And there are doors. Real ones. Doors nobody names for a fifty-eight-year-old man the month his life's work disappears, because who stands up at coffee hour and says he got laid off and he is scared.

Start with the first thing he needs to hear: the plant closing was not his failure, and filing for what he earned is not a handout. He did everything the right way for thirty years. He is not lazy — he is the hardest-working man most people know. He is not obsolete — thirty years of skill does not evaporate because a company made a spreadsheet decision. And the unemployment insurance is not charity. He paid into it out of every check he ever earned. It is his, the way the money in a jar he filled himself is his. Reaching for it is not weakness. Leaving it unfiled out of pride is just handing back wages he already earned.

So here are the doors almost no one names for him plainly. The first is the one his pride makes hardest to see: file for what you earned, and do it now, because these things have deadlines and every week unclaimed is money gone. Then there is real, free reemployment help — and this is the door almost nobody knows exists. There are American Job Centers and, here in Illinois, Illinois workNet, with dislocated-worker services built for exactly his situation: free career coaching, help rewriting his resume, interview practice, and — this is the part that changes everything — free or fully funded retraining and certifications through a program called WIOA, so a man who ran machines for thirty years can get a credential for the next thing without paying a dime for the schooling. He needs to understand his options — unemployment insurance, dislocated-worker programs, WIOA retraining, registered apprenticeships that pay while you learn, and Trade Adjustment Assistance if the work moved overseas — so he can see what is actually open to him instead of assuming it is nothing. There may be help he does not know to ask for while he is between checks: a SNAP, utility, or medical bridge, health coverage through COBRA or the marketplace so a health scare does not finish what the layoff started, and scholarships for retraining. And underneath all of it, for the shame he has been carrying alone, there are trusted friends, his faith, one call to 211, and 988 if the weight of losing his work and his footing ever gets too heavy to hold. His church's benevolence fund and that same 211 call can cover the gap and point him to the reemployment help near him. He is standing in front of a whole row of doors — file for what you earned, free reemployment help, understand the options, help he may qualify for, support for the shame, and his church and 211 — and he is sitting there certain there are none, because nobody ever told him a single one was his.

But hear this warning before he answers one more text, because the scams circle the laid-off man the moment he starts applying. Hear it plainly, and I will say it again at the end: a real employer never asks you to pay to work. Beware the "job" that wants money up front — for training, for equipment, for a background check, for a starter kit. Beware the offer that mails you a check, tells you to deposit it and send part back or buy gift cards or "supplies" — that check is fake, it will bounce in a week, and the bank will come after him for every dollar. Beware the "recruiter" who never gets on a video call, who moves him to a chat app, and who asks for his Social Security number, his bank or direct-deposit information, or a photo of his ID before there is any real job. Beware the MLM pitch dressed up as a career. And beware the texts and emails and lookalike websites claiming to be the state unemployment office, demanding a fee or his login or his SSN to "release your benefits" — the real agency does not work that way. The rule is flat: a real employer never asks you to pay to get a job, never sends you a check to deposit and wire part back, and never needs your bank login or full Social Security number before a genuine offer; unemployment is filed for free through the official state agency; and no legitimate program charges a fee to unlock a benefit you already earned.

So he almost fell for it. Not because he is foolish — foolish is not the word for a man who kept a plant floor running for thirty years. Not because he did not work hard enough — he outworked almost everyone he will ever apply against. He almost fell for it because nobody ever walked him to the real doors, and the only voice offering him work was the one built to rob him.

Here is the thing about the man across town. Same story. Same thirty years, same plant, same closed doors, same age, same shame that kept him quiet in the pews. But a friend at church sat down next to him and said: "Listen to me. Before you take that first ‘job’ that wants a fee — that's a scam. The unemployment is yours, you paid into it, go file it. And there's a place that will retrain you for free and help you get hired. Let me take you there." So he filed his claim, walked into an American Job Center, sat with a coach who rewrote thirty years of real skill into a resume a computer could finally read, enrolled in a funded certification, and a few months later he was working again — steady, and proud of it. He was not smarter than the man in our pew. He was not more deserving. He had one friend who knew the real doors were there and named them before the shame and the scammer won. One man is back on his feet with help he earned. The other almost cashed a fake check and almost left his own wages unclaimed. The whole difference was somebody who said "the unemployment is yours, the retraining is free," and a silence where nobody ever did.

The man in our pew can be met before he answers that text. Not with a lecture about how he should have saved more, or reskilled years ago, or how he is too old to bother retraining. With a hand on his shoulder and the plain truth that he has doors, and that a closed plant does not mean a scammer's text is his last chance.

Because this is where a tool like AI actually meets a man like him — quietly, at the kitchen table, when the applications have gone into a void for three weeks and the shame is doing his thinking — doing the part that the not-knowing-where-to-start and the distance from the computer have been blocking. It can be the patient someone that explains what "unemployment insurance," "a dislocated worker," "WIOA," "an American Job Center," "Illinois workNet," "an apprenticeship," and "Trade Adjustment Assistance" actually mean, in plain words, as many times as he needs, and never once make a thirty-year tradesman feel stupid for not already knowing the acronyms. It can take everything his hands know and help translate it into a resume that a hiring system will actually surface instead of bury. It can help him practice for an interview he has not sat in for decades, until his voice steadies. And it can name the fake check, the pay-to-work "job," the SSN-phishing "recruiter," and the fee-demanding "benefits" text for exactly what they are, before they take one more thing from a man the layoff already took enough from.

Not to be the one who decides anything. Let that be plain. The machine does not file his claim for him, and it does not get to decide whether he qualifies for a benefit or which program is right — that belongs to the state workforce agency and the programs, under their rules, not a chatbot's guess. It is not the unemployment office, it is not an employer, it is not a caseworker. And it must never, ever be fed his full Social Security number, his bank or direct-deposit details, or his account logins — a public AI tool is not the place for a single line of that. It is only the patient someone that takes a cold, confusing, shame-soaked moment and makes it walkable, so that a man who did everything right for thirty years does not hand what little he has left to a stranger with a fake job.

That is the gap. It was never that the man across town deserved it more, or was smarter, or was less proud. Same thirty years. Same plant. Same age. Same shame. It is that one man had a friend who said "the unemployment is yours, the retraining is free, let me take you there," and the other was left alone at the table with a void of applications and a scammer who felt like a yes. Not more worthy. Not more skilled. Just never told the doors were his.

A word of care, plainly, and I will say the important parts twice. This is general navigation, not legal, financial, or career-guarantee advice, and not a determination of whether you qualify for any benefit or which program fits you. Eligibility, amounts, timelines, and outcomes are decided by the state workforce agency and the programs under their rules — not by a machine and not by a guess. AI tools can be confidently wrong, so never trust a rule, an amount, or an eligibility answer one gives you without confirming it with Illinois workNet, an American Job Center, the official state unemployment agency, and 211. And never put your full Social Security number, a bank login, or your direct-deposit information into a job posting or a public AI tool. Hear the heart of it twice, because it is built for someone in exactly this spot: a real employer never asks you to pay to get a job, never sends you a check to deposit and wire part back, and never needs your bank login or full SSN before a real offer — and unemployment is filed for free through the official state agency, so if a "job" or a "benefits" text wants your money or your logins, walk away. Route the real help to real, trusted places: the official state unemployment agency to file the claim you earned; an American Job Center and Illinois workNet for free reemployment help, dislocated-worker services, and funded WIOA retraining; a trusted friend and your faith community for the shame; your church's benevolence fund for the gap; 988 if the weight gets too heavy; and 211 or 211.org to find local reemployment help in one call.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Dolton. Robbins. Chicago Heights. Matteson. South Holland. Not a webinar. A room. Real people. A real displaced worker, taught how to make the same tool a younger applicant already knows how to use sit with him at the kitchen table, explain the words nobody explained, turn thirty years of real skill into a resume a computer can read, coach him for the interview, and name the fake-check "job" and the phishing "recruiter" for exactly what they are — so he can claim what he earned and get back to work instead of getting robbed on the way.

The out-of-work men and women in our churches are not falling into these traps because they are foolish or did not work hard enough. They are falling because a plant closed through no fault of theirs, because pride and shame keep them quiet, and because nobody ever told them the unemployment is theirs, the retraining is free, and no real job asks you to pay to work.

We are here to sit with them before they cash the first fake check.
