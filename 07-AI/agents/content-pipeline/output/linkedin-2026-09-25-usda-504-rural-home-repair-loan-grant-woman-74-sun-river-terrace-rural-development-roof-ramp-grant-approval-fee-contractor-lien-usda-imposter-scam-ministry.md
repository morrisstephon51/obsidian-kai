**Scam spine:** A paid-off house owned by an elderly person on a fixed income is one of the most heavily targeted assets in America — because it holds real value, the owner often can't afford upkeep, and "government home-repair grant" is a phrase just true enough to disarm the careful. Predators work the ache of a failing house against the honest hope that help exists. **(1) The advance-fee "release your approved grant" scam:** a call, text, or email — *"You've been approved for a $9,700 USDA home-repair grant! Pay a one-time $495 processing / approval / release fee to receive your funds."* **A real grant never charges you a fee to get it,** and the government does not "approve" you for a grant you never applied for. The "fee," the specific dollar amount, and the urgency are the whole scam. **(2) The door-knocking "USDA-approved contractor" / "storm inspector":** a truck in the yard, a man in a vest who "handles the grant paperwork for you" and wants you to **sign a blank or vague contract, hand over a big cash deposit, and — buried in the paper — grant him a LIEN on your home or an assignment of the grant funds.** Then the work is shoddy, half-done, or never done — and now there's a **lien on the homeplace,** which is how families **lose land that was paid off for seventy years.** USDA does not send contractors door to door; **you** choose a licensed contractor through USDA's real process, and no legitimate repair should put a lien on your deed. **(3) The "USDA Rural Development" imposter phishing for your identity:** a caller "verifying your grant" who needs your **Social Security number, date of birth, and bank routing and account numbers** to "direct-deposit the funds." That is **identity theft and a drained account,** not a deposit. The real USDA takes your application in person or through official channels and does not cold-call for your bank login. **(4) The upfront "grant writer / packager" fee:** a flyer or ad — *"Let our federal grant specialists write your USDA application for $750."* Applying is **free,** free and low-cost help exists (HUD counselors, USDA-certified packagers), and no one legitimate demands a big upfront fee to fill out a government form for you. **(5) The "sign your deed over and we'll fix it free" equity-strip:** the darkest one, aimed straight at the paid-off house — *"We'll do all the repairs; just put the house in our name / add us to the deed / take our reverse deal and we'll handle everything."* This is how a widow ends up **no longer owning the home she owns free and clear.** Never sign your deed to anyone offering repairs; call **free legal aid** first, every time. **(6) The gift-card / wire / Zelle / prepaid cash-out:** the finish line of the fee scams — because those payments are **instant and irreversible,** which is the only reason a "government fee" would ever be demanded that way. Deacon Biggers wired $600; the wire is why he never got it back. **The real Section 504 program is free to apply for, it comes through your local USDA office and not a truck in your yard, it never asks for a fee or your bank password, and it never touches your deed. The "fee," the yard visit with a paper to sign, the phone call wanting your Social Security number, and the "sign the house over" offer are the tells.**

**Key facts:**
- **There IS a real federal home-repair program for rural homeowners — and it's free to apply.** USDA Rural Development's **Section 504 Single Family Housing Repair Loans & Grants** helps **very-low-income** people who **own and live in** a home in an **eligible rural area.** You apply at your **local USDA Rural Development office** at **no cost.** Check your address and income on the **USDA eligibility map (eligibility.sc.egov.usda.gov)** first.
- **The loan is about 1% interest; the grant is for age 62+ who can't repay a loan.** The **loan** — roughly **1% fixed, up to about $40,000, over up to 20 years** — is for repairs and removing hazards. The **grant** — up to about **$10,000** — is only for homeowners **62 or older** who **cannot afford to repay a loan,** and it must be used to **remove health-and-safety hazards** (or make the home accessible). Sometimes a loan and grant can be **combined.** *(Confirm current amounts and limits with USDA — they change.)*
- **A real grant NEVER charges a fee to "release" it.** Any call, text, or email saying you've been **"approved"** for a grant you didn't apply for and must **pay a "processing," "approval," or "release" fee** is a scam — always, no exceptions. So is any demand for a **gift card, wire, Zelle, or prepaid card.** The government doesn't collect fees to give you a grant.
- **Never give your SSN or bank number to someone who called you about a "grant."** The real USDA works through its **local office** and official application, not a cold call wanting your **Social Security number, date of birth, or bank routing number** "to deposit the funds." That call is **identity theft.** Hang up and call the office yourself.
- **Get a written contract — and NEVER sign a lien or your deed for repairs.** Illinois's **Home Repair and Remodeling Act** requires a **written contract** for jobs over **$1,000,** and you generally have a **3-day right to cancel** a contract signed at your home. A door-knocking "contractor" who wants a **blank paper, a big cash deposit, a lien on your house,** or your **deed** is how families **lose paid-off land.** Have **free legal aid** read anything before you sign.
- **The grant has one honest string — and it is NOT a scam.** A Section 504 **grant** generally must be **repaid if you sell the home within a few years** (commonly three). That is a **real, disclosed federal rule** — very different from a con artist's secret **lien.** Ask USDA to explain it plainly; a legitimate condition is written down and explained, not sprung on you.
- **Free help exists — you never have to pay to apply.** **HUD-approved housing counselors (1-800-569-4287),** USDA **certified application packagers,** and nonprofits like **Rebuilding Together, Habitat for Humanity,** and **AgeOptions / 2-1-1** can help you apply or find repair help **free or low-cost.** Anyone charging a big **upfront fee** to "write your grant" is selling you something the government gives away.

**The House Amos Built: How a Deacon Who Once Wired $600 to a Ghost to Fix His Own Roof Stopped a 74-Year-Old Widow From Paying a "$495 Grant Fee" and Signing Her Land Away — and Walked Her Into the Real USDA Office That Fixed It All for Free.**

Ella Mae Dorsey had the $495 counted out on the kitchen table, under the brown stain, on the morning she almost lost it.

Not cash — she didn't keep that kind of cash — but she had her checkbook out and her reading glasses on and the text message open on the phone her granddaughter had set up for her, and she was doing the thing the text told her to do, which was call the number to "confirm your grant and arrange the processing fee."

The text had come three days before, and she had read it maybe forty times:

**"USDA HOME REPAIR: Congratulations! Property at [her road] APPROVED for $9,700 home repair grant. To RELEASE funds, one-time processing & approval fee $495 required within 72 hrs. Reply YES or call agent."**

Nine thousand seven hundred dollars. She had said the number out loud in the empty kitchen. *Nine thousand seven hundred dollars.* That was the roof and the steps and money left for the septic. That was Amos's house made whole. That was the pot off the floor and the stain painted over and the ceiling light she could turn on without a prayer. That was getting down her own back stairs to the car and to church on a Sunday like a person and not a shut-in.

And all it wanted was $495.

She had almost nothing, but she had *that* — barely, if she skipped the thing she'd been saving toward — and $495 to unlock $9,700 was the best math a poor woman had ever been offered.

The only reason she had not already sent it was that the "agent," when she'd called the first time, had wanted her to pay by a **prepaid card** she'd have to go buy at the Dollar General in Momence and read the numbers off the back over the phone, and something about that had snagged in her — *why would the government want me to read numbers off a card I bought by the candy?* — and she'd said she needed to think, and he'd gotten a little sharp, said the grant would **expire,** and that had snagged too. But three days of a leaking roof will wear a snag smooth. This morning she'd decided. She'd go get the card after she called back.

And then, before she called back, she'd gone to the repast at Sweet Canaan for Sister Odessa's husband, because you go, and she'd found herself standing next to Deacon Thurman Biggers at the folding table with the coffee, and he'd asked her — the way he asked everybody, she'd find out — *"Ella Mae, how's that house of Amos's holding up? Anybody called you about a grant?"*

And she'd been so tired, and so alone with it, and Deacon Biggers had known her since they were young, and it had just come out of her: *"Thurman, I got approved for a grant. Nine thousand dollars. I just got to pay the little fee to get it."*

Deacon Biggers set his coffee down.

"What little fee, Ella Mae."

"Four hundred and ninety-five. To release it. It's a — it's a processing thing. The government's."

And Deacon Thurman Biggers, seventy-nine years old, a deacon and a trustee and a veteran, took Ella Mae Dorsey gently by the elbow, walked her over to two chairs by the wall away from the crowd, sat her down, and said, "I need to tell you about the worst thing I ever did with money, and I've never told it to anybody in this church. And I need to tell you before you go home, because if you send that four hundred ninety-five dollars, you are going to do the exact thing I did, and there is not going to be any grant, and there is not going to be any four hundred ninety-five dollars, and you are going to feel a way I would not wish on my enemy. So sit here with me."

And he told her about the six hundred dollars.

---

He told it plain, the way a man tells a thing he's carried too long.

How his roof had gone, fifteen years back, when his wife Cordelia was still living. How a man had called — friendly, official, *sorry* about the roof like he cared — and told Thurman there was a **government home-repair grant** for veterans and seniors, thousands of dollars, and Thurman qualified, and all he had to do was cover the **"federal release fee."**

"Six hundred dollars, Ella Mae. He said wire it, Western Union, out of the gas station. And I did. Deacon of the church. Thirty years at the plant. Never owed a man a dime. I stood at that Western Union and wired six hundred dollars to a name I never checked, and I drove home and told Cordelia the roof was fixed, praise God."

He was quiet a second.

"Wasn't no grant. Wasn't no man. Number went dead that afternoon. Six hundred dollars — and back then that was *real* money to us — gone. And you know what was worse than the money? I couldn't tell a soul. Not Cordelia's people, not the pastor, not you, not nobody. Because I was the careful one. I was the one folks came to. And I'd wired my savings to a telephone. So I patched the roof myself with tar and a young fella from up the road, and I let it eat at me, and I never said the word 'grant' out loud again for years."

Ella Mae had her hand over her mouth.

"Now here's the part you need," Deacon Biggers said, and he leaned in. "A few years on, my niece — smart girl, works at the clinic — she found out I'd been played. Don't ask me how, she just knew her uncle. And she said, 'Uncle Thurman, there is a *real* one.' She said the government — the **USDA,** the farm people, Rural Development — really does help old country folks fix their houses. A loan at almost no interest. And if you're past sixty-two and you can't pay a loan back — like me, like *you,* Ella Mae, on just that check — a **grant.** A real one. To fix what's *dangerous.* And it don't cost a *dime* to ask. You go to the *real office.* You sit with a *real person.*"

He shook his head slowly.

"So I went. Seventy-two years old, hat in my hand, still half-ashamed. And it was *real.* They looked at my income — wasn't much, pension and my check — and they looked at my house, out here in the eligible country, and I was over sixty-two and couldn't carry a loan, and they gave me a **grant.** Free. And they put a **new roof** on my house and a **ramp** off my back door for when my knees went. The same house I'd wired six hundred dollars to a thief to fix. And I stood in my yard the day they nailed that roof on and I cried like a child, Ella Mae, and not all of it was glad. Because I paid six hundred dollars to the devil to learn what the government would've handed me for *free* — if one person, *one,* in my whole life had just *said it to me.*"

He took her hand.

"So I say it now. To everybody. That's my folder in the back — 'Homeplace Helpers.' That's why I ask everybody about their house. Because I will be *hanged* before I let another old saint in this church pay a fee to a ghost when the real thing is sitting free at an office thirty minutes away. Now. That $9,700 text. Read it to me again."

She read it to him.

"Ella Mae," he said, "the *real* USDA does not text you that you're approved for a grant you never applied for. You never filled out a thing, did you? No. So how'd they *approve* you? They didn't. There's no grant on that phone. And the *real* USDA does not want four hundred ninety-five dollars, or a card from the Dollar General, or a wire from the gas station, to *release* anything. A real grant don't cost money to *get.* That's the whole trick, baby. That fee *is* the scam. There is no nine thousand seven hundred dollars behind it. There's a man waiting for your four hundred ninety-five, same as there was a man waiting for my six hundred."

Ella Mae Dorsey put the phone face-down on her knee and sat very still.

"Then what about the other one," she said quietly. "The man came to my *yard.*"

Deacon Biggers went still himself. "What man came to your yard."

She told him about the truck.

How, two days after the text, a pickup with a magnet sign had pulled into her yard, and a man in a safety vest got out, real friendly, said he was a **"USDA-approved storm inspector and contractor"** working the area, said he'd noticed her roof from the road, climbed right up on it before she could say much, came down shaking his head — *"Ma'am, that's bad, that's a fire waiting to happen, that wiring gets wet you're gone"* — which had scared her to death because it was the exact thing that kept her up at night.

And then he'd gotten *helpful.* Said he could **"handle the whole grant paperwork for you, take that headache right off you,"** and do the roof, and he had a paper for her to sign to **"get her on the schedule before the weather,"** and he needed a **deposit** — a few thousand, he'd take a check — to **"order materials."**

"Did you sign it," Deacon Biggers said. "Ella Mae. Did you sign his paper."

"I told him I'd think," she said. "He left it. It's on the counter. He's coming back Thursday for it and the check." She looked at her hands. "Thurman, he was going to *fix it.* He climbed right up. He knew about the wiring."

"Of course he knew about the wiring," Deacon Biggers said, not unkindly. "He said the scariest true thing he could find so you'd sign scared. That's the *job.* You bring me that paper he left. You hear me? Before Thursday. We are going to have somebody who knows how to read it *read* it, because I will bet you this coffee there is something in there about a **lien** — about him being able to put a claim on *this land,* Amos's land, if there's any dispute about his money. That is how it goes, Ella Mae. Old widow, paid-off house, sign a paper you didn't read, and next thing there's a **lien** on seventy years of your family, and the roof still leaks. We are not doing that. We are calling the *free lawyers.*"

He stood up, slow, on his knees that USDA had built a ramp for.

"Here is what we are going to do, and we are going to do it together, because I did the first part of it alone and ashamed and I am not going to let you. We are going to **not** send that four hundred ninety-five dollars. We are going to **not** sign that man's paper Thursday — you let *me* be here Thursday when he comes. We are going to **not** give one soul your Social Security number over that telephone. And you and me, Ella Mae Dorsey, are going to get in my car and drive to the **real USDA Rural Development office,** and we are going to sit down with a real person, for free, and we are going to see about fixing Amos's roof the way I fixed mine — the *real* way."

---

They did it over the next few weeks, and Ella Mae kept waiting for the catch that never came, because she had lived long enough to believe that anything free had teeth.

First, the paper. Deacon Biggers drove the contractor's contract to **Prairie State Legal Services,** and a young lawyer read it and then read the important part to Ella Mae over the phone in a voice that got tighter as she went: it was a vague, mostly-blank agreement with a **big non-refundable deposit,** a clause **assigning any "grant or program funds" to the contractor,** and — buried on the back — a **"mechanic's lien" provision** that would have let him **put a claim against her home** if there was any fight about payment. "Miss Dorsey," the lawyer said, "if you had signed this and given him a check, and he'd done bad work or no work, he could have tied up your *house* — the house you own free and clear — to get paid. Do not sign it. And you have every right to tell him not to come back." When the man showed up that Thursday, Deacon Biggers was sitting on Ella Mae's porch, and he stood up, and the man looked at the deacon and got back in his truck and never came back. "They don't want a *witness,*" Deacon Biggers said. "They want a scared woman by herself."

Then, the phone. When "USDA Rural Development" called again wanting her **Social Security number and her bank routing number** to "set up the deposit," Ella Mae — coached now — said, "I'll come to the office and do it in person," and the caller got sharp and then got gone. She reported that number, and the $9,700 text, and the contractor's magnet-sign company, to the **USDA Office of Inspector General at 1-800-424-9121,** to the **FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov,** and to the **Illinois Attorney General at 1-800-386-5438** — three reports, because Deacon Biggers said *"your one report is the next widow's warning, same as mine would've been if I'd made it."*

Then, the real thing. They checked her address on the **USDA eligibility map** at the library, with the librarian's help, and Amos's land out in the country was **eligible.** They drove to the **USDA Rural Development office,** and a woman named — Ella Mae would remember it — a woman who called her "Miss Dorsey" and not "sweetie," sat with them for over an hour. She looked at Ella Mae's income: a small survivor's check, nothing else. Very-low-income, plainly. She noted Ella Mae was **seventy-four** — well past sixty-two — and asked, gently, whether she could realistically repay a loan, and Ella Mae said no, truthfully, she could not carry a note. And the woman nodded and said the words Deacon Biggers had promised: "Then we look at a **Section 504 grant** for you, Miss Dorsey. It's for exactly this. Homeowners sixty-two and over, low income, who can't repay a loan, to remove **health-and-safety hazards.** A roof letting water onto your wiring is a health-and-safety hazard. Rotted steps and no way for you to get out of your house safely is a health-and-safety hazard. Let's see what we can do."

There was paperwork, and it took some weeks, and a **real** inspector came — one who worked for the program, who showed identification, who wanted nothing signed in the yard and no check — and USDA ran its own process to line up a **licensed contractor** the proper way. The woman explained the one honest string plainly, the way a real program does: the **grant** would have to be **repaid if Ella Mae sold the house within three years** — but Ella Mae almost laughed, because she was never selling Amos's house; they'd carry her out of it. "That's a real rule, written down," the woman said, "not like the fee that man wanted. You'll see it in the papers. Ask me anything." And Ella Mae did, and got real answers.

They fixed the roof.

They built the ramp.

They got the septic looked at.

Ella Mae Dorsey stood in her yard — the way Deacon Biggers had stood in his — on the day the new roof went on the house Amos built, and looked up at it not leaking, and did not know she was crying until Deacon Biggers, standing next to her on his own good knees, handed her his handkerchief and said, "I know. I know. Free. It was free the whole time."

She did not pay $495. She did not pay $600. She did not pay $750 to a grant writer or a nickel to a contractor with a lien in his paper. She did not give away her Social Security number or her bank number or one square inch of Amos's deed. She paid *nothing,* because the real thing costs nothing to ask for, and she got the roof, the ramp, and her Sundays back — because she could get down her own new steps to the car now, and roll her walker up her own new ramp coming home.

And on the second Sunday of the next month, Ella Mae Dorsey sat down next to Deacon Thurman Biggers at the **HOMEPLACE HELPERS** table in the back of Sweet Canaan, with his battered accordion folder open between them, and when old Brother Pruitt came shuffling up saying somebody'd called him about a grant for his porch and a fee to release it, it was Ella Mae who patted the chair and said:

*"Sit down here, Brother Pruitt. Let me tell you what almost happened to me — and let me tell you what Deacon Biggers here did fifteen years before that, that he'll tell you himself. There's a real one. USDA. Free to ask. But that fella on your phone wanting a fee is a thief, sure as I'm sitting here. Don't you send him a dime and don't you sign nobody's paper. We're going to take you to the real place ourselves."*

---

The whole near-disaster turned on a single thing Deacon Biggers had learned the hardest way and Ella Mae almost learned it too: that **a real grant never charges you a fee to get it,** and that the moment anyone — on a phone, in your yard, in your mailbox — asks you to **pay, sign, or hand over your numbers** to "release" free help, you are not looking at help. You are looking at the thief who found the paid-off house with the widow inside.

In the gap between a leaking roof and a real office, a text nearly took $495 off a woman who barely had it, a contractor nearly tied a lien to seventy years of family land, a phone call nearly harvested her identity, and — as it had done to a good deacon fifteen years before — a "government grant fee" nearly turned a proud, careful person into someone too ashamed to tell a soul.

*"I got a text I'm approved for a USDA home-repair grant — I just have to pay a fee to release it. Is that real?"*
*"A contractor showed up in my yard, climbed on my roof, and wants me to sign a paper and give a deposit today — should I?"*
*"Somebody from 'USDA' wants my Social Security number and bank number to deposit a grant — do I give it?"*
*"I own my house free and clear but I can't afford to fix the roof and I'm 74 — is there any real help?"*
*"A company wants $750 to write my government grant application — do I need to pay that?"*

Those are five three-minute questions. The answers — *no, a real grant charges no fee to release it, so it's a scam; no, never sign or deposit for a yard contractor — get it in writing, keep your 3-day right to cancel, and never sign a lien or your deed; no, the real USDA doesn't cold-call for your SSN and bank number; yes, USDA Rural Development's Section 504 program has a 1% loan and, for owners 62+ who can't repay a loan, an actual grant to remove health-and-safety hazards, applied for free at the local office; and no, applying is free and free help exists, so never pay a big upfront "grant writer" fee* — are the difference between a widow wiring away money she doesn't have to a ghost, and that same widow standing in her yard under a new roof that cost her nothing.

To do it is free: the **real USDA Rural Development office (rd.usda.gov)** and the **eligibility map (eligibility.sc.egov.usda.gov)** to check and to apply; **USDA OIG (1-800-424-9121)** and **reportfraud.ftc.gov** to report the imposters; the **Illinois Attorney General (1-800-386-5438)** for home-repair fraud and the written-contract / 3-day-cancel law; **Prairie State Legal Services (prairiestate.org)** — or **CARPLS 312-738-9200 / Legal Aid Chicago** in Cook County — before you sign any contract, lien, or deed; **HUD housing counselors (1-800-569-4287)** and **2-1-1** for free help applying and for volunteer repair through **Rebuilding Together** and **Habitat.** The sentence is simply, *"A real grant is free to get — I don't pay a fee, I don't sign in my yard, I don't give my numbers on the phone, and I go to the real USDA office myself."*

Deacon Thurman Biggers wired $600 to a ghost because nobody told him the real thing was free — and now a widow he saved from doing the same sits beside him at a church table, handing the next old saint the number that costs nothing to call.

**For deacons, trustees, benevolence teams, senior and homebound ministries, and anybody in your pews who owns an aging home on a fixed income:**

Somewhere in your congregation is a widow, a widower, an elder who **owns their home free and clear** — the paid-off homeplace, the family land, the house a husband built — and **cannot afford to fix it.** The roof leaks toward the wiring. The steps are rotted and there's no ramp. The furnace is on borrowed time. On paper they're secure; in truth they're **land rich and cash poor,** and their house is failing around them faster than a fixed check can catch. That is the exact person the "government home-repair grant" scam is built for — because *"there's a grant to fix your house"* is a sentence just true enough to open the door. And the vultures come in a specific order: a **text or call saying you're "approved" for a grant you never applied for, if you'll just pay a "release fee"**; a **truck in the yard** with a "USDA-approved contractor" who wants a **blank paper, a big deposit, and a lien on your land**; a **phone imposter** wanting your **Social Security and bank numbers** "to deposit the funds"; a **"grant writer"** charging a fat upfront fee for a **free** application; and, aimed straight at the paid-off house, the **"sign your deed over and we'll fix it free"** equity-strip. Every one of them works the same two things: the **ache of a house you can't fix** and the **hope that help is real.**

What Deacon Thurman Biggers learned the hardest way — six hundred dollars wired to a ghost, and fifteen years of silent shame, before he found out the real program had been free the whole time — is the whole thing your church can carry: **there IS a real one — USDA Rural Development's Section 504 Home Repair loan and grant — it is FREE to apply for at the local USDA office, the loan is about 1% and the grant is for owners 62 and up who can't repay a loan and needs to fix what's dangerous; a real grant NEVER charges a fee to "release" it; you never give your SSN or bank number to a caller; and you never sign a contract, a lien, or your deed without free legal aid reading it first.**

Put these four lines on your benevolence and senior-ministry resource card:

**THERE'S A REAL PROGRAM — AND IT'S FREE TO APPLY. USDA Rural Development's Section 504 Home Repair loan (≈1%) and grant (for homeowners 62+ who can't repay a loan, to remove health-and-safety hazards) is real. Apply FREE at your local USDA Rural Development office; check your address and income on the USDA eligibility map (eligibility.sc.egov.usda.gov). Rural includes many small towns and country addresses — checking costs nothing. Free help to apply: HUD counselors 1-800-569-4287.**

**A REAL GRANT NEVER CHARGES A FEE TO "RELEASE" IT. A text/call saying you're "approved" for a grant you never applied for and must pay a "processing," "approval," or "release" fee — or send a gift card, wire, Zelle, or prepaid card — is a SCAM, every time. So is a caller wanting your Social Security number or bank routing number "to deposit the funds." Hang up, call the real USDA office yourself, and report it to USDA OIG 1-800-424-9121 and reportfraud.ftc.gov.**

**NEVER SIGN IN YOUR YARD — GET IT IN WRITING, AND NEVER SIGN A LIEN OR YOUR DEED. A door-knocking "contractor" who wants a blank paper, a big cash deposit, or a lien is how families lose paid-off land. Illinois law requires a WRITTEN contract over $1,000 and gives you a 3-DAY right to cancel a home-repair contract signed at home. Have Prairie State Legal Services (prairiestate.org · Kankakee Co.) or CARPLS 312-738-9200 / Legal Aid Chicago (Cook Co.) read anything before you sign — and NEVER sign your deed over for repairs.**

**THE REAL PROGRAM'S ONE STRING IS DISCLOSED — A SCAM'S IS HIDDEN. A Section 504 grant may have to be repaid if you sell within a few years — that's a real, written, explained federal rule, not a con. A legitimate condition is on the paper and spoken out loud; a thief's "fee," lien, or "just sign here" is sprung on you scared. Ask the real USDA to explain every term, and bring somebody with you — a deacon, a counselor, a lawyer. Nobody should sit at that table alone.**
