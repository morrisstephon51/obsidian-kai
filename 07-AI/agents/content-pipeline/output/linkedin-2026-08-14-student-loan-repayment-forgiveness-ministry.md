There is a young man in that church everybody points to when they want to prove it can be done.

He is the first one in his family to finish college. Four years, a real degree, walked across the stage while his mother cried in the third row and his grandmother stood up before they even called his name. That photo is on the wall in both their houses. The church clapped for him from the pulpit. For a whole family that had been told, one way or another, for a long time, that a place like theirs does not produce that — he was the proof that it does. He carried that for all of them. He still does.

And he is drowning, quietly, and he has not told a soul.

Because the degree did not come free. There was no college fund, no family money, no cushion — there was him, eighteen years old, signing for federal student loans because every adult he trusted told him that was the responsible thing, that was the smart thing, that was the thing you do to get out and get up. So he did it. He borrowed what it cost. And now he is twenty-eight, working a real job — a paraprofessional at a district school, or a caseworker at a small nonprofit down the road, the kind of work that helps people and does not pay much — and the balance on those loans feels bigger than his whole life. The monthly bill is more than he can carry on what he makes. So he fell behind. And then he fell further.

And nobody taught him what happens next, so he assumed the worst of it.

The letters started coming in a different tone. Words like default. Words like collections. Words that started reaching toward his paycheck and his tax refund. His credit score, the thing he was told to protect, started sliding, and every apartment application and car loan started to feel like a door closing. He stopped opening the mail. He tells his family he is "figuring it out." He cannot make himself say the other thing out loud — that the degree they are all so proud of came home with a chain around his neck, and that the success story is scared to check his own bank account.

Here is what almost nobody sat this young man down and told him: the number on that bill is not the only payment there is.

There are federal repayment plans built to tie the monthly payment to what you actually earn instead of to the size of the balance — income-driven repayment, they call it — and for a young man doing low-paid public-service work, that can be a very different number than the one that has been crushing him. He does not know that. He thinks the bill is the bill, take it or leave it, and he has been leaving it because he cannot lift it. Nobody told him you can call the people who hold the loan and say, "this payment is impossible, what are my actual options," and that the honest answer is often "more than you think." I have to say it carefully, because these programs and their rules change often and only his official servicer and the official federal student-aid site can tell him what applies to his loans right now — but the point is he never even asked, because he never knew there was a question.

And here is the part that would stop him cold if anyone said it to him: the job he already works might be exactly the kind of job some forgiveness programs are built for. Public service. Government. A lot of nonprofits. Public schools. Healthcare. The very fields full of people who help other people for not much money — that is often precisely who loan-forgiveness programs are aimed at. He is a caseworker. He is a school aide. He might be standing inside the door and not know it. I am not telling him he is definitely forgiven — nobody honest can promise that, and the rules move — I am telling him to ask his servicer and check the official site, because an enormous number of people who might qualify never once ask, and you cannot be told yes about a thing you never raised.

And a default is not a life sentence, though it feels like one. He thinks he is finished — that once you fall in, you are in for good, credit wrecked, refund gone, nothing to be done. That is not true. There are real ways back out of default, and a collection notice or a threat to your refund is not automatically the end of the story. The steps are specific and his servicer and the official process set them, so this is not a thing to guess at — but "in default" is very often "in default, for now, until you do the thing nobody told you existed."

Because — and hear this part, because it hunts young people in exactly his spot — the minute somebody with student debt starts searching for help, the wolves are already in the results and on the phone. The "debt relief" companies that charge a monthly fee to "manage" loans they are not even doing anything to. The robocalls and the texts that say "your loans qualify for forgiveness, act now," dressed up to sound official. The outfits that ask for his FSA ID and password so they can "handle it for him" — which is exactly how they seize control of the account. The up-front fees. The lookalike websites. He might already be paying one of them, a little every month, for the privilege of not doing the free thing he could do himself. Applying for federal repayment and forgiveness is free. You do it through the official site and your own servicer. No honest company charges you a fee to lower a payment or apply for forgiveness, and nobody — nobody — needs your FSA password to help you.

So he sits in a pew the whole family points to, the proof that it can be done, avoiding his mail, paying a "relief" company for nothing, sliding deeper into a default he thinks is permanent, working a public-service job that might be exactly what forgiveness was built for — and never once told any of it. He is not going without a fix because he failed. He did the hardest, rightest thing his family ever asked of him. He is going without because nobody told him the payment could bend to his paycheck, that his job might count, and that there is a way out of the hole.

Here is the thing about the young man across town. Same degree. Same first-in-the-family. Same good job that does not pay much. But he had a coworker on his second week who said, "Call your servicer. Get on a plan based on what you make, not what you owe. And ask about public-service forgiveness — this job counts, dummy, that's the whole point of it. And if you're behind, there's a way out of default, don't just let it ride." And when the "debt relief" texts came, that same coworker said, "Delete those. That's a scam. You never pay to lower your payment, and you never give anybody your login." So the young man across town has a payment he can actually breathe under and a clock quietly ticking toward forgiveness on a job he was going to work anyway. He is not smarter than the young man in the pew. He is not more deserving. He just got told, by somebody who knew, what the fix was and where the real door is. One of them is building toward free. The other is paying a scammer to sink. And the difference was never the work ethic or the degree. It was who had somebody in their ear saying the payment can bend, your job might count, and default is not forever.

The young man in the pew can be walked to that same door now. Not a bailout he should be ashamed of. The plain, free, already-existing options on a debt he took on for all the right reasons.

Because this is where a tool like AI actually meets a young man like him, quietly, at eleven o'clock at night with the mail he has been avoiding, doing the part his fear and his pride have been blocking. It can be the patient someone who explains what "income-driven repayment" and "servicer" and "default" and "rehabilitation" and "consolidation" and "forgiveness" and "forbearance" actually mean, in plain words, as many times as he needs, and never once makes the family's success story feel like a failure. It can help him find the real, official federal student-aid site and figure out who his actual servicer even is, and steer him clear of the fee-charging "relief" companies and the lookalike sites. It can help him line up his own basic facts before he logs in — who holds his loans, roughly what he earns — so he walks into that call ready instead of ashamed. It can draft the exact questions to ask the servicer or a legitimate nonprofit counselor. And it can help him actually read a default or collection notice and find the deadline buried in it before it passes.

Not to be his loan advisor of record, and not to be his lawyer. Let that be plain. The machine does not decide which plan he qualifies for or whether one dime of his debt gets forgiven — only his official servicer and the official process can look at his actual loans and say. It does not give legal or financial advice, and for a garnishment, a disputed loan, or a servicer error, a free legal-aid attorney or a legitimate nonprofit counselor should guide him. It does not log into his account, and it does not apply for anything for him. It does not replace the servicer, the counselor, or the lawyer. And it must never, ever be handed his FSA ID password, his Social Security number, or his account login — that is exactly what the fake sites and the "relief" companies are fishing for, and a public AI tool is not the place for any of it. It is only the patient someone who makes a frightening, confusing, shame-soaked process feel possible, so that a young man who carried his whole family's hope across that stage can finally get out from under what it cost him.

That is the gap. It was never that the young man everybody points to is less deserving of a way out than the young man across town. It is that one of them had somebody say "your payment can bend, your job might count, and default is not forever — here's the real site," and the other got a stack of unopened mail, a head full of shame, and a phone full of scammers pretending to be the help. Not less faithful. Not less hardworking. Just never told the fix was real and free and his to reach for.

A word of care, plainly, and I will say the important parts twice. This is general navigation, not an endorsement of any one company, servicer, counselor, or attorney over another, and it is not legal or financial advice. AI tools can be confidently wrong, and student-loan rules change often, so never trust a plan name, a payment, a deadline, or a dollar amount one gives you without confirming it with the official federal student-aid site, your actual loan servicer, or a legitimate nonprofit counselor — the specific programs and their rules move, and only the official site and your servicer can tell you what applies to your loans. Never put private information into a public AI tool or hand it to a company: not your FSA ID password, not your Social Security number, not your account login. And hear this one twice, because it is built for people in exactly this spot: applying for federal repayment and forgiveness is FREE, so no one honest ever charges you a fee to lower a payment or "apply for forgiveness," and nobody needs your FSA password to help you — anyone who does is a scam, and a fee-charging "debt relief" company is not the same thing as the real, free help that exists. Route the real help to real, trusted places: the official federal student-aid site and your own loan servicer to see your plans and apply; a legitimate nonprofit student-loan or credit counselor for free guidance; a free legal-aid provider for a garnishment, a dispute, or a servicer error; and 211 or 211.org to bridge food, rent, and utilities while you get the payment down.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Dolton. Robbins. Chicago Heights. Matteson. South Holland. Not a webinar. A room. Real people. A real first-in-the-family graduate, taught how to make the same tool the young man across town never even needed sit with him at the kitchen table and help him find the real servicer, understand the plan that bends to his paycheck, ask whether his job counts, read the notice, and dodge the scam — so the young man everybody in that building points to as proof can finally get out from under the chain the degree came home with.

The most promising young people in our churches are not sinking because they failed. They are sinking because nobody ever told them the fix was real, and free, and theirs to reach for.

We are here to walk them to it.

The plug is in.
