There is a woman at that church who knows which grandmothers are raising their grandbabies.

Nobody assigned it to her. She just started noticing. The grandmother who showed up one Sunday with two little ones and a garbage bag of clothes and no explanation, because her daughter went to prison on a Tuesday and the kids had nowhere else to go. The great-aunt who took in a nephew when his mother relapsed. The grandmother burying a daughter one week and enrolling that daughter's children in school the next. She started gathering them. A circle after second service, coffee in the fellowship hall, a place for women raising children they never planned to raise, on a fixed income that already ran out before the first of the month.

And then the hard part starts. Because taking in a child you love is one thing. Getting that child what the state already sets aside for children in exactly this situation is another, and the distance between the two is a maze nobody hands a grieving grandmother a map to.

Because there are two doors, and no one tells her which one she walked through. If a grandmother just takes the kids, quietly, the way our families always have, she gets almost nothing. If those same children come to her through the state as a licensed relative foster parent, she gets the full board payment, medical coverage, and services attached to it. Same child. Same grandmother. Same love. A difference of hundreds of dollars a month, and she never knew the second door was there. Because there is a TANF grant she can get for the children alone, that does not count her income at all, that would put food on the table this month, and she has never heard the words child-only grant in her life. Because this state has a subsidized guardianship program, so she does not have to adopt her own grandchild — does not have to sign a paper that feels like erasing her daughter — just to get the help that comes with keeping them. Because the school told her she could not enroll the children without custody papers, and she believed them and kept a child home for three weeks, when the law gave her the right to enroll them the whole time. Because the clinic would not treat a sick boy without a parent's consent, and there is a short-term guardianship form that would have fixed it in an afternoon, and no one at that front desk mentioned it. Because at some point somebody says the word adoption, and somebody else says guardianship, and the two are not the same, and the choice will shape this family for the rest of their lives, and nobody sits her down and lays it out.

So the woman with the circle tries to hold all of it. For every grandmother on her list. From what she picked up helping the last one. She tells a grandmother the state does not help with this, because that is what happened to her cousin, not knowing there was a formal door that pays. She never says child-only grant, because no one ever said it to her. She is one faithful woman, working from memory of a system that changes its own rules faster than any one person can keep them.

So the money stays on the table. The grandmother takes the informal door because it is the only one she can see, and the board payment that would have kept the lights on never comes. The child-only grant goes unclaimed. The children start school three weeks late. The subsidized guardianship that would have steadied the whole family never gets filed. And a grandmother who did the most sacred thing a person can do — stood in the gap for a child whose parents could not — does it on her Social Security check and her knees, when the state had set money aside for that child all along.

Here is what a family with money and a good lawyer gets.

They get a kinship navigator who knows both doors and walks them through the one that pays. They get a children's-law attorney who files the guardianship, secures the subsidy, and makes the school enroll the child the same day. They get someone paid to know that a child-only grant does not count the caregiver's income, that a short-term guardianship solves the doctor's office, that adoption and guardianship are different roads with different costs. That family is not more devoted than the grandmother in the third pew. They were just handed a navigator, and she was handed two grandbabies and a garbage bag and told to figure out the rest.

She can have that help too. For free. On the same phone a grandmother called her from.

She can find out that the formal relative-foster door exists and what it pays, and decide with real numbers instead of a rumor. She can learn that a child-only TANF grant does not count her income and how to apply for it this week. She can learn that subsidized guardianship means she does not have to adopt her grandchild to get support. She can get the plain words a grandmother has the right to say to a school so a child is enrolled tomorrow, not in three weeks. She can learn that a short-term guardianship form solves the doctor's office. She can get the difference between adoption and guardianship laid out in plain language, so a family chooses on purpose instead of by default. She can get the whole maze — the two doors, the child-only grant, the subsidy, the school, the consent form, the adoption-versus-guardianship fork — broken into an order of operations she can run this week, and then walk it in to the free kinship navigator line and the legal-aid children's-law clinic that will sit with the family the rest of the way.

Not to replace what she does. The circle is hers. The coffee in the fellowship hall is hers. The grandmother crying in the parking lot the week she buried her daughter is hers, and no machine will ever sit in that car and just stay. It only takes the maze off her shoulders so she can do the part only she can do, which is to look a worn-out grandmother in the eye and say you are not doing this alone, these babies are ours too, and I am not going anywhere.

That is the gap. It was never that the grandmothers in our pews deserve a harder road to what the state already set aside for their grandchildren than the family that could afford a lawyer. It is that that family got a navigator, and ours got a phone call at midnight and two children who needed her by morning.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities and the people navigating resources beside them in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Matteson. Dolton. Not a webinar. A room. Real problems. A real grandmother, a real grant finally claimed, a real child enrolled in school the same week instead of the next month.

The grandmothers standing in the gap for children whose parents could not are not behind. They were just never handed the tools the funded programs take for granted.

We are here to hand them over.

The plug is in.
