**Key facts:**
- **A "contract for deed" / "rent-to-own home" is NOT the same as owning — until the deed is signed over and recorded in your name, the SELLER still legally owns the house.** No matter how many years you've paid, no matter that you fixed the roof and pay the heat bill, the person on the deed is the legal owner. Knowing this is the first thing that protects you.
- **Illinois law gives residential installment-contract buyers real protections — and a snap-forfeiture may not be legal.** Under Illinois's installment-sales-contract law, sellers of a home on contract generally must **record the contract** and **disclose** the property's taxes, liens, and any existing mortgage — and, critically, **if you've paid a substantial portion, the seller often can't simply "forfeit" and evict you but may have to go through a court process more like a foreclosure,** which can give you the right to catch up (reinstate) or be treated fairly. **A missed payment is not automatically the end.** Do not walk away on a threat letter alone — get a lawyer to tell you your specific rights.
- **Pull the title BEFORE you sign one — and right now if you're already in one.** At the **Cook County Clerk's land-records office** you can see **who legally owns the property, whether your contract was recorded, and whether there are liens or a mortgage;** at the **Cook County Treasurer** you can see **unpaid back taxes and any tax sale.** Ten dollars and an afternoon at the county building can reveal everything the seller hoped you'd never look up.
- **Undisclosed back taxes, liens, or an unrecorded contract are red flags — and often the seller's breach.** If you're paying on a house that's secretly loaded with the seller's unpaid taxes or a hidden mortgage, or a contract that was never recorded, that isn't your failure — it may be the seller violating the law, and a lawyer or the **Attorney General (1-800-386-5438)** should hear about it.
- **Never pay a surprise "reinstatement," "deed-clearing," or "pay-to-stop-eviction" fee to a caller, texter, or letterhead you can't verify.** Real disputes over a home go through **the contract, a lawyer, and a court** — not a wired lump sum to "National Deed Solutions." And the "forfeiture" the letter waved around like a loaded gun was, on these facts, a great deal less certain than National Deed Solutions wanted a scared man to believe.

He spent a Saturday at the county building. Ten dollars and an afternoon, exactly like she said. He came home with the whole story on paper: the deed still in the seller's name, the contract nowhere in the records, the tax delinquency, and — the part that made his lawyer sit up — an old mortgage the seller had taken out against the very house he'd been "selling" Curtis for six years. The family paying like owners; the seller quietly borrowing against the roof over their heads.

His attorney sent National Deed Solutions and the seller a letter of a very different kind than the ones Curtis had been getting. A complaint went to the **Illinois Attorney General's Consumer Fraud line at 1-800-386-5438** — the unrecorded contract, the undisclosed taxes, the hidden mortgage, the churn. The forfeiture threat, once a lawyer was standing in front of it, stopped arriving.

And Curtis called the **HUD-approved housing counselor at 1-800-569-4287,** and sat down — for free — with a woman who looked at his real, healed, six-years-later numbers and did not shake her head. It is not a fairy tale and it is not finished; untangling a house sold this crooked takes months, and there are still hard days in it. But Curtis Braswell is on a road now that ends with a recorded deed in his own name and the seller's back taxes and hidden mortgage handled as the seller's problem, not his — a road he did not know existed the night he almost wired away his sons' fund to "clear a deed" he already had the right to fight for.

His mother's yellow roses came back along the south fence that spring, the way they do. Curtis stood at the kitchen window he'd painted, in the house he was — finally, legally, actually — on his way to owning, and watched his youngest run through the yard, and did not once think about moving.

At the next Deed & Key table, he sat down at the *other* end — next to a young woman holding a folder like it might bite, who had just signed a rent-to-own on a house off a sign in a yard, and thought she owned it.

Curtis took off his cap. "First thing," he said gently. "And it's the hardest one, so we get it out of the way first."
