There is a man in that church who works hurt. Fifty-one years old, loads trucks and runs a forklift, and a few weeks back something let go in his lower back and shoulder while he was lifting — and instead of anyone asking if he was alright, his supervisor told him to walk it off and not make it a thing. So he did. He buys his own painkillers at the drugstore, paid cash for one doctor visit he could barely afford, and clocks in every morning gritting his teeth, because he is certain that if he reports it, he loses the job — and he has told almost no one, because in his mind a working man who gets hurt and speaks up is a man who gets replaced.

You know him. He did not choose to get hurt. He was doing the hard physical work that pays his bills and that his body has given to that company for years, and one ordinary lift went wrong the way it does. He is certain of two things, and both of them are hurting him. The first is that filing a claim is the fastest way to get himself fired — that the moment his name is on paper as the guy who reported an injury, they will find a reason to cut him. The second is that even if he filed, guys like him don't win those — too many rules, too much paperwork, some system built for other people that was never going to side with a forklift driver from the south suburbs. So he stays quiet. He works injured, pays out of pocket, and carries the whole thing alone.

And he may be walking past protection he already has a right to. That is the part that has to be said plainly, and I will say it again before I am done: reporting a work injury is generally your right, legitimate help to understand and file a claim is free or works on a regulated share with no upfront cash fee, and no one honest offers you a high-interest loan against your settlement or demands your Social Security number and a payment to "process" your claim. He does not know that yet. He does not know that a workplace injury is usually something he has a right to report, and that firing him for filing a legitimate claim is in many cases illegal. He does not know that the medical care for a work injury — and often a share of the wages he loses while hurt — is exactly what workers' compensation exists to cover, so he should not be the one paying cash. He does not know there is a clock on reporting, and that staying silent to protect the job can quietly cost him the very protection built for this. Nobody sat him down and told him. So he assumes the door is locked and keeps working through the pain.

And there are doors. Real ones. Doors nobody names for a fifty-one-year-old man who is too proud to say he is scared and too worried about his paycheck to fight a company he assumes holds all the cards.

Start with the first thing he needs to hear: getting hurt doing heavy work does not make you careless, and working through real pain to keep a paycheck is not milking anything — it is the opposite, a man carrying an injury on his own back rather than risk his family's income. Not knowing your rights is not stupidity; it is the exact silence a bad boss counts on, the same silence the scammers count on too. And needing help to understand a comp claim is not weakness. It is using the free help that already exists and was built for exactly this moment.

So here are the doors almost no one names for him plainly. The first is the one his fear hides completely: he generally has a right to report a workplace injury, there are usually short deadlines to report it and to file, and punishing or firing him for filing a legitimate claim is in many cases against the law — governed by state rules, so confirm the specifics and the clock with the state workers' comp agency or legal aid, but do not stay silent by default and let a deadline pass. The second is the one that stops the bleeding from his own wallet: workers' comp is meant to cover medical treatment for a work injury, which means a hurt worker generally should not be paying cash out of pocket or burning his own health insurance for something that happened on the job. The third is the one nobody mentions: a portion of his lost wages — temporary wage replacement while he can't work or is stuck on light duty — may be available, governed by state rules, not a number to guess at. The fourth is the one that costs him nothing to use: the state workers' comp board, legal aid, and worker centers will explain his rights at no charge, and many comp attorneys work on contingency with no upfront fee — though he should understand the fee arrangement plainly before he signs anything. The fifth is the one for if it turns ugly: if they fire him, cut his hours, or punish him for reporting, that itself may be unlawful, so writing down the dates, what was said, and who said it, and getting to legal aid, matters. And underneath all of it, for the fear of losing everything and the pride that keeps him quiet, there are trusted mentors, his faith, one call to 211, and 988 if the weight ever gets too heavy to hold alone. He is standing in front of a whole row of doors — the right to report, medical care covered, wages while he heals, free help to understand it, protection if they retaliate — and he is frozen certain there are none, because nobody ever told him a single one was his.

But hear this warning before he signs a single paper or takes a dime from a stranger, because the scams hunt hurt workers who are scared and short on cash harder than almost anyone. Hear it plainly, and I will say it again at the end: real help understanding a claim is free or works on a regulated share, and nobody legitimate charges you an upfront cash fee to file or hands you a loan against your own settlement. Beware the "settlement advance" or "lawsuit loan" outfit offering fast cash against a future settlement at brutal, compounding cost — a legitimate claim does not require you to borrow against it at loan-shark rates. Beware anyone charging an upfront "filing fee" or "claim service" fee to file your claim for you — the state agency does not charge you to file, and a legitimate comp attorney is generally paid a regulated share out of a recovery, not cash up front. Beware the caller or text pressing for his full Social Security number, his bank account, or a card to "process," "verify," or "release" his claim — real claims move through the state agency, the employer's insurer, and a licensed attorney, not a stranger who called him. The rule is flat: reporting a work injury is your right, real help to file is free or works on a regulated contingency with no upfront cash, and no honest party offers you a high-interest advance on your settlement or demands your Social and a payment to "process" your claim — so anyone charging an upfront filing fee, dangling a settlement loan, or demanding your SSN and a card to "release" your claim is the danger, not the doorway.

Now one more thing, because it matters and the scam hides right next to it: a real official notice is not the same as a scam call. If the state workers' comp board or the employer's insurance carrier sends him a real letter with a deadline to respond, to appeal a denial, or to attend a scheduled medical exam, that is genuine paperwork with a real clock on it, and ignoring it can quietly forfeit the very claim he needs — so he must not treat every envelope like a con. Part of the help, a big part, is simply telling the official letter apart from the shark's pitch — and that is exactly the kind of thing to bring to legal aid or the state agency, not to guess at alone.

So he almost fell for it. Not because he is foolish — foolish is not the word for a man carrying a real injury on his own back to protect his family's paycheck. Not because he is naive — he is careful with every dollar, careful enough that a "settlement advance" ad nearly got him to sign a loan against a claim he had not even filed yet. He almost fell for it because nobody ever walked him to the real doors, and the only voices offering him anything were the ones built to take a cut of a man in pain.

Here is the thing about the man across town. Same story. Same wrenched back, same "walk it off," same certainty that filing meant firing, same cash out of his own pocket for pills. But a brother from church sat down next to him and said: "Before you pay another dime out of pocket or sign anything, report it in writing and call legal aid or the state comp office — that part is free, and they can't just fire you for filing a real claim. And don't you dare take one of those settlement loans." So he did. He learned he had a right to report, and a deadline he almost blew past. He got his treatment covered and a part of his lost wages while his back healed, and he never took a loan against his claim or paid a nickel to "file." He was not smarter than the man in our pew. He was not luckier. He had one person who knew the doors were real and free, and who named them before the fear and the loan-shark ad won. One man got protected and healed. The other almost signed away a chunk of a claim he hadn't filed and kept working injured. The whole difference was somebody who said "report it, call legal aid, don't take the loan," and a silence where nobody ever did.

The man in our pew can be met before he signs that first predatory paper. Not with a lecture about how he should have been more careful, or filed already, or known his rights by now. With a hand on his shoulder and the plain truth that getting hurt at work is exactly what this system is for, that reporting it is his right, and that the help to understand it is free.

Because this is where a tool like AI actually meets a man like him — quietly, at the kitchen table, after a shift he shouldn't have worked, on a night he finally admits how much it hurts — doing the part that the not-knowing has been blocking. It can be the patient someone that explains what "workers' compensation," "no-fault," "reporting the injury," "the claim," "temporary disability," "independent medical exam," "contingency fee," and "retaliation" actually mean, in plain words, as many times as he needs, and never once make a hard-working man feel small for not already knowing the vocabulary of a system he was never taught. It can help him find the state workers' comp agency, legal aid, and a worker center he can reach himself. It can help him understand the reporting deadline and figure out what he should be writing down — dates, what happened, who he told. It can help him read a claim letter or a denial he has been too anxious to open and find what it is actually asking. And it can name the settlement-loan pitch, the upfront "filing fee," and the SSN-phishing "claim processor" for exactly what they are, before they take a cut of a man who is already hurting.

Not to be the one who decides anything. Let that be plain. The machine does not file his claim, and it does not get to decide what his claim is worth or whether he should settle — that belongs to the state agency, the insurer, and a licensed attorney and his treating doctor, not a chatbot's guess. It is not a lawyer, it is not a doctor, it is not a claims adjuster. And it must never, ever be fed his full Social Security number, his bank login, or his account numbers — a public AI tool is not the place for a single digit of that. It is only the patient someone that takes a frightening, prideful, jargon-soaked moment and makes it walkable, so that a good man who got hurt on the job does not work one more month in silent pain, or sign a loan against a claim he was too scared to file.

That is the gap. It was never that the man across town deserved it more, or was smarter, or managed better. Same age. Same injury. Same fear for the same paycheck. It is that one man had a brother who said "report it, call legal aid, don't take the loan," and the other was left alone with a bottle of drugstore painkillers, a boss telling him to walk it off, and a settlement-loan ad that felt like the only hand reaching out. Not more worthy. Not more careful. Just never told the doors were his.

A word of care, plainly, and I will say the important parts twice. This is general navigation, not legal or medical advice, and not a determination or a valuation of your claim. Workers' comp rights, deadlines, benefits, and outcomes are governed by state law and decided by the state workers' comp agency, the employer's insurer, and a licensed attorney and your treating doctor — not by a machine and not by a guess. AI tools can be confidently wrong, so never trust a deadline, a benefit amount, or a rights answer one gives you without confirming it with the state workers' comp board, legal aid, and a licensed attorney. And never put your full Social Security number, a bank login, or account numbers into a public AI tool, and never take a high-interest advance against a settlement or pay an upfront fee to "file" or "process" a claim. Hear the heart of it twice, because it is built for someone in exactly this spot: reporting a work injury is generally your right, legitimate help to understand and file a claim is free or works on a regulated share with no upfront cash fee, and no honest party offers you a loan against your settlement or demands your SSN and a payment to "process" your claim — so if someone is charging you to file, dangling a settlement loan, or demanding your Social and a card to "release" your claim, walk away. And do not confuse that scam with a real official notice — if the state comp board or the insurer sends you a genuine letter with a deadline to respond, appeal, or attend an exam, do not ignore it; answer it and get to legal aid fast. Route the real help to real, trusted places: the state workers' compensation agency to report and file; legal aid or a licensed workers' comp attorney to know your rights and your options; a worker center for guidance and support; a trusted mentor and your faith community for the fear and the pride; your church's benevolence fund for a true gap while wages are short; 988 if the weight gets too heavy; and 211 or 211.org to find local legal aid, worker, and injury-help resources in one call.

A real injured worker, taught how to make the same tool the settlement-loan sharks and the scammers already use sit with him at the kitchen table, explain the words nobody explained, help him find the state agency and legal aid, understand the deadline and what to write down, read the denial letter he was afraid to open, and spot the settlement-loan and filing-fee scams for exactly what they are — so he can understand the rights he actually has and not get bled on the way to them.

The men and women in our churches are not working hurt because they were careless or looking to game anything. They are working hurt because they got injured doing hard work, were told to walk it off, and because nobody ever told them reporting is their right, the care and lost wages may be covered, and no honest party ever charges a fee to file or offers a loan against a claim.
