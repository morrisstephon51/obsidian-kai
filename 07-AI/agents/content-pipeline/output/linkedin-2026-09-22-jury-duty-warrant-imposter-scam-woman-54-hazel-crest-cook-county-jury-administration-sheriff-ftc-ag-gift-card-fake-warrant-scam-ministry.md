If a caller claims you owe a jury "fine," hang up and call **Jury Administration at the number listed on the official .org/.gov site** (look it up yourself — never use the number that called you) to confirm you have no summons and no warrant.
- **The Illinois Jury Act (705 ILCS 305)** — state law that makes it **unlawful for an employer to fire, threaten, or penalize** you for receiving or responding to a jury summons or serving on a jury. Serving does not have to cost you your job.
- **You can be excused or rescheduled for FREE.** A hardship excusal or a one-time postponement is a normal, no-cost request you make **to the court** (online or by mail) — no service charges a "fee" to defer jury duty, and no legitimate court demands payment to "clear" a missed date.

**Scam spine:** A person who has never been in trouble a day in her life — who would rather die than have "warrant" attached to her name, and whose paycheck depends on a clean record — is exactly who this scam is built to break. It is a government-imposter call, and the tells run in a chain. First, the **spoofed authority**: the caller ID shows the real Sheriff's office or a "312"/"708" courthouse number, and the voice gives a name, a badge or "officer ID," and a case or "citation" number — "Deputy ___, Cook County Sheriff's Office, regarding a failure to appear." Second, the **manufactured crime**: you "missed jury duty" on a date you don't remember getting, and now there is a **bench warrant, a body attachment, or a contempt-of-court citation** with a **fine** — usually $500 to $2,000. Third, the **pressure and isolation**: an arrest is "imminent today," a "warrant squad" could come to your home or your job, and you must **stay on the line, tell no one, and resolve it now** — the fear of being handcuffed in front of your kids or at your workplace is the whole product. Fourth, the **impossible payment**: to "post the bond over the phone" or "pay the fine to recall the warrant," you must use a **gift card, prepaid card, wire, Cash App, Zelle, or a Bitcoin ATM** — no court, sheriff, or clerk on earth is paid this way, ever. Fifth, the **authenticity theater**: real judge names, a real courthouse address, official-sounding hold music, a "supervisor" who confirms the story, and instructions to "verify" by calling the spoofed number back. Sixth, the **identity harvest**: "to clear the warrant, I need to verify your Social Security number and date of birth," or a link to a fake "jury duty portal" or "county payment site" that swallows your card and your identity. Seventh, the **reverse hook — fake jury pay**: a friendlier version says you're *owed* jury "stipend" or "compensation" and just need to pay a small "processing fee" or hand over your bank login to receive it. Under all of it is a simple lie dressed as the law: that a phone call, a threat, and a gift card can make a warrant appear or disappear. Gwen's call had the badge number, the case number, the real Sheriff's number on her screen, and a man who never once raised his voice — and by the time she reached the church parking lot she had $600 in her hand and directions to a pharmacy.

**Key legal and program facts:**

- **Courts summon jurors by mail — and a missed date is handled by mail, not a phone call for money.** In Illinois you learn about jury service through a **mailed summons**, and if you miss a date, the court's response is another **mailed notice**, not a deputy phoning to demand a gift card. A real bench warrant is resolved in a courtroom, in front of a judge — not over the phone, and never with prepaid cards.

- **Your job is protected when you serve.** Under the **Illinois Jury Act (705 ILCS 305)**, an employer may not fire, threaten, or penalize you for receiving or responding to a jury summons or for serving. You generally must give reasonable notice, but the fear the scammer sells — "you'll be arrested at work and lose everything" — has the law exactly backwards: the law is on the side of the person who shows up to serve.

- **Being excused or rescheduled is free.** Genuine hardship excusals and one-time postponements are ordinary, **no-cost** requests made directly to the court (often online or by mail). Anyone charging a "fee" to defer jury duty, or a "fine" to "reschedule," is running a scam — the court does not sell relief from jury duty.

- **No court, clerk, or sheriff takes gift cards, wire, crypto, or Cash App — and caller ID lies.** Payment by prepaid card or wire is a certain sign of fraud, and a number on your screen proves nothing; spoofing is trivial. The safe move is always the same: **hang up, look up the official number yourself, and call the court or Sheriff back** to confirm there is no summons and no warrant.

- **Never "verify" your identity to a caller — and if you paid, report fast.** A real official who already had a warrant would not need your Social Security number over the phone. Give nothing. If you already sent money or read off a gift-card number, call **your bank/the card issuer immediately**, then report to the **FTC (reportfraud.ftc.gov)**, **IC3 (ic3.gov)**, and the **Illinois Attorney General (800-386-5438)** — speed sometimes saves the money and always helps stop the next call.

**The Voice That Knew Her Case Number.**

The call came at 2:52 on a Tuesday, ten minutes before Gwen pulled the afternoon route out of the yard.

She almost let it ring — she was doing her walk-around, checking the tires, the flashers, the stop arm — but the screen said **COOK COUNTY SHERIFF**, and Gwen Maddox is not a woman who ignores the Sheriff.

The man was polite. That was the first thing she'd tell Sister Beverly later — how *polite* he was. Deputy something. An officer ID he said twice, slowly, so she could write it down. A case number. He was sorry to reach her at work, but this was time-sensitive.

She had **failed to appear for jury duty**.

"I never got a summons," Gwen said. Her mouth had gone dry.

"That's what the notice was for, ma'am. It was mailed to your address of record. When a juror fails to appear, the court issues a citation, and after the second missed date the judge signs a bench warrant. I'm showing an active warrant under your name as of this morning."

*A warrant. Under her name.* Gwen drove children. She got fingerprinted every year. A warrant was the one word that could end her.

"There's got to be a mistake."

"There may well be, and that's exactly why I'm calling instead of sending a unit. I'd rather resolve this administratively than have a deputy come to your place of employment. But I have to be honest with you — the warrant is active, and if it isn't cleared today, I can't stop the field team."

Gwen looked at the bus. At the list of eleven-year-olds she was about to go pick up.

"What do I — how do I clear it?"

"There's a failure-to-appear fine. Twelve hundred dollars. If you can post that today, I can recall the warrant electronically and you'll never hear from us again. If you can't, you'll need to surrender at the county building and post bond there, and I won't lie to you, that's an overnight process."

*Overnight.* Marcus got off his bus at 3:40. Nobody was home if she wasn't.

"I don't have twelve hundred dollars."

"I understand. Let me see what I can do." A pause, some clicking. "My supervisor has authorized a reduced amount to keep this out of the system — six hundred, paid today, and we call it a compliance bond. Now, because it's after banking hours for our office, the court's authorized payment vendor takes it in the form of a verified voucher. You'll go to a pharmacy — a Walgreens, a CVS — and purchase the amount in gift cards, and I'll stay on the line and take the confirmation numbers so I can post it directly to your case. Do not hang up, Ms. Maddox. If we're disconnected, the warrant reactivates and I can't intervene again."

And Gwen — steady, church-raised, nobody's fool — turned off the bus, called the dispatcher to say she had an emergency, and drove to the Walgreens on 183rd with a man's voice in her ear telling her not to tell anyone, because good people who help their neighbors and fear the law and have a grandbaby to protect are precisely the people this works on.

**Here is how jury service in Illinois actually works — and what a "deputy" on the phone prayed you'd never check:**

**1. The summons comes by mail, and so does the follow-up.**

You are notified of jury service by a **mailed summons**, and a missed date is handled by another **mailed notice** asking you to reschedule — not by a phone call threatening arrest. When something arrives by phone claiming a "jury fine," treat it as a scam until the real court, reached at a number you looked up, tells you otherwise.

**2. Your job is protected by law.**

The **Illinois Jury Act (705 ILCS 305)** makes it unlawful for an employer to fire, threaten, or penalize you for responding to a summons or serving. The scammer's core threat — that serving or "failing to appear" will cost you your livelihood — is the opposite of the truth.

**3. Excusal and rescheduling are free.**

Hardship excusals and one-time postponements are ordinary, **no-fee** requests made to the court. Nobody legitimate charges to defer jury duty or "clear" a missed date. A fee or a fine demanded over the phone is the scam announcing itself.

**4. No court is paid in gift cards, and caller ID can't be trusted.**

Gift cards, wires, crypto, Cash App, and Zelle are never how a court, clerk, or sheriff collects anything — and a familiar number on your screen proves nothing, because spoofing is easy. Hang up and call the official office back yourself.

**5. Give no information, and if you paid, move fast.**

A real official with a real warrant doesn't need your SSN over the phone. Verify nothing to an incoming caller. If you already paid or read off card numbers, call your bank and the card issuer immediately, then report to the FTC, IC3, and the Illinois Attorney General.

**And hold this next to all of it:** jury duty itself is real, ordinary, and even a little bit sacred — a mailed summons, a day at the courthouse, a modest check, and a job the law keeps safe for you while you go. Answering a real summons is a good and civic thing, not a danger. The caution here is narrow and specific: the *phone call* claiming a warrant and a fine, the gift-card "bond," the "stay on the line," the SSN "verification," and the fake "jury pay" that wants a processing fee — never about the honest, boring, protected duty that arrives, as it always has, in an envelope.
