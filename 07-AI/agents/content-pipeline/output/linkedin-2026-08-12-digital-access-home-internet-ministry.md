There is a boy in that church who does his homework in the parking lot.

You know him. Everybody knows him. He is on the honor roll. He says "yes ma'am" and carries the folding chairs and helps the older members to their cars, and after service, when everyone else has gone home, he is the one still sitting on the church steps or out in the lot with his phone tilted toward the building, because that is where the wifi is. At home there is one phone, the screen cracked and held together with a piece of tape, shared between him and his mother and his little sister, and there is no internet, because sixty dollars a month is a light bill, or a bus card, or a little more food, and every single month it loses to the things you can actually hold in your hand. So he does his essays with his thumbs. He submits his assignments from a McDonald's lot. He writes a paper on a screen the size of a playing card, standing outside a closed church, in the cold, because the family across town has something his family has quietly decided they will never have.

His teachers love him. Understand that clearly. He is not behind because he is slow. He is doing, on a taped-up phone with one bar of borrowed signal, the exact same assignment the kid across town is doing on a laptop the school never had to give him, on fiber internet that never blinks, in a quiet room with a door that closes. Same assignment. Same brain. Same effort. A completely different starting line.

And his family has decided, quietly and a long time ago, that this is just how it is. That internet is a luxury for people with more. That a computer in the house is a Christmas that keeps not coming. That "getting connected" is a thing for other people, people with room in the budget and somebody who knows how all of it works, and they are not those people, so they have stopped asking.

Here is the thing nobody has ever said to them plainly: the connection is not a luxury, and it is not out of reach, and the only reason no one ever walked them to it is that no one ever told them the door was theirs to walk through.

Because the big federal internet discount that everyone once told them about — the one a neighbor swore by, the one from the news — ended in 2024, and it was never brought back, so the family that finally worked up the nerve to try found a dead program and took it as one more closed door, never knowing that the other doors are still wide open. Because Lifeline is real, and federal, and still here, a monthly discount on a phone or on home internet for households that qualify, and you sign up for it through the government's own official site, never through a stranger at a folding table or a voice on the phone. Because the internet companies themselves run low-cost plans, around ten dollars a month, for families with a child on free lunch or on certain benefits, and you apply straight to the company, and the family that does not know that pays the full price or, far more often, simply does without. Because a computer is not a someday and not a Christmas that never comes — there are nonprofits that sell a good, refurbished, ready-to-use computer for less than a family spends on groceries in a month, built for exactly this household and no other. Because the public library they already drive past every week has free computers and free internet inside it, and many of those libraries will lend a family a laptop and an internet hotspot to take home, for nothing, on a library card they already qualify for. Because 211, three digits on the very phone they already carry, will connect them to local help they never knew was a phone call away. Because there are digital navigators — real, patient human beings whose entire free job is to sit down with a family, get them connected, get them a device, and teach them the first steps — and this family has never once been handed their number. Because the "free government phone," the "free tablet," the "you qualify for an internet grant" that arrives by robocall and by text and by a stranger with a clipboard, the one that wants a small fee or a gift card or a Social Security number, is a scam, and the family working hardest to get their child online is precisely the family the scammer is hunting. And because — this is the part underneath all of it — the connection was never just one gap. It is the gap behind every other gap. The financial aid form is online now. The benefits application is online. The job application is online. The doctor lives behind an online portal. The free business adviser's intake, the housing list, the school's own grade book where this family cannot even see how well their brilliant boy is actually doing — all of it, online. Every single door this church has ever tried to open for this family is a door you now reach only through a connection they do not reliably have.

So the boy keeps going the only way he knows how. Parking-lot wifi. A cracked screen. Essays typed with his thumbs in the cold. A mind that could run circles around that whole room, and one flickering bar of borrowed signal to run it on. He is not behind because he is less. He is one of the sharpest children in that building. He is behind because being connected turned out to be the quiet foundation under everything, and his family was never handed a single brick of it.

Here is the thing about the family across town, the one with resources. The internet was on in that house before their children could read. The laptop was already on the desk. When school moved online, when the doctor moved online, when the whole world moved online, someone in that family already knew how, or already knew someone who did, or could just pay someone to make it work. Being connected was never something that family achieved. It was the air they breathed. Their child is not smarter than the boy on the church steps. Their child's essays are not better. The signal was simply always there for him, from the day he was born, and it was never once there for ours.

He can have that too. Starting this week. With a discount his family did not know was still standing, and a library card they already qualify for.

He can get the Lifeline discount if his household qualifies, and learn to sign up only through the real site. His family can find the internet company's own low-cost plan and apply straight to the source instead of paying full price for less. They can get a real, working, refurbished computer for less than a month of groceries. They can walk into the library that was always theirs and walk out with a laptop and a hotspot in a bag. They can call 211 and reach a person. They can be handed a free digital navigator who sits with them until the light on the router turns green. They can have the whole tangle — the discount, the low-cost plan, the device, the library loan, the navigator, the scams to dodge — broken into a plain order of operations they can start this month, and then be walked straight to the real programs that carry it the rest of the way.

And once the signal is on, they have the thing the family across town never even had to name: a patient, tireless someone who knows how all of this works. The one that helps them see which programs they might qualify for. The one that reads the fine print beside them so no one gets tricked. The one that walks them through the application, gets the new computer set up when the box finally comes, and teaches a mother and a son their very first steps online without ever once making them feel slow for asking. A resourced family just always seemed to have that person. Ours never did. Now it can.

Not to do his homework. Let that be plain. The essays are his. The mind is his, and the discipline that had him writing papers with his thumbs in a parking lot rather than turning in nothing, and the teachers who see him, and the library that has always been the poor family's university — none of that gets replaced by a machine. It only takes the wall down. It only gets him online, gets him a real screen, and gets him to the exact same starting line the other boy has been standing on since the day he was born.

The Plug AI says three things, in this order. Get Connected. Get Skilled. Get into AI. This lane is the first word. You cannot get skilled if you cannot get on. You cannot get into AI from a cracked phone in a McDonald's lot. Connection is the front door to every other door, and for far too many of our families it has been painted, carefully and cruelly, to look like a wall.

That is the gap. It was never that the boy in our pews is less capable than the boy across town. It is that one of them was handed the signal, the screen, and the quiet room on the day he was born, and the other one has a gift, a phone held together with tape, and a church parking lot with one bar of wifi.

A word of care, plainly. Lifeline is real, but you sign up only at the official site, lifelinesupport.org, and never through a stranger, a robocall, or a text — a real program never charges you a fee, never asks for a gift card, and never demands your Social Security number over the phone. The "free government tablet," the "free internet grant," and the "you qualify, just pay to process it" offer are scams every time, and the harder a family is trying to get their child online, the more of these will come hunting them. Do not assume the old federal discount that ended in 2024 has quietly come back — check what is actually current. And eligibility for every one of these programs is specific and set by the program, not by me — so this is general navigation, not a guarantee that anyone qualifies, not an endorsement of any one internet company over another, and not legal or financial advice. Helping someone learn that Lifeline exists, or that the library lends hotspots, or that a ten-dollar plan is real, is not the same as enrolling them or promising they qualify. So route the actual eligibility and the actual sign-up to the real programs: Lifeline at lifelinesupport.org, the low-cost plan through your own internet provider's official site (Internet Essentials at internetessentials.com, AT&T Access, and the like), a low-cost refurbished computer through a nonprofit like PCs for People at pcsforpeople.org, your local public library for free access and laptop and hotspot lending, 211 or 211.org to find local help, and a free digital navigator through the National Digital Inclusion Alliance at digitalinclusion.org.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities and to the families getting online right beside them in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Matteson. Dolton. Not a webinar. A room. Real problems. A real boy who did his homework in a parking lot, a real discount his family did not know was still standing, a real computer that finally came in a box with his name on it, a real light on a router that finally turned green in his own house.

The children doing brilliant work on cracked phones in cold parking lots are not behind. They were just never handed the connection the connected never even have to think about.

We are here to hand it over.

The plug is in.
