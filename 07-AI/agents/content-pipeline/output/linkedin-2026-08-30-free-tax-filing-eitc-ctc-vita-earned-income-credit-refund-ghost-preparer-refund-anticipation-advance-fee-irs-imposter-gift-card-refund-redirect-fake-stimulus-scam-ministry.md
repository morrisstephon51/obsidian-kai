There is a woman in that congregation who flinches every time someone says the word "taxes." She is thirty-nine, she works two part-time jobs — mornings at one place, evenings at another — and in between she is raising kids on a schedule that has no gaps in it anywhere. And every year, when the forms start showing up, her stomach drops, because somewhere along the way she got it into her head that taxes are a bill she cannot pay, a trap that ends with her owing money she does not have. So a couple of years she just did not file. She hid from it. And what nobody ever sat her down and told her is the thing that would turn her whole picture upside down: she may not owe anything at all — she may be owed money. Real money. Thousands of dollars she earned by working two jobs, sitting in a credit with her name on it that she has never once claimed, because the Earned Income Tax Credit and the Child Tax Credit are refunds for exactly the kind of working parent she is, and every year she does not file is a year that money just sits there unclaimed.

You know her. She is not irresponsible, and she is not hiding from a debt she ran up — she is a woman who works harder than almost anyone and got scared of a system nobody ever explained to her in plain words. Nobody told her that for a working parent at her income, taxes are often not a bill at all but a refund — that the government takes money out of every check she earns and that filing is how she gets the rightful part of it back, plus credits designed specifically to put money into the hands of parents who work. Nobody told her that getting her taxes done is free — that the IRS never charges you to file, and that trained volunteers at free tax sites will do the whole return with her, sitting right beside her, at no cost. And nobody told her the one thing that would protect her the moment she finally reaches for help: that a real tax preparer signs the return and never takes a slice of her refund, so the first person who promises her a giant refund and wants a cut of it, or an upfront fee, or won't put their name on the return, is not a helper — they are a thief who found a scared, tired working mother at exactly the wrong moment.

And that has to be said plainly, and I will say it again before I am done: a woman working two jobs who is still owed money she never claimed is not someone who failed at anything. She is proof of the opposite. The Earned Income Tax Credit and the Child Tax Credit are not a handout and not welfare — they are money she earned with her own labor, wages the system holds back and gives back to working parents, and claiming them is not begging. It is collecting her own pay. Being afraid of a form nobody ever taught her to read is not stupidity — it is what happens when a working person is left alone with a system built by people who never had to work two jobs to understand it. That is not a deficit. That is a mother who has been carrying everyone and was never shown that the system owes her something back. And it is the exact moment the ghost preparer, the "instant refund" hustle, the IRS phone imposter, and the refund thief are all standing in front of, waiting for a tired woman who is finally desperate enough to trust the first confident voice that says "I can get you a big refund."

And there are doors. Real ones. Doors nobody names for a working mother who was raised to keep her head down, pay what she is told, and never ask anybody for anything.

Start with the first thing she needs to hear: getting her taxes done is free, the credits she may be owed are hers because she earned them, and she does not have to pay anyone a cut of a refund to claim it. The honest first move is not a strip-mall office that skims a third of her refund for an "instant" version of money that was already coming to her — it is a free tax site, because for a working parent the distance between "I'm scared I owe" and "here is the refund I earned" is often a few free doors nobody ever told her she could walk through.

So here are the doors almost no one names for her plainly. The first is free tax preparation: VITA sites — Volunteer Income Tax Assistance — and AARP Tax-Aide sites, where trained volunteers do the whole return with her for free, plus the Free File options at the real IRS.gov. The second is the credits themselves — the Earned Income Tax Credit and the Child Tax Credit, which she may be owed not just this year but for prior years she never filed, because in many cases you can still go back and file and claim a refund you missed. The third is help if she is behind or thinks she owes: a community tax clinic or a Low Income Taxpayer Clinic can help her sort out old years and deal with the IRS for free, so the fear of one bad year does not keep her from claiming everything she is owed. The fourth is the simplest and the most powerful: set up her own account at the real IRS.gov herself and have the refund deposited straight into her own bank account, in her own name, so no preparer ever touches the money. And underneath all of it is what she already carries — her own W-2s and pay stubs, the taxes already withheld from every single check she earned, her own steadiness — kept in her own hands, with her Social Security number and her bank login staying private, plus her church and a coworker whose family got back thousands in EITC for free at a VITA site instead of losing a third of it to a strip-mall preparer's "instant refund" fee, so she is not the first from this church to walk this, and the door was never as far as it looked.
