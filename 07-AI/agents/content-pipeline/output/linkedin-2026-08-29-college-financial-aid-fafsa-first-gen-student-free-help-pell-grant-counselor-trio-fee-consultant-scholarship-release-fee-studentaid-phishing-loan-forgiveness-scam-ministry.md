There is a woman in that congregation who keeps a folded college printout in her purse and takes it out at red lights when nobody is looking. She is forty-four. Her oldest, her daughter, is about to be the first person in the whole family — not the first in her house, the first in the whole family, going back as far as anyone can name — to go to college. And she is proud in a way that closes her throat when she talks about it. But last week she finally clicked all the way through to the part of the college website that lists the price, the real one, the number for a year, and her stomach dropped, because it is more money than this family makes in years, and she sat in the car in the parking lot and could not make the number make sense. She never went. Nobody taught her the words on that screen — FAFSA, Pell Grant, cost of attendance, net price, subsidized, work-study — and every one of them landed on her like a door she did not have the key to. And into that exact moment, that week, came a message: a "financial aid consultant" who, for a fee, would "fill out the FAFSA for you and guarantee your daughter gets aid," and a second one that said her daughter had "been selected for a scholarship" and just needed to pay a small processing fee to release it. To a mother sitting in a parking lot holding a number she cannot afford and a dream she will not give up, it did not sound like a trap. It sounded like someone who finally knew the way in.

You know her. She is not ignorant, and she is not behind, and she did not fail at anything — she is a working mother staring at a language nobody in her family ever needed to learn, because none of them ever stood where she is standing now. That is not a flaw. That is what being the first looks like. And nobody ever taught her the things that would have taken the number down out of the sky and put it back on the ground. Nobody told her that the FAFSA is free — that the F in FAFSA literally stands for Free, that it is filed on a .gov website, and that you never, ever pay a fee to send it in. Nobody told her that the sticker price on that website is almost never the price a family like hers actually pays, that after aid the real number can be a completely different number. Nobody told her that real federal aid is not one giant loan the way she fears — that it includes grants, like the Pell Grant, that never have to be paid back at all. And nobody told her the thing that would have protected the little bit of money this family has been scraping together: the "consultant" texting to guarantee her daughter's aid for a fee is not the way in. He is the first person trying to make money off a mother who is finally reaching for the door.

And that is the part that has to be said plainly, and I will say it again before I am done: a mother who never went to college and does not know the words is not less-than, and she is not the problem. She is the first one in her family to walk into this country, and of course it feels foreign — she is the one drawing the map the rest of them never had. Being first is not a deficit. It is one of the proudest, strongest things a parent ever does, and the fact that no one handed her a translation is a gap nobody prepared her for, not a failing in her. And it is the exact gap the fee-charging consultant, the "you won a scholarship" release fee, the phishing imposter, and the advance-fee loan mill are all standing in, waiting for a proud, frightened mother who will do anything to give her daughter the thing she never got.

And there are doors. Real ones. Doors nobody names for a woman who has spent her whole life working and was never once shown that the paper terrifying her tonight has a door built into it for her daughter too.

Start with the first thing she needs to hear: the "consultant" charging a fee to file the FAFSA is not her only option, and it is not the honest one. The honest first step is not money handed to a stranger — it is the free official site, studentaid.gov, the actual .gov, and a free sit-down with her daughter's high school counselor, because for most first-gen families the distance between "we can't afford this" and "she's going" is a handful of free doors nobody ever told them they were allowed to knock on.

So here are the doors almost no one names for her plainly. The first is that the FAFSA is free: the F stands for Free, the real site is a .gov, and filing it never costs a dime — so a paid "consultant" is never required and never worth handing money to. The second is that free expert help is real: her daughter's high school counselor, TRIO programs like Upward Bound and Talent Search and the Educational Opportunity Centers, the college's own financial aid office, and the free FAFSA-completion nights that libraries and churches hold every fall will all sit down with this family at no cost, and a first-gen family in exactly this spot is the whole reason those people show up to work. The third is that grants exist, not just loans: the federal Pell Grant and state grants like Illinois MAP do not have to be paid back, and aid is not the one crushing loan she is picturing. The fourth is that the sticker price is not the real price: net price after aid is what a family actually pays, the financial aid office can tell her the real number for her daughter, and if this family's income has dropped she can ask that office about a special-circumstances appeal. The fifth is that scholarships are real and free to apply for: through the high school, community foundations, churches, and local groups — and a real scholarship never charges a fee to apply or to release the money. The sixth is that help is a phone call away: 211, the studentaid.gov help line, and the college financial aid office answer questions for free. And underneath all of it there is her own two hands, and her daughter's name as the first to go, and her church, and a neighbor on that same block whose kid got all the way through on a Pell Grant and a free counselor's help instead of paying a "consultant who knows the system" — so she is not the first mother from the block to carry this, and the door was already open to her daughter.

But hear this warning before she pays one fee or sends one dollar, because the scams hunt a proud, loving, first-gen mother as hard as anyone — a woman this determined to give her child a door she never had will move the second a confident voice says "I can guarantee her aid today." Hear it plainly, and I will say it again at the end: real help does not work this way. Beware any "financial aid consultant" charging a fee to fill out the FAFSA and promising to "guarantee your aid" or "get a bigger award," because the FAFSA is free to file and no one on earth can guarantee an award. Beware any message saying your daughter has "been selected" for a scholarship or grant and just needs to pay a fee, send gift cards, or hand over a bank login to "release," "process," or "activate" it, because a real scholarship never charges a cent to claim it — you cannot win money you have to pay to receive. Beware the call, text, or email claiming to be the school or the federal aid office, demanding her Social Security number, her FSA ID password, or a bank login to "verify" or "finish" the application, because the real people never ask for her FSA ID password by text. Beware anyone charging money up front to "lower," "forgive," or "consolidate" student loans, because the federal system handles all of that for free at studentaid.gov. And beware the "guaranteed scholarship match" you have to subscribe and pay for, because free scholarship search is real and nothing is ever guaranteed. The help is real, and much of it is free, and the honest path runs right through the free counselor, TRIO, the financial aid office, and the .gov site. A counselor who sits down and walks her daughter through the FAFSA line by line, or a financial aid officer who gets on the phone and explains the award letter, is not a trick — it is exactly how the real thing is supposed to work. The caution is about the fee-charging "consultant," the "you've been selected" release fee, the phishing imposter, the advance-fee loan mill, and the guaranteed-match subscription — not about the real counselor, the honest financial aid office, or the church scholarship whose whole purpose is to help a kid from the block get through.
