There is a man at that church who never stopped writing letters to people the rest of us forgot.

Nobody put him in charge of it. He just started answering the ones who wrote to the church from inside, and kept answering, year after year, until half the men coming home to that neighborhood knew his name before they knew their own release date. He drives to the gate. He is standing there at six in the morning with a coat and a sandwich when a man walks out with everything he owns in a clear plastic bag.

And then the hard part starts. Because coming home is not the finish line. It is the first day of a maze.

The man walks out with a state ID that expired while he was inside. To replace it he needs a birth certificate he does not have, from a county office that wants the ID he cannot get. The benefits that kept his mother's household afloat got terminated the month he went in, and turning them back on means a form, a caseworker, a window that is only open when he has a parole appointment across town and no car. Every job application has a box, and he has learned to watch the face change the second he checks it honestly. Parole does not forgive a missed bus. And somewhere in all of it is an expungement he might qualify for that could take the box off the applications for good, if anyone had ever sat down and explained how.

So the deacon tries to hold all of it. He makes the calls. He drives the routes. He co-signs and vouches and shows up at the hearing in his good suit. And he is one man, with a day job and a family and a congregation full of other people who need him.

So something slips. A deadline for the ID passes and the whole chain stalls behind it. A benefit that would have fed a family stays off because nobody filled the form in time. An expungement the man qualified for never gets filed, and he checks that box for another ten years, and the door closes a little more each time it does.

Here is what a young man on the other side of the county gets when he makes one mistake.

He gets a lawyer who knows the diversion program before charges are even filed. He gets a case manager whose whole job is the ID and the benefits and the housing list. He gets a career counselor who knows which employers do not care about the box. He gets a team paid to walk him back into a normal life one step at a time, so the mistake stays a chapter instead of becoming the whole book. That team is not more decent than the deacon. It is just resourced.

The deacon can have that help too. For free. On the same phone he uses to answer the letters.

He can describe exactly what a man is facing coming home and get back a clear order of operations — what to replace first, which office needs what, which document unlocks the next one. He can hand it the denial letter that terminated the benefits and have it explained in language a tired man can act on tonight. He can ask what this man's record actually qualifies him for and get a plain map of the expungement path instead of a rumor. He can have it hold every parole date, every appointment, every window, so none of them passes while he is standing at the gate for the next one.

Not to replace what he does. The showing up is his. He is the one who was there at six in the morning, who wrote back when nobody else would, who believed a man was more than the worst day of his life before that man believed it himself. No machine has ever met somebody at a prison gate. It just takes the maze off his shoulders so he can do the part only he can do, which is believe.

That is the gap. It was never that our sons deserve less of a second chance than the ones across the county. It is that the ones across the county got handed a whole team at the first stumble, and ours got handed one deacon with a coat, a sandwich, and a faith stubborn enough to keep writing letters.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities and the people navigating resources beside them in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Matteson. Dolton. Not a webinar. A room. Real problems. A real man's clear plastic bag on the table.

The people walking returning citizens back into their own lives by hand are not behind. They were just never handed the tools the funded programs take for granted.

We are here to hand them over.

The plug is in.
