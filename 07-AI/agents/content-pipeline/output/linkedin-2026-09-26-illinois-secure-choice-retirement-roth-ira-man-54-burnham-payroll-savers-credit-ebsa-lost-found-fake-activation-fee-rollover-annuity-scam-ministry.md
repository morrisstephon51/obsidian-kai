**Centered on:** BROTHER ALPHONSO TEASLEY, 54, Burnham, IL — everybody calls him **Fonso.** Twenty-six years of work and never a gap in it longer than three weeks: six years boning out at a meat-packing house on the state line, five years on a moving crew, two years on a loading dock, and the last nine years running the big tunnel washer on second shift at a fourteen-employee **industrial linen service** that launders sheets and surgical towels for nursing homes and clinics all over the south suburbs. He makes **$21.40 an hour.** He has never been fired, never been written up, and has the kind of attendance record that made the owner give him a key. He raised his son **Deandre** — now nineteen and in his second semester at a community college, the first Teasley to see the inside of a college classroom — and he did it on a check with no cushion under it. And here is the arithmetic that keeps Fonso Teasley awake at four in the morning: after twenty-six years of getting up and going, his total retirement savings is **zero dollars.** Not a small amount. Not "behind." Zero. Not one dollar, not one account, not one statement, nothing with his name on it anywhere. And not one minute of that is because he was reckless. He has never had a gambling problem, never had a drinking problem, never bought a boat. The reason is embarrassingly simple and almost nobody says it out loud: **not a single one of those jobs ever offered a retirement plan.** No 401(k). No pension. Nothing to sign up for, nothing to opt out of, nothing to be disciplined about. Twenty-six years of American labor with no bucket under the spout. The thing that finally turned the light on was a man at the next machine — **Willie Earl Shackelford,** sixty-eight years old, still clocking in, still working Saturdays because the overtime is the only reason the lights stay on — who said to Fonso one night over the noise of the dryers, not bitter, just factual: *"I ain't retiring, young blood. I'm just gonna die here."* Fonso went out to his truck on lunch and sat there with the engine off, because he had just looked fourteen years into his own future and it had Willie Earl's hands on it. Then he remembered something. Twice in the last year and a half, an envelope had come to his house from the State of Illinois about some kind of retirement program with his employer's name in it. **Both times he threw it away.** Not because he didn't care — because *everything* that comes in the mail with a state seal on it wanting his information is a scam, because he has been burned twice already, because a man who works second shift in Burnham has been trained by hard experience that unsolicited mail about your money is a trap. He was right about the instinct. He was wrong about that particular envelope. And the two years he lost to being wisely, reasonably, defensively suspicious is the thing this whole post is about, because **the scammers have gotten so good that they have made the real help look fake.**

**Faith anchor:** SISTER LURLINE GAINWRIGHT, 71, of Gethsemane Missionary Baptist Church in Burnham — thirty-one years in the laundry and dietary department of a hospital in Harvey, union card in her purse the whole time, eight years of it as the shop steward nobody on management's side enjoyed meeting. **Resources — the real doors, free or low-cost, no fee to a stranger, ever:**

- **ILLINOIS SECURE CHOICE — ilsecurechoice.com — the state-facilitated retirement savings program run out of the Office of the Illinois State Treasurer. Free to join. No fee to enroll, ever.** Here is the plain-English version, and it is the most important paragraph in this post. If your Illinois employer has been in business a couple of years, has at least a handful of employees, and does **not** offer a retirement plan, that employer is generally **required by state law to facilitate** Secure Choice — meaning they have to make the payroll deduction available to you. The account that gets opened is a **Roth IRA in your own name.** Not the company's. Not the state's. **Yours.** Money comes out of your check automatically at a default percentage (the program sets the default rate and a small automatic yearly step-up — **ask them what both numbers are right now**), and you can **change that percentage or stop entirely, for free, whenever you want.** Your employer does not contribute, does not match, and does not pay a fee — and cannot get into your account. If you change jobs, **the account goes with you,** which for a man who has had four employers in twenty-six years is the entire ballgame. There is a **small annual fee taken as a percentage of your balance** to run the thing — that is normal, and you should still **make them tell you the exact number today.** The default investment is generally a target-date-style fund, with other choices available. And if your employer isn't in the program, or you're self-employed or driving or doing day work, **ask them directly whether you can enroll on your own** — the program has been expanding who can sign up without a boss involved, so ask instead of assuming the door is closed.
- **THE SAVER'S CREDIT — IRS Form 8880 — free money back at tax time for being a low-or-moderate-income person who saved anyway.** This is the one that makes grown men sit up. If you put money into a retirement account and your income is under the limits, the IRS can give you a **tax credit** worth up to **$1,000** (up to $2,000 for a married couple filing together) — a credit, which comes off what you owe, not just a deduction. The income limits and the percentages change every single year, and a lot of paid preparers never bring it up. **A free VITA volunteer can tell you in five minutes whether you qualify and will put the form in for free.** Also worth asking about: under the federal SECURE 2.0 law, a **"Saver's Match"** — where the federal government deposits a match directly into the saver's account — is **scheduled to replace the Saver's Credit starting in 2027.** That is a scheduled change, not a promise, so **confirm the current year's rules** with the IRS or a free tax counselor before you plan around it.
- **U.S. DEPARTMENT OF LABOR — EBSA BENEFITS ADVISORS — 1-866-444-3272 — free.** A real federal employee, on the phone, for free, for anything touching a 401(k), a 403(b), a pension, a rollover — or the situation where **money was taken off your check for retirement and never showed up in an account.** That last one is not a complaint you have to hire somebody for. It is theft, and there is a free number for it.
- **THE FREE "RETIREMENT SAVINGS LOST AND FOUND" AT THE U.S. DEPARTMENT OF LABOR (dol.gov) — for money from jobs you already left.** If you *ever* had anything anywhere — a few months in a plan at one job in 2006, a tiny balance somebody rolled into something — the federal government now runs a **free searchable database** to help people find retirement money left behind at old employers. Free. And while you're at it: the **Illinois Treasurer's unclaimed property search** is also free and also worth ten minutes, for old paychecks, deposits, and refunds with your name on them. **Anyone charging you a "finder's fee" to search free government databases is selling you a library card.**
- **FREE OR LOW-COST NONPROFIT CREDIT COUNSELING — nfcc.org** (a National Foundation for Credit Counseling member agency). Because the real reason a lot of men can't save thirty dollars is a car loan at 24%, a hospital balance, and a credit card that eats the difference. A nonprofit counselor will build a plan on your actual numbers with an actual end date, and will tell you honestly whether you should be paying down the 24% before you raise your savings rate. **A fee-charging "debt relief" outfit will charge you a percentage to do worse.**
- **CHECK YOUR SOCIAL SECURITY ESTIMATE FREE AT ssa.gov ("my Social Security").** Not because this post is about claiming it — it isn't — but because you cannot plan the small leg until you know the size of the big one. It's free, it takes fifteen minutes, and most people over fifty have never once looked.
- **FREE VERIFICATION BEFORE ANYBODY TOUCHES YOUR MONEY: FINRA BrokerCheck (brokercheck.finra.org), investor.gov, and the Illinois Securities Department in the Secretary of State's office.** If a man with a folder wants to move your retirement money anywhere, you can look him and his firm up, free, in about four minutes, before you sign one thing.
- **2-1-1 (call 211 or 211.org)** — a live local person for the gap underneath all of this: utility assistance, a food pantry, a free tax site, a benefits screening. And **988 (call or text)** if the math you just did about being fifty-four with nothing has put you somewhere dark. Said plainly, without drama, because shame about money is heaviest at 2 a.m.
- **To report the vultures: the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov or 1-877-382-4357; the Illinois Attorney General Consumer Fraud Hotline at 1-800-386-5438; the FBI's IC3 at ic3.gov for the online ones; the Illinois Department of Labor and U.S. DOL/EBSA (1-866-444-3272) if an employer took a deduction and didn't send it; and Illinois Secure Choice itself at ilsecurechoice.com if somebody is using the program's name to phish you.**

Let me tell you the truest thing in this whole post: **the help came to Alphonso Teasley's house. In an envelope. With the state seal on it. And he threw it in the garbage — twice — and he was not being foolish when he did it.**

He was being exactly as careful as twenty-six years of getting hustled had taught him to be.

That is the part I need the church to sit with, because we spend a great deal of energy telling people to be careful, and almost none reckoning with what happens to a person who has become careful enough to survive. Fonso has gotten a letter saying his student loan could be forgiven for a fee. He has gotten a call from "the IRS" threatening a warrant. He has gotten a text from "his bank" about a charge in Dallas. He has a cousin who lost eleven hundred dollars to a man who said he could clear a warrant. So when an envelope shows up unannounced, from a government, about *retirement* — a word he has only ever heard used to sell something — his hand went to the trash can before his brain finished reading, and **that reflex has protected him more often than it has cost him.**

But this time it cost him almost two years of contributions he will never get back, in the one decade of his life when he had any room at all. And that is the actual damage the scammers do that nobody adds up: they don't just take money from the people they catch. They **poison the mail** for everybody else. They make a free, boring, legitimate state program with a Roth IRA in it look exactly like the thing that stole from your cousin. The con doesn't have to reach you to hurt you. It only has to make you unable to tell the difference.

So this post is not mainly a warning. It is a **decoder.** Here is the difference, and you can teach it to a whole fellowship hall in one Sunday.

**Because the real door never asks you for money, and the fake one always does — eventually, always.** Illinois Secure Choice is free to join. There is no enrollment fee, no activation fee, no application fee, no "processing" fee, no fee to your boss, no fee to you, ever. Money reaches your account exactly one way: **as a deduction off your own paycheck that your employer sends in.** You do not fund it by wiring anything, sending a card number, buying gift cards, Zelle-ing a stranger, or "verifying" with a bank login on a link somebody texted you. Which means the tell is beautifully simple and works even if you know nothing about investing: **the moment anybody claiming to be this program asks you to pay, send, wire, or log in through their link, you have proof it is not them.** Not a suspicion. Proof. And the second rule that makes the first one safe: **you go to them, they do not come to you.** Type **ilsecurechoice.com** with your own thumbs, or call the number you found on that site yourself. Never the number in the text. Never the link in the email. Never the man who called. Sister Lurline's kitchen-table rule is better than most fraud training I have read: *"We don't call nobody back. We look it up and we call fresh."*

**Because the difference between Sister Lurline's retirement and Fonso's is not virtue, and pretending otherwise is a lie the church tells too easily.** She filled out one form in 1988 because a woman in personnel slid it across a desk. He never got a form. Twenty-six years, four employers, zero forms. That is it. That is the whole gap. When we talk about retirement in our buildings we almost always talk about it as **character** — discipline, delayed gratification, being a good steward — and there is truth in that, but it is *second* truth. The **first** truth is **access.** Roughly half the private-sector workforce in this country, at any given moment, works somewhere with nothing to sign up for, and the places with nothing are overwhelmingly the small ones, the hourly ones, the ones with fourteen employees and a tunnel washer. The people in our pews on the second row are not less disciplined than the people in the choir loft with 403(b)s. **They were hired somewhere that didn't have the form.** Illinois Secure Choice exists, at bottom, to hand out the form. That's all it is. It is not charity and it's not a handout — it is your own money, your own account, your own name — it is the state finally putting the form in front of the fourteen-employee linen service the way personnel put it in front of Lurline Gainwright in 1988.

**Because automatic enrollment is not a trick and it is not a gift — and the single most useful sentence in this post is "lower it, don't leave it."** Here's what surprises people: under Secure Choice, if your employer facilitates it and you don't respond, you generally get **enrolled automatically** at the program's default percentage, and you have every right to **opt out, free, any time.** Fonso's first reaction to hearing that was the normal one and it was hot: *"So they gon' take money out my check without asking me?"* And the honest answer is: it's your money, you can stop it with a phone call or a few clicks, and nobody keeps a dime of it — **and** the default exists for one reason, which is that the whole history of this shows that people who *mean* to start saving mostly don't, and people who get started automatically mostly stay. So understand the game you're actually in, and then play it on purpose instead of by reflex. **If the default rate is more than your check can bear — do not opt out. Lower it.** One percent. Two. Ten dollars a paycheck. Anything above zero keeps the account open, keeps the habit alive, keeps you eligible to look at a statement with your own name on it, and leaves the door unlocked for the January when you get a raise or pay off the car. **Opting out sets you back to Sonny Gainwright. Lowering it keeps you in the room.** Fonso started at two percent — about **thirty-one dollars a paycheck.** Willie Earl at the next machine, sixty-eight years old, started at one percent, and when Fonso asked him what the point was at his age, Willie Earl said: *"Point is I want to see it say something besides nothing."*

**Because a Roth IRA is not a box you can never open again, and the fear that it is makes people slam the door on themselves.** This is the misunderstanding that costs working folks the most. A man making $21.40 an hour hears "retirement account" and pictures a vault that locks until he's sixty-five while his transmission goes out — and so he says no, because he cannot afford to be the man who put money somewhere he can't reach it. Here is the general shape of the truth, and you should confirm it for your own situation: Secure Choice uses a **Roth IRA,** which is funded with money you already paid tax on, and **generally the contributions you yourself put in can come back out to you without tax or penalty.** The **earnings** are a different animal with different rules, and that is exactly the kind of thing you ask the program or a free tax counselor about rather than a guy at the barbershop. But the headline matters enormously for a man deciding whether to start at all: **this is not a one-way door.** Nobody can garnish it out of your check into somebody else's pocket, your boss cannot touch it, it is not a company account that dies when the company does, and it follows you from the linen service to the next job to the one after that. And I want to be honest about the other side too, because this ministry does not sell miracles: **it is invested money, which means the balance can go down some months.** A statement smaller than last quarter's is not fraud, it is not a mistake, and it is not somebody stealing from you — it is what an invested account does on the way to a long average. Hear that now, in a calm moment, so it cannot be used against you later, because **"look how the state plan is losing your money"** is the exact sentence the man with the folder will say.

**Because starting at fifty-four will not make you rich, and anybody who tells you it will is selling something.** Let's do Fonso's real arithmetic out loud, because false hope is its own kind of predator. Thirty-one dollars a paycheck, twenty-six paychecks a year, is about **eight hundred dollars a year.** Eleven years to sixty-five, with some growth and some raises and a couple of bad market years in there, is not a golden retirement and it is not a beach. It is a number in the low five figures if things go reasonably. Now here is why it is still one of the most important things this man will do this decade, and you have to say all of it: **Social Security is still the main leg** — for a man with twenty-six years of documented wages, it is most of the floor, and he should check his estimate free at ssa.gov tonight. What Secure Choice adds is a **second, smaller leg,** and what a second leg actually buys is not luxury, it is **the difference between a transmission and a catastrophe.** It is money for a funeral that doesn't require a plate to be passed in a fellowship hall. It is a month of dignity between a layoff and the next job. It is **not being Deandre's bill** the year that boy is trying to start his own life. And it is one more thing that does not show up in any spreadsheet: Fonso Teasley now has an account with his name on it, and a man who has an account behaves differently than a man who has none. Sister Lurline says it flat: *"Ain't nobody promised you rich. We promised you **not nothing.** You ever seen what 'not nothing' does to a man's back when he stands up?"*

**Because there is free money at the end of the year for the exact people who think none of this is for them — and almost nobody claims it.** The **Saver's Credit** is, in my opinion, the most under-claimed thing in the working-class tax code. You put money in a retirement account; if your income is under the limits, the IRS gives you a **credit** — up to a thousand dollars, up to two thousand for a couple — off what you owe. Fonso, at $21.40 an hour with one dependent stretch behind him, is squarely in the neighborhood this credit was built for. And the cruel comedy of it is that the people who qualify are exactly the people most likely to (a) not be saving, because no form, and (b) get their taxes done at a storefront that never mentions Form 8880. So get it done at a **free VITA site** by a volunteer who has to be certified, ask out loud about the **Saver's Credit,** and ask whether the **Saver's Match scheduled to start in 2027** will apply to you. Two sentences at a free tax table can turn a thirty-one-dollar-a-paycheck habit into a thirty-one-dollar habit **plus** a credit at tax time. That is the closest thing to a free lunch a second-shift man is ever offered in this country, and it is sitting there unclaimed because nobody told him.

**Because money from jobs you already left may already be yours, and the people who charge to find it are charging you for a library card.** Fonso was sure he had *nothing,* ever, anywhere. Then Sister Lurline made him think back, and it turned out that at the moving company in 2007, for about fourteen months, there had been **some kind of deduction** on his stub he never understood. Maybe it's nothing. Maybe it's four hundred dollars sitting in a plan with a 2007 address on it. The free way to chase that: call **EBSA at 1-866-444-3272** and talk to a benefits advisor for nothing, search the U.S. Department of Labor's free **Retirement Savings Lost and Found,** and run the free **Illinois Treasurer's unclaimed property** search while you're at it. The **$249** way is a man in a barbershop with a laminated business card. **Same databases. His are just the ones with a fee attached.**

So that is the door. Now the wolves — and pay attention to the shape of these, because retirement-savings predators are a *different species* from the ones in most of these posts. They are not hunting a nest egg. Fonso has no nest egg. **They are hunting the moment a poor man starts paying attention to his money,** which is the most vulnerable and most hopeful he will ever be, and they have gotten good at arriving in that exact week.

**The "your state retirement account is on hold" phish — and it is scary precisely because it uses the real name.** A text, an email, or a call: *"ILLINOIS SECURE CHOICE: Your state retirement account is suspended. Verify your Social Security number and bank credentials within 24 hours or forfeit your balance."* Fonso got one nine days after he enrolled, which tells you something about how these lists move. It had the program's actual name in it, decent grammar, and a link that went to a page that looked right. Run the decoder from earlier: the program is funded **off your check,** so there is nothing for you to "verify with a bank login"; nobody legitimate imposes a **24-hour forfeiture deadline** on a retirement account; and the word "forfeit" is a fear word, not a finance word. Here is the safe reflex that costs nothing and works every time: **do not click, do not call back, delete it, and then go type ilsecurechoice.com yourself and ask them if anything's wrong.** You will be told it's fine, you will have lost four minutes, and you will have kept your Social Security number. Fonso did it exactly that way — because Sister Lurline had already made him practice it at her table with the percolator going — and then he did the next right thing, which was to **report it,** so somebody knows this wave is out there.

**The "$249 — we'll find your lost retirement money" mill.** Barbershop, church parking lot, Facebook ad, a card that says *Benefits Recovery Specialist.* The pitch is real-sounding and even partly true: yes, people do leave money at old jobs, and yes, there are databases. What he does not tell you is that **the databases are free and the government will help you search them for free.** He charges **$249** up front (or a percentage of whatever turns up) to type your name into the same free federal and state searches you can use tonight, and if nothing turns up — which is most of the time — the $249 is gone and that was always the business model. **Tell:** anybody charging an up-front fee to search a government database for your own money. The free version is **EBSA at 1-866-444-3272,** the **DOL Retirement Savings Lost and Found,** and the **Illinois Treasurer's unclaimed property** search. All three cost zero.

**The man at the plant gate with a folder and a "guaranteed 9%."** Two weeks after the deductions started, a pleasant, well-dressed man was in the parking lot of the linen service at shift change with a leather folder, and he knew things — he knew the company had just started facilitating Secure Choice, and he knew what day people got paid. His pitch: *"That state plan is a starter kit. Look how it dipped in March. Let me roll you into something with a **guaranteed** nine percent — your principal's protected, you can't lose."* What was in the folder was a product with a **surrender charge** running the better part of a decade, which in plain English means: put money in, and taking it out early costs you a percentage that gets smaller each year — so a man who might need his own money in three years has just agreed to be fined for needing it. Four tells, any one of which ends the conversation: the word **"guaranteed"** attached to a return; **urgency** ("this rate's only through Friday"); **he came to you**, in a parking lot, with your employer's business in his mouth; and the pitch is built on **the balance going down some months,** which is not a malfunction, it's arithmetic. The free move is four minutes long: **look him and his firm up on FINRA BrokerCheck and investor.gov, and check with the Illinois Securities Department,** before you sign a thing. And a boundary worth saying out loud: a licensed advisor can be a genuinely good thing for somebody with money to manage. That is a different conversation, had sitting down, with somebody you looked up, whose fees you can state in a sentence, initiated by **you.** Not at a gate. Not at shift change. Not with the word *guaranteed* in it.

**The employer who takes the deduction and never sends it — the one almost nobody knows is a crime.** This one is Fonso's friend **Jermaine,** who works at a different small shop across the line. Jermaine's pay stub showed a retirement deduction for **seven months.** No statement ever came. No account was ever opened. When he asked, the owner said it was "in process with the state." It was not in process with anybody; it was in the owner's operating account, which is where cash-poor small businesses put money they mean to send and then don't. **Money withheld from your check for retirement is not the boss's money to float.** Here is the free, dead-simple audit every single person in this post should run twice a year: **hold your pay stub next to your account statement and make sure the deduction on one shows up as a contribution on the other.** If it doesn't — you call **EBSA at 1-866-444-3272** for free, you contact the program directly at **ilsecurechoice.com,** and you can raise it with the **Illinois Department of Labor.** You do not need a lawyer to make a phone call. And Sister Lurline, thirty-one years union, has one more instruction: *"Keep the stub. Keep every stub. The stub is the witness."*

**The phone-shaped versions: the "self-directed crypto retirement" app and the cash-it-out predators.** Two flavors, same hunger. The first is an app promising a **"self-directed retirement account"** with crypto or something exotic in it — slick, urgent, a referral bonus, and at the point of withdrawal it needs a **photo of your driver's license** and a "verification deposit," after which your withdrawal is "under review" indefinitely and a stranger has your ID and your bank information. The second shows up the month you finally have a balance: *"unlock your retirement early,"* *"get a cash advance against your account,"* a payday or title loan for the car you need to get to second shift. Understand who they target — **not the rich.** They target the person whose account just crossed a thousand dollars, because that person is brand-new to having anything, has nobody to ask, and is proud enough not to want to ask. **A title loan on a running car converts a money problem into a no-transportation problem,** and a man who can't get to Burnham at eleven at night doesn't have a savings problem anymore, he has a job problem.

**And the quiet thief that isn't a scam at all — the voice that says fifty-four is too late and thirty dollars is too little.** Sister Lurline says this is the one that got Sonny, and it does not sound like a wolf. It sounds like humility. It sounds like *being realistic.* *"Thirty dollars ain't gon' do nothing. I'm fifty-four. That train left.
