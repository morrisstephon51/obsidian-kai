There is a sister at that church who worked thirty years in a hospital billing office. She retired, but she never really left it. Because now the bills come to her. Somebody's daughter shows up after service with a grocery bag full of envelopes, half of them unopened, and says the same thing everybody says: they're calling me, and I don't even know what I owe.

Nobody gave her the job. She is just the one who sat on the other side of that counter, who cut those bills and knows exactly how they are built, who watched families pay in full what they never actually owed because the letter looked official and the number was big and nobody told them there was another door. So she opens the envelopes for the ones too scared to. She sat with a man last month who could not bring himself to slit one open for three weeks, because as long as it was sealed he did not have to know.

And then the hard part starts. Because getting sick in this country and paying for getting sick are two different fights, and the second one is a maze built out of bills that are not final, discounts nobody mentions, and forms the hospital is required to have and never hands you.

Because a hospital bill is not the last word. You can demand an itemized statement, and when you do, the duplicate charges and the coding errors and the thing you never received come up out of the fog — she found them for a living. Because nonprofit hospitals are required by law to have a written financial-assistance policy, charity care, that can cut a bill in half or wipe it out entirely, and applying is free — yet families who would have qualified pay it off over years, or let it wreck their credit, because no one ever said the words "financial assistance" to them. Because here in Illinois the law is on the patient's side more than they know: an uninsured patient under the income limits is owed real discounts, and a hospital cannot drag you into collections or sue you before it checks whether you qualified for help first. Because there is a law that voids a whole class of surprise bills — the out-of-network anesthesiologist nobody chose, the ER that was not in the network — and guarantees someone with no insurance a good-faith estimate before the procedure, not a shock after. Because Medicaid can reach backward, and a bill from a hospital stay three months before a woman ever applied can sometimes still be covered, and she is paying on it in cash right now. Because there is free help to get covered at all — a real trained navigator, at no cost — and the difference between uninsured and insured is often one conversation she does not know she can have. Because the insulin an old man is rationing has a patient-assistance program with his name on it if someone knows to look. And because a bill is not a debt until it is verified, and a collector counting on you not knowing that is counting on your fear.

So the sister tries to hold all of it. In her head. From billing codes she learned years ago and a charity-care policy that reads differently at every hospital and changes when she is not looking. She tells a family the wrong income cutoff. She sends someone to a financial-assistance office that reorganized. She knows the door is there — she used to stand behind it — but she is one retired clerk working from memory of a system that rewrites its own rules between the bill she fought last spring and the one on the table tonight.

So the bill gets paid that never should have been. The charity-care application never gets filed, and a debt the hospital would have forgiven follows a family for years. The credit score drops over a bill that was wrong. The surprise charge gets paid in full because it looked legal. The Medicaid that could have reached back never gets applied for. And a man rations his insulin three miles from a program that would have handed it to him, because the door was there and no one walked him to it.

Here is what a family with resources gets when the bills come.

They get a private patient advocate, or a benefits counselor, whose entire job is to read the itemized bill line by line, find the charity-care policy, know the state law cold, dispute the errors, and negotiate the rest down before a dollar changes hands. They get someone who knows the bill is a first offer, not a verdict. That family is not more deserving than the daughter with the grocery bag of envelopes. They were just handed a professional, and ours was handed a retired billing clerk with a good heart and a memory going out of date.

She can have that help too. For free. On the same phone the collector is calling.

She can photograph a confusing bill and get back a plain read on what it actually is and what to question. She can find out whether that hospital's charity-care policy would cover this family and how to apply before they pay a cent. She can learn the Illinois discount an uninsured patient is owed and that collections cannot start until eligibility is checked. She can check whether a surprise bill is one the law already voids. She can find out if Medicaid could reach back over a stay from months ago, and where the free navigator is. She can surface the prescription-assistance program for the insulin. She can have the whole maze — the itemized bill, the charity-care form, the state discount, the surprise-billing protection, the Medicaid application, the prescription help — broken into an order of operations, and then walk each family straight to the hospital's financial-assistance office, the certified navigator, and Illinois Medicaid that carry it the rest of the way.

Not to replace what she does. The kitchen table is hers. The envelope she opens for the man who could not is hers. The reason a scared daughter brings the whole grocery bag to her and to no one else is hers, and no machine will ever have sat behind that counter or earned that trust. It only takes the maze off her shoulders so she can do the part only she can do, which is to put her hand on somebody's arm and say you do not owe what that letter says, there is a door, and we are going to walk through it together before you pay one dollar you did not have to.

That is the gap. It was never that the families in our pews deserve a smaller share of the mercy the law already wrote down than the family that can put a patient advocate on retainer. It is that that family got a professional, and ours got one faithful sister with a memory that goes out of date.

A word of care, plainly: legitimate help with medical bills is free. The hospital's own financial-assistance office is free. A certified Get Covered Illinois navigator is free. A nonprofit like Dollar For, which helps families apply for hospital charity care, is free. So anyone charging you upfront to "erase," "settle," or "negotiate" a medical debt — when the charity care that would forgive it costs nothing to apply for — is selling you something you can get for free, and any collector treating an unverified bill as a proven debt is counting on you not knowing your rights. This is general navigation, not medical, financial, legal, or insurance advice — helping someone understand a bill is not the same as being their patient advocate or benefits counselor — so always route the actual application to the hospital's financial-assistance / patient-advocate office, a certified navigator through Get Covered Illinois at 1-866-311-1119, Illinois Medicaid through ABE, or a nonprofit like Dollar For. For a billing dispute you cannot resolve, the Illinois Attorney General Health Care Helpline is 1-877-305-5145, and 2-1-1 can point to local help.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities and the people navigating resources beside them in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Matteson. Dolton. Not a webinar. A room. Real problems. A real bill cut in half, a real charity-care application filed, a real family that kept its credit and its dignity because somebody knew the door was there.

The people carrying our families' medical bills by hand are not behind. They were just never handed the tools the resourced take for granted.

We are here to hand them over.

The plug is in.
