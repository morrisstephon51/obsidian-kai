There is a young mother in that congregation who sits toward the back so she can slip out fast when the baby fusses, a diaper bag on one shoulder and a toddler leaning on the other knee. She is twenty-seven. She works — real hours, a real check — and still the money runs out before the month does, so she does the quiet math every good mother learns without being taught: which bill waits, which meal shrinks, whose plate stays full. The kids eat. She makes sure of that first, every time, even the nights her own plate is mostly nothing and she calls it "not being hungry." And here is the part that would change her whole month if anyone had ever said it to her plainly: she was raised that a good mother provides for her own and that her family does not take handouts, so she has never once signed up for WIC — when the truth is that WIC is not a handout at all. It is nutrition help written into public health for exactly her — a pregnant, postpartum, or nursing mom with children under five — and claiming it means her babies eat and grow, which is the whole job, not a failure of it.

You know her. She would never call herself someone who needs help — she is proud of the way she stretches a check, proud that her kids are fed and dressed and loved, and she would wave you off before you finished the sentence. That is exactly why she has gone without. Nobody ever told her that WIC — the Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children — is not welfare but nutrition help the law holds open for a mother during pregnancy and after, and for every child she has under the age of five, covering healthy food and infant formula and fruits and vegetables and milk and eggs, plus free breastfeeding support, free nutrition counseling, and referrals to a doctor and a dentist. Nobody told her that a working paycheck usually does not put her over the line the way she assumes — WIC reaches far more working families than most people believe, and if she is already on Medicaid or SNAP she very likely qualifies automatically. Nobody told her that applying is free, through Illinois WIC and her local WIC clinic, her county or city health department, or an FQHC community health center — no fee, no cost, no catch. And nobody told her the one thing that would protect her the moment she finally goes looking: that WIC is always free, that no real WIC office ever demands a gift card, a wire, or an "enrollment" or "activation" fee, and never texts or calls out of the blue asking her to "verify" her benefits with her Social Security number, her card number and PIN, or her bank login — so the first voice that asks her to pay to sign up, or to read off her card and PIN to "keep" her benefits, is not the help, it is a thief who found a proud young mother too busy and too tired to ask twice.

And that has to be said plainly, and I will say it again before I am done: a working mother feeding her babies through WIC is not begging and is not a burden. Working full hours and still watching the check run out before the month is over is not proof she failed — it is the arithmetic of raising little children on what most jobs pay, and it is true in more homes than anyone says out loud. WIC is not welfare. It is nutrition help built into public health for exactly the pregnant, postpartum, and nursing mother it names, and for the young children it was made to feed. Signing up for it is not asking for a favor; it is claiming help her own pregnancy and her own children already qualify her for, the same as any other thing a family is entitled to and simply has to apply for. Skipping her own plate and telling herself she's managing was never proof she's strong enough not to need it — it is what a proud mother does when no one ever handed her the map. That is not a deficit. That is a woman doing the hardest job there is, well, while sitting on help that was hers the whole time. And it is the exact moment the fake "enrollment fee" ad, the card-skimming thief, the "your benefits will be cut off unless you verify" caller, and the lookalike WIC website are all waiting for — a mother worried enough about feeding her kids to trust the first voice that says "just pay this today and I'll get you approved."

And there are doors. Real ones. Doors nobody names for a proud young mother who was raised to provide for her own in silence.

Start with the first thing she needs to hear: signing up for WIC is free, and it costs nothing to find out if she and her children qualify, so she never has to pay a stranger a dime to claim help her babies already qualify for. The honest first move is not an ad charging an "enrollment fee" and not a text promising to "speed up" her approval for a cut — it is her local WIC clinic, which does it for free, because for a young mother the distance between the nights she skips her own plate and the help her children already qualify for is often a few free doors nobody ever told her she was allowed to walk through.

So here are the doors almost no one names for her plainly. The first is Illinois WIC — start at the official state site (dph.illinois.gov, WIC) — and her local WIC clinic, her county or city health department, or an FQHC community health center, where she applies for free and a real person walks her through what she and her children qualify for and what to bring. The second is the referral already sitting in her own life: her child's doctor, her OB, the hospital where she delivered, or a Head Start program can point her straight to WIC, because they see families qualify for it every day. The third is 2-1-1 — one free call that finds the nearest WIC agency and, while she's at it, other food help she may also qualify for, from food pantries to SNAP to free school meals, whatever her corner of the south suburbs, from Harvey to Dolton to Markham to Chicago Heights to Riverdale to Calumet City to South Holland to Blue Island to Robbins to Ford Heights. Add to that what she already carries — her children, her church, a sister two pews over who signed up for WIC while she was pregnant and never once felt smaller for it — and she is not the first young mother from this church to walk this, and the door was never as far as it looked.

But hear this warning before she pays a single stranger or reads her information to anyone, because the scams hunt a proud, worried mother harder than almost anyone — a woman terrified her kids will go without will move the second a confident voice says "there's a problem with your WIC benefits and they'll be cut off today unless you verify your card and pay a small reactivation fee right now." Hear it plainly, and I will say it again at the end: WIC is always free, no WIC office ever charges a fee to enroll or "speed up" approval, no real office demands gift cards or a wire, and no one real ever asks her to "verify" her benefits by giving her card number, her PIN, her SSN, or her bank login over a text, a link, or a call. Beware the person, ad, or site charging an "enrollment fee" or "activation fee" to sign her up, or promising to get her "approved faster" for money — WIC is free and no fee makes it come faster. Beware the call or text saying her benefits are "suspended" or "about to be cut off" unless she verifies or pays right now — that urgency is the scam, not a real process, and the real answer is only at her clinic or the official Illinois WIC site. Beware the lookalike WIC website or "benefits" app that charges a fee or harvests her login, card number, and SSN.
