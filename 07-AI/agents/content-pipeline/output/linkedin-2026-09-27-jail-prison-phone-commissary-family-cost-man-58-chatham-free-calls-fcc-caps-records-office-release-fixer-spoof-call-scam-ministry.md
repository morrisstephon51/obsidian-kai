# Post #396 — WHOLLY NEW DOMAIN: *THE SECOND LINE — THE $14,218 A BOILER-ROOM ENGINEER SPENT IN $3.95 INCREMENTS TO HEAR HIS SON'S VOICE, AND THE YEAR THE PRICE WENT DOWN AND NOBODY TOLD HIM* — HOW A 58-YEAR-OLD LICENSED STATIONARY ENGINEER WHO KEEPS THE HEAT ON IN OTHER PEOPLE'S CHILDREN'S SCHOOLS PAID A FEE TO HAND HIS OWN MONEY TO HIS OWN CHILD FOR FOUR YEARS, NEVER SAW A SINGLE STATEMENT, NEVER KNEW THE TOTAL, AND NEVER LEARNED THAT THE RULES HAD CHANGED UNDERNEATH HIM — BECAUSE **A PRICE CUT IS NEVER ANNOUNCED TO THE PERSON WHO WAS OVERPAYING**, AND BECAUSE THE ONLY PEOPLE WHO KNEW WERE THE ONES COLLECTING

**Centered on:** BROTHER PERNELL STOVALL, 58, Chatham, on Chicago's South Side — twenty-four years a **licensed stationary engineer**, which is the man in the basement of a public school at 4:40 in the morning with a clipboard and a flashlight, reading gauges and bleeding radiators so that when six hundred children walk through the doors at 8:15 the building is 71 degrees and nobody thinks about him at all. He has a license from the city and a ring of keys that weighs more than a hammer. He has never missed a heating season. He can hear a bad circulator pump through a concrete wall. His wife passed in 2019. His son **QUINCY STOVALL, 27**, is four years into a sentence downstate, and for four years Pernell Stovall has paid a fee to put his own money into his own son's account, paid by the minute to hear him say "I'm all right, Pop," paid by the message on a tablet his son rents, and has never once in four years received a bill, a statement, an annual summary, or a single piece of paper with a total on it. He is not behind on anything. He has no debt in collections. He has a credit score in the 700s. And when he finally sat down in a church fellowship hall and searched his own bank app for one vendor's name, the number that came back was **$14,218** — which is, to the dollar, the roof his house needs.

**Faith anchor:** SISTER DELOIS DUPREE, 70, of Greater Mount Moriah Missionary Baptist Church in Chatham — for **thirty-one years the church's recording secretary and clerk**, which is the reason she is in this post and not somebody else. Because the clerk keeps **the roll.** She keeps the membership book, the sick-and-shut-in list, the transfers in and the transfers out, and the attendance the pastor reads from at the annual meeting. Which means that for thirty-one years, Sister Dupree has been the only person in that building who could tell you exactly who is missing — and, if she is honest, why. She can tell you which pew went empty in 2011 and which family stopped coming in the spring of 2019 and did not transfer anywhere. There wasn't a line for it. So I made him a man who just didn't come to church."*

Verify everything for the exact facility where your person actually is, using that facility's own published information. And nothing here will shorten anybody's sentence.)*

**Resources — the real doors, free or low-cost, no fee to a stranger, ever:**

- **THE FREE QUESTION THAT IS THE WHOLE POST — ASK THE FACILITY, IN 2026, "WHAT DOES A PHONE CALL COST NOW, AND WHAT DOES A DEPOSIT COST NOW?"** Not what it cost when he went in. What it costs **now.** Because the rules moved. **Illinois is among the states that have moved to provide phone calls at no charge to families in state custody, and Cook County Jail eliminated charges for calls years ago** — and separately, the **FCC now regulates and caps what these companies may charge**, and those caps have been coming **down.** Whether that applies to *your* person depends entirely on *which system he is in* — state, county, or federal — so **verify it for that facility.** But understand the thing that makes this post necessary: **nobody mails you a notice when a price goes down.** There is no company on earth whose job it is to call and tell you that you may stop paying them. Ask the question out loud, of the facility itself, once a year.
- **ADD IT UP BACKWARD, TONIGHT, FOR FREE, IN YOUR OWN BANK APP.** You will never get a statement for this, because you are not a borrower and there is no account in your name. So do what Pernell Stovall did: open your banking app, search your transaction history for **the vendor's name** (it is on every charge — the calling or deposit company, not the prison), set the date range to as far back as it goes, and read the total. **Your own bank is the only institution that has been keeping this ledger.** It took him forty minutes. It is free. It is the single most clarifying thing in this post, and it is also the hardest, so do it with somebody sitting next to you.
- **PAY FOR THE MONEY THE CHEAPEST PUBLISHED WAY — THE FEE IS ON THE TRANSFER, NOT ON THE MONEY.** Most systems publish **more than one way** to put money on an account, and they do not cost the same: the phone-app "instant" option is usually the most expensive per dollar; a **money order through the mail** or an in-person option, where the facility allows it, is often far cheaper or free. And because the fee is charged **per transfer**, not per dollar, **two deposits of $50 can cost half what four deposits of $25 cost.** That is the entire trick. Ask the facility for the complete list of deposit methods **and the fee for each one, in writing or from their own published page** — then pick the cheapest one that works for your household, and deposit less often.
- **BUILD THE SECOND LINE: A STAMP CANNOT BE SHUT OFF.** A letter costs well under a dollar, and it is the only channel in this entire domain that **no vendor bills you for by the minute, no lockdown cancels, no billing problem suspends, and no rate change touches.** A legal pad and a book of stamps is the cheapest and most durable line to a person inside — and people keep letters; nobody keeps a phone call. **But check that facility's mail rules first, because many now route personal mail through a scanning vendor**, which means the paper you send may never physically arrive. So here is the rule, and it is the same rule as the storage post: **send a photocopy and keep the original.** If you cannot buy it again, it does not go in the mail.
- **A RELEASE-DATE, SENTENCE-COMPUTATION, OR "WHERE IS HE" QUESTION IS ANSWERED BY THE RECORDS OFFICE, FOR FREE.** Every facility has a records or sentence-computation office, and the department has constituent- or family-services staff whose actual job is to answer exactly these questions. **You do not pay anybody to find out a date.** Illinois also publishes an inmate locator you can search yourself. If a person is charging you money to "look up," "confirm," "recalculate," or "expedite" a release date, that is not a service — that is the scam in this post with a necktie on.
- **FREE LEGAL HELP EXISTS — FOR HIM, AND SEPARATELY FOR YOU.** For a person inside, on conditions of confinement, medical care, or prisoners' rights: **Uptown People's Law Center** in Chicago, and the **Illinois Prison Project**; and **Equip for Equality (1-800-537-2632)** for disability issues in custody. For the household on the outside dealing with the money, a collector, a lease, or a benefits problem caused by all of this: **Illinois Legal Aid Online (illinoislegalaid.org)**, plain English, free, no appointment; **CARPLS at 312-738-9200**, the Cook County legal-aid hotline; **Legal Aid Chicago**; and **Land of Lincoln Legal Aid** and **Prairie State Legal Services** downstate. These are genuinely free. They are also genuinely oversubscribed — write anyway, and write early.
- **TO REPORT A CALLING OR DEPOSIT CHARGE THAT LOOKS WRONG: the FCC at fcc.gov/complaints**, which is the agency that now caps these rates, and **the Illinois Attorney General Consumer Fraud Hotline at 1-800-386-5438.** If somebody sold you a "release," a "transfer," a "clemency filing," or an "expedite" — that is the Attorney General, plus the **FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov or 1-877-382-4357**, and **the FBI's IC3 at ic3.gov** if it started online or by app. If a leftover third-party balance turns into a collection letter with your name on it, that is the **CFPB at consumerfinance.gov/complaint** — and a letter about a debt is never junk mail.
- **KNOW THE VISIT RULES BEFORE YOU SPEND THE GAS.** Visiting rules, approved-visitor lists, ID requirements, dress codes, scheduling windows, and whether **free or low-cost video visits** are available vary by facility and change. A four-hour round trip that ends at a desk because a form wasn't on file is a real, uncompensated cost to a working family. One phone call to the facility, or five minutes on its own page, prevents it. And **get on the approved visitor list early** — that paperwork takes longer than anyone expects.
- **START THE COMING-HOME PAPERWORK BEFORE HE COMES HOME.** State ID, birth certificate, Social Security card, benefits, and the record itself all have their own doors, and I have written about those doors already — **expungement and sealing, reentry, and the state-ID and vital-records path are each their own separate post**, and I am not going to re-teach them here. Just write the date on the refrigerator and start ninety days early instead of the week after. The paperwork is the part that strands people, not the gate.
- **FOR THE HOUSEHOLD'S OWN GAP: 2-1-1 (call 211 or 211.org).** Understand this clearly: **when $300 a month leaves a house for four years, that is a household financial crisis** — it just doesn't have a name, so nobody treats it like one. Township general assistance, utility help, food pantries, St. Vincent de Paul, the Salvation Army, and church benevolence are for exactly this, and they do not require you to explain where the money went. And **988 (call or text)** is there for the parent, the grandmother, the wife, and the child, because this domain runs on a grief that nobody brings a casserole for.

School counselors, Big Brothers Big Sisters, and church youth workers can help — and **988** is for a teenager too. The shame in this post is generational, and it is transmitted by nobody saying the word.

## I. The man in the basement

Pernell Stovall gets to the school at 4:40 in the morning.

He does this because a building that will hold six hundred children at 8:15 has to be started like an engine, and because the boiler in that particular building is older than he is and has opinions. He walks the basement with a flashlight. He reads the gauges and writes the numbers on a clipboard that hangs on a nail, the way he was taught in 2002 by a man named Sylvester who has been dead eleven years. He bleeds the radiators on the north side because the north side never heats right. He has a city license in his wallet that took him two tries and a year of night classes to get.

Twenty-four heating seasons. Never missed one. Not the winter his wife was sick, and not the winter after she died.

Here is what I want you to understand about this man before we talk about a single dollar: **Pernell Stovall's entire working life is being the person who makes sure other people's children are warm.** That is not a metaphor I'm reaching for. That is his job description, and it is the reason the rest of this post has the shape it has.

Four years ago, his son Quincy went away.

I am not going to litigate Quincy Stovall's case in this post, and I want to be very direct about why. **This post does not require anybody to be innocent.** Some of the people reading this have a son who did exactly what they said he did. Some have a son who didn't. Some don't know and will never know for certain. It does not matter one bit for the mechanics that follow, and here is the reason, which is the thesis of the whole thing:

**Whatever Quincy Stovall did or did not do, his father didn't do it.**

And his father is the one being charged.

## II. The bill that isn't a bill

Every post I have written in this series has had a piece of paper at the center of it.

A storage agreement signed in a parking lot. A rent-to-own contract with a total on page two that nobody read. A lease. A denial letter. A notice of default. A statement with a number on it. Every single time, my advice eventually comes down to some version of the same instruction: **find your paper, read these four lines, and ask for the total in writing.**

That instruction does not work here. And the reason it does not work is the most important structural fact in this domain.

**Pernell Stovall has no contract. There isn't one. He is not a party to anything.**

Think about it. He didn't sign an agreement. He didn't apply for credit. He isn't a borrower, a tenant, a policyholder, a claimant, a beneficiary, or a customer with an account. He has no clause to find, no disclosure he was owed, no statement he can demand, no appeal rights, no grace period, no regulator he can write to as an aggrieved account-holder — because **there is no relationship.** The person with the relationship to that facility is his son, who has no money.

So what, exactly, is Pernell Stovall paying for?

He is paying for **proximity to a person he loves.** That's the product. That's the whole product. The thing being sold is contact with your own child, and the price is set by a company that did not have to persuade you of anything, because **you cannot shop.** There is exactly one vendor. There is no second vendor. Your options are that vendor's price, or silence.

That is not an abuse of a market. **That is the definition of the market.** And it's why the usual advice — compare, negotiate, switch, walk away — has nothing to grab onto.

Every protagonist I have ever written about had a relationship with a name: a lease, a loan, a claim, a policy, a benefit. Sister Rosalind Winbush, in the storage post, at least had a contract in a drawer that she could finally go read. **Pernell Stovall has a bank statement full of charges and no counterparty.** He is billed for love, and love does not come with a disclosure form.

## III. Fourteen thousand two hundred and eighteen dollars

Here is how it actually happened, month by month, and I want to walk it slowly because the slowness is the mechanism.

**$25 on the account, twice a month.** That's the money itself, and that money is fine — that's a father feeding his son, and no part of this post is going to tell a man not to do that. On top of the $25, **a fee to move it.** Three dollars and ninety-five cents, give or take, depending on how he sent it. He sent it the fast way, from the app on his phone, at 11 o'clock at night, because that is when a man who gets up at 4:40 does his business.

**The calls.** Priced by the minute, and the meter is honest — it starts when the call connects and stops when it ends, and Pernell Stovall always knew, in the abstract, that talking to his son cost money. What he did not have was a total, because a phone call does not send you a receipt at the end of the month with a subtotal on it. It just quietly draws down a balance he keeps refilling.

**The tablet.** His son rents one. Messages on it cost per message. A photograph attached to a message costs more than a message. Pernell learned, in year two, that if he wrote a long letter as four short messages it cost him four times as much as writing it as one, which is a sentence I want you to sit with for a second, because it means **the price structure was quietly teaching a fifty-eight-year-old man to say less to his son.**

**The video visits.** By the minute, and the minutes are short.

**The commissary.** This is the captive store at the other end. When you put $50 on an account and your son buys soap, noodles, coffee, a thermal shirt, and stamps, the prices in that store are whatever the prices in that store are, and neither of you is going anywhere else. Whatever the markup is on any given item, **the structural fact is that there is one store and the customer cannot leave.**

Now add four years.

Pernell Stovall could not have told you what any of it came to. Not because he is careless with money — this is a man who knows what a circulator pump costs and can tell you what he paid for his furnace in 2014 — but because **the information does not exist in any place he was ever shown.** There was no bill. There was no statement. There was no APR, no due date, no minimum payment, no annual summary, nothing to open, nothing to file, nothing to dread in the mailbox. It arrived in his life in three-dollar-and-ninety-five-cent increments, ninety-six of them, plus minutes, plus messages, plus a store.

**A thing with no bill has no total.** And a thing with no total is invisible even to the person paying it.

Sister Dupree made him find it. She sat him down at a folding table in the fellowship hall and she said: *"Baby, your bank has been keeping the book. Go get the book."*

Forty minutes in his banking app. One vendor name in the search field. The date range dragged all the way back.

**$14,218.**

He sat there with the phone in his hand and he said the first thing that came into his head, which was not about his son and was not about the company. It was: *"That's the roof."*

And this is where I want to say the thing that this post exists to say, and to say it against the last one I wrote. In the storage post, a woman lost forty-one years of her mother's household, and the whole point of that post was that **the harm had no dollar unit** — photographs, a quilt with a name sewn in it, things that cannot be bought again, so the money was almost beside the point.

This post is the exact inversion, and it is worse in a different direction. **Here the harm is nothing but dollar units.** Every penny of it is countable, itemizable, and sitting in a database somewhere. And it still never got counted — for four years, by a competent man, in his own house — **because nobody ever sent him the number, and a number nobody sends you is a number nobody adds up.**

## IV. The year the price went down and nobody told him

Now here is the part that made me write this post instead of a different one.

Somewhere in the middle of those four years, **the rules moved.**

Illinois is among the states that have acted to stop charging families for phone calls from state custody. Cook County Jail stopped charging for calls years ago. And separately from any of that, at the federal level, **the FCC now has authority over these rates and has been capping them — downward.** The details of who is covered, by which rule, in which facility, on what date, are genuinely complicated, and that complication is exactly why I am going to keep telling you to verify it for your own person's facility rather than take a number from me.

But the structural fact does not need any complication at all:

**The price of some of this went down. And not one human being called Pernell Stovall to tell him.**

Think about why that is, because it isn't a conspiracy and it isn't an accident. **There is no entity in the world with an incentive to inform you that you can stop paying it.** When a price goes *up*, you find out immediately — the charge declines, the call drops, the balance runs out, somebody sends a notice. When a price goes *down*, the only parties who know are the regulator, who is not writing to you, and the company, which is not writing to you either. **A price cut is never announced to the person who was overpaying.**

Every other post in this series has been about a notice that didn't arrive, or a fee that went up, or a deadline that ran out. The mail was stolen. The notice went to a dead woman's house. The late fee doubled. **This is the first post I have written about a right that showed up quietly and nobody claimed it** — because the only people positioned to tell you were the ones collecting.

In the retirement-savings post, a man threw away the state's envelope twice, and the lesson was that scammers had poisoned the mail so badly that he couldn't tell a real door from a fake one. **This is a step past that.** Pernell Stovall didn't throw anything away. **No envelope ever came.** You cannot ignore a notice you were never sent, and you cannot decline a right you never heard of.

So the whole domain turns on one free question, asked out loud, of the right office, once a year:

**"What does this cost now?"**

Not what it cost when he went in. What it costs **now.** And ask it of the facility itself — its own published information, its own family-services line — not of the app that is billing you.

## V. The six things hunting this family — and the first two are legal

I order these deliberately. The first two are not crimes. They are the business operating exactly as designed, and they took far more money out of this house than any con did. That ordering is the point.

**1. THE DEPOSIT FEE (entirely legal).** You pay a fee to give your own money to your own child. That's it. That's the product. Three or four or six dollars to move twenty-five. And because **the fee is charged per transfer rather than per dollar**, the household that is most broke — the one that can only spare $25 at a time, twice a month — pays the highest effective rate on its own money. The father who can front $100 once a month pays a quarter as much in fees as the father who sends $25 four times. **The fee structure charges the poorest household the most, automatically, without anybody deciding to do that.** *Defense:* get the complete list of deposit methods and the fee for each, and use the cheapest published one, less often. Mailing a money order, where the facility allows it, is frequently the cheapest door in this entire post.

**2. THE CAPTIVE STORE AND THE RENTED TABLET (entirely legal).** Commissary prices, tablet rental, per-message charges, per-minute video. There is one vendor, the customer cannot leave, and the person paying is not the person consuming. *Defense:* there is no clever defense to a monopoly, and I'm not going to pretend there is. What there is, is **knowing the published price list** so the family can decide together what's worth it, and understanding that the pricing quietly pressures you to communicate *less* — so you route around it with the mail.

**3. THE FIXER WHO SELLS A FAVOR.** This is the one that takes the most money in the fewest transactions. Somebody — sometimes a stranger on the phone, often a friend of a cousin, occasionally a man in a suit with a real-looking website — offers, for $500 or $1,200 or $2,500, to *get him transferred closer to home*, *file his clemency*, *expedite his parole*, *recalculate his time*, *get him out of segregation*, or *talk to somebody I know at the facility.* **Every single one of these is a favor being sold.** *The tell, and it is the signature rule of this post:* **a favor has a price, and a rule has a phone number.** Transfers, sentence computation, clemency, and parole all run on **published rules** with **offices that answer for free** — the records office, the department's family or constituent services, the Prisoner Review Board's published process, and a licensed attorney you looked up yourself and called fresh. If what's being offered is access to a person's discretion rather than a step in a written process, **you are being robbed**, and the arithmetic insult is that the fee is usually larger than a year of the deposit fees you're actually stuck with.

**4. THE CALL THAT DOESN'T HAVE TO FAKE A VOICE.** A phone rings at 9:40 at night. Your son has been hurt. Your son is in segregation. Your son needs a phone card, a transfer, $200 right now, and you need to send it by cash app or gift card to this number. Now — I have written before about the grandparent scam and the cloned voice and the family safe word, and that post owns that ground. **This one is different, and worse, and cheaper to run.** The caller here **does not need to clone anybody's voice**, because he is telling a parent something that is *always plausible* about a place the parent **cannot see, cannot call into, and cannot verify at 9:40 at night.** He isn't faking a person. He's exploiting an information blackout. *Defense:* hang up, and call the facility on the number you look up yourself. **No facility on earth resolves a medical emergency or a transfer by gift card.** And put that sentence somewhere you'll see it at 9:40 at night, because at 9:40 at night you will not be the sharpest version of yourself.

**5. THE FAKE VENDOR, AND THE BILL THAT ARRIVES AFTER.** Websites that take "care package" orders that never arrive; fake deposit portals that harvest a card number; a "family liaison" service that charges a monthly subscription for publicly available information. And the tail end of it: **a leftover third-party balance can turn into a collection letter with your name on it.** That letter is not junk mail. Read it, and if it's wrong, that's what the CFPB and legal aid are for.

**6. THE QUIET THIEF IS A SENTENCE: "We don't put our business in the street."**

That's it. That's the one that costs the most, and it doesn't take a dime directly.

Because here is what that sentence does: it guarantees that **eleven families in one congregation, sitting in the same four hundred square feet every Sunday for years, each paying the same company, never compare a single note.** They don't know they're eleven. Each one believes she is the only one. And so when the price changed, there was no network for the news to travel through. There was nobody to say *"girl, did you know they stopped charging for the calls?"* because nobody had ever admitted there was a call.

**Every predator in this post needs exactly one condition: that you don't say it out loud.** The fixer needs you too ashamed to ask the records office. The 9:40 caller needs you too ashamed to call the church. The deposit fee needs you never to compare notes with the woman three pews up who found the cheaper method. **All of it is priced on silence** — and unlike the silence in the storage post, which was a man's private shame about a unit, **this silence is a community agreement.** We taught it to each other. We called it dignity.

## VI. What Pernell Stovall actually did, in order

Ten steps. None of them cost more than a stamp.

**One.** He asked the question. He called the facility's own number — not the app — and asked what a call costs now, what a deposit costs now, and what all the deposit methods are. It took eleven minutes and he had to be transferred twice. **He had been paying, for a stretch of that four years, for a category of contact whose price had changed.**

**Two.** He added it up backward in his bank app. Forty minutes. **$14,218.** He wrote it on a legal pad in marker and he did not tear the page off.

**Three.** He changed how he sends money. Larger amounts, less often, by the cheapest published method instead of the 11-o'clock-at-night instant one. **Same dollars to his son. Substantially fewer dollars to a company.** This is the only step in the whole list that changes a number going forward, and it required no permission from anyone.

**Four.** He built the second line. A legal pad, a book of stamps, and a rule: **he writes on Sunday afternoons.** He checked the facility's mail rules first and learned that personal mail there gets scanned, which is why he now sends **photocopies of photographs and keeps every original** — the same rule as the storage post, arrived at from the opposite direction. His son now has forty-one letters. His son had, before this, approximately none, **because they had been spending their whole relationship on a meter.**

**Five.** He called the records office himself and asked his own question about his son's date, and got an answer, for free, from a person whose job that is. He had been carrying a number in his head for two years that he'd gotten secondhand from a man who wanted $600 to "look into it." **The number in his head was wrong.**

**Six.** He wrote to Uptown People's Law Center about a medical issue his son had been describing for months and getting nowhere on. He did not get a fast answer. He got an answer.

**Seven.** He filed an FCC complaint about a charge he could not get explained. He does not know yet if anything will come of it. He filed it anyway, and I want to name why that matters, because it's the same reason the mail-theft post insisted on reporting a crime after the money came back: **one family's complaint is the evidence base for the next family's rate.** You are not filing it for you.

**Eight.** He called 2-1-1 about his own house. This is the step men skip. **Three hundred dollars a month leaving a household for four years is a household financial crisis**, and he had never once treated it as one, because it had no name and no bill, so it felt like weather instead of a budget. The roof is still not fixed. But the winter shutoff protection and the township office were things he did not know applied to him.

**Nine.** He told his pastor. Out loud. In the office, with the door closed, at fifty-eight years old, about a twenty-seven-year-old son. *"I have never said that sentence in this building,"* he said. *"I have been sitting in the fourth row for nineteen years and I have never said my son's name in this building."*

**Ten.** He said the number out loud in the fellowship hall. **$14,218**, to a room of people who had fed him after his wife's funeral. And the room did not laugh, and the room did not flinch, and four people cried, and then **the room started talking** — and inside of twenty minutes it turned out that of the people sitting there, **six were still paying a third-party app for a category of call their facility now provides at no charge**, and one had been paying a deposit fee twice a month for **twenty-six months** into an account her son had stopped using a year earlier, **because he had been transferred and nobody had told her which of the two accounts was live.**

## VII. What this post will not tell you

Seven of these, and I will not soften any of them.

**One. Sometimes the calls really do cost money, and I am not promising anybody a free call.** State prisons, county jails, and federal facilities run on different rules, and those rules differ by facility and keep changing. The instruction is *ask*, not *assume*.

**Two. "Spend less" can never mean "call less."** I want to be very careful here, because a post about the cost of contact could accidentally talk a family into less contact, and that would be a genuinely harmful outcome. Contact between a person inside and their family is associated with better outcomes for everyone, including after release. So the goal of this entire post is **to pay less per unit of contact, not to have less contact.** If your only two options are pay the fee or go silent, **pay the fee.** Then go read the paragraph about the stamps. And if the weight of this is sitting on your chest at 2 in the morning, **988** is a call that costs nothing and is answered by a person.

**Three. He may have done it.** Nothing in this post depends on anybody being innocent, and nobody reading it owes me or anybody else a defense of their child. The fee is charged to you either way, **and you didn't do it either way.**

**Four. There is no door in this post that brings anybody home one day sooner.** Not one. Everything in here is about money, information, and contact. **Anybody who tells you otherwise is selling you something**, and now you know the tell: they are selling a favor.

**Five. The $14,218 is gone.** All of it. There is no refund, no restitution, no class action I can point you to, no undo button. The only number anybody can change is the one going forward, which is exactly what the storage post had to say about a unit that had already sold, and it is just as unsatisfying here.

**Six. Free legal help for people inside is real and badly oversubscribed.** A letter may take months to get an answer, and may get an answer that is no. Write it anyway. And a lawyer you pay is a different, legitimate thing from a fixer you pay — **the difference is that you looked the lawyer up and called him, and the fixer found you.**

**Seven. And the dignity, plainly.** A man who keeps six hundred children warm from a basement at 4:40 in the morning is not a bad father, and his son's sentence is not his sentence. Whatever anybody owes for whatever happened, **that debt is not paid down in installments of three dollars and ninety-five cents charged to a fifty-eight-year-old boiler engineer's debit card at eleven o'clock at night.** That money doesn't go to a victim. It doesn't go to a court. It doesn't reduce anything by one day. **It goes to a company.** And the reason it's collectible is not that Pernell Stovall did anything wrong — it's that **he loves somebody who cannot leave, and someone figured out how to put a meter on that.**

## VIII. Four hundred and sixty-eight Sundays

Sister Delois Dupree has kept the roll at Greater Mount Moriah for thirty-one years, and she is the one who made this a table instead of a conversation.

Her own son went away for nine years. She was the clerk then too. And every Sunday for nine years she wrote **"absent"** next to his name in a book she knew would be read aloud at the annual meeting — because the book had a column for absent, and a column for sick, and a column for transferred out, and **no column for where he actually was.**

*"I have thought about this for a long time,"* she says. *"That book is the most honest thing in this church. We write down who was here. And for nine years I used the honest book to tell a lie by leaving something out. Four hundred and sixty-eight times, in my own hand, in ink."*

Her son has been home six years. He sits in the third row now. And she says the thing that made Pernell Stovall say his number out loud:

*"Baby, I could have told you. I knew. That's the part I have to carry — I have known for years exactly which families in here are paying, because I'm the one with the book. I knew and I kept the roll and I never said a word to any of them, because I was protecting them the same way I protected him. And what I was actually doing was keeping eleven families from ever finding each other."*

So on a second Sunday in September she put a folding table in the fellowship hall.

Nothing on it but a roll of stamps, a box of legal pads, a laptop open to one facility's own published rules page, a legal pad with **$14,218** written on it in marker, and her thirty-one-year-old membership book, **open to the page with her son's name on it.** Poster board propped against a chair:

**WHAT DOES IT COST NOW? AND WHO TOLD YOU?**

No sermon. No seminar. Nobody selling. A boiler engineer and a church clerk at a table.

**Seventeen people sat down.**

Eleven were families with somebody inside, **and not one of the eleven had ever said it to another one of them, in that building, in years.** They found out that morning that they were eleven.

**Six** were still paying a third-party app for a category of call their facility now provides at no charge. One of them had been doing it for over two years.

**One** woman had been paying a deposit fee twice a month for twenty-six months into an account her son stopped using a year ago, after a transfer.

**A seventy-one-year-old man** learned from the records office — for free, on the phone, from the table, in nine minutes — that his grandson's date was more than a year different from what he'd been told by a man who had taken **$600** to "look into it." He put his hand flat on the table and said, *"I paid six hundred dollars for a wrong answer I could have had for nothing."*

**Four people wrote a letter at the table**, right there, on the legal pads, and Sister Dupree put the stamps on and walked them to the mailbox on the corner herself. Two of them had not written in over a year. One of them, a woman of about forty, said she had stopped writing because she never knew what to say, and Sister Dupree told her: *"Then write what you cooked. That's not nothing. Where he is, what you cooked is the news."*

And **three people together added up their own bank apps** at that table and got three numbers, and then sat with each other while they were true.

They call it **THE SECOND LINE.** Second Sunday, every month, by the kitchen door.

Sister Dupree brought the membership book that first morning on purpose, and she brought it back the second month, and she does something with it now. She has started a new page in the back — not a list of the absent. **A list of the eleven.** Names of people not in the room, written in the clerk's hand, in the church's book, where the clerk's hand means *these are ours.*

*"The roll was never a list of who showed up,"* she says. *"Thirty-one years I had that wrong. It's a list of who belongs. And you cannot be absent from a church. You can only be away from it."*

She read her son's name out loud at the annual meeting this year. First time in sixteen years. He was sitting in the third row while she did it.

And Pernell Stovall keeps that legal-pad page with **$14,218** on it folded in the pocket of the jacket he wears into the boiler room at 4:40 in the morning.

*"Not because I'm mad about the money,"* he says. *"I'd pay it again tomorrow, every dollar, and anybody with a child knows that, so the company knows it too — that's the whole business. I keep it because for four years I was a man who reads gauges for a living and writes the numbers on a clipboard, and I never once wrote down this number. Twenty-four years I have not guessed at a temperature in my life. And the biggest thing going out of my house, I never measured, because nobody handed me a gauge."*

*"So now I'm the gauge."*

## IX. Where The Plug AI actually fits

This is a decoder at a kitchen table, and nothing more than that.

It can take a facility's own published rate sheet, deposit-method list, mail rules, and visiting rules and put them in plain English — what each deposit method actually costs, which one is cheapest for how much you can spare, and what the mail policy means about sending a photograph. It can help you do the backward addition in your bank history and sit with the number. It can write out **the six sentences to say to a records office** so you don't freeze up on the phone, and the sentences to ask about a transfer, a date, or a medical issue. It can draft **a first letter when you have not written in a year and don't know what to say** — starting with what you cooked. It can help you find the free legal-aid numbers, the FCC complaint form, the inmate locator, and 2-1-1. It can help you tell the difference, in thirty seconds, between **a rule with a phone number and a favor with a price.** It can help a grandmother write the nine sentences to ask a pastor for a folding table. **It augments the people already in that fellowship hall; it does not replace a single one of them** — not the clerk with the book, not the pastor, not the records officer, and not the legal-aid lawyer. And it can help with the hardest text in this post, the one to an eight-year-old who asked why she only gets to talk to her daddy for fifteen minutes.

**And it is an augment, never a replacement — twice, because this one matters.** It is not a lawyer and gives no legal advice. It is not a financial advisor. It does not represent anybody, does not contact a facility for you, does not file anything, cannot get anybody transferred, released, or resentenced, and will never tell you a release date — **the records office does that, for free.** It does not read a facility's rules as law, because those rules change and only that facility's own current information governs. It never asks for an SSN, a bank login, a card number, or a gift card. **And if anything calling itself The Plug AI ever asks you for a fee to help with somebody who is incarcerated, it is not us — and that request is itself the tell.**

Eleven families sat in the same room for years, each one certain they were the only one, while the price changed and nobody told them. That is not a failure of eleven families. **That is a failure of eleven families never being in the same conversation** — which is the one thing a church is structurally built to fix, and the one thing it cannot do while everybody is protecting everybody else with silence.

A favor has a price. A rule has a phone number. And a stamp cannot be shut off.

**Ask what it costs now. Ask who told you. And then go tell somebody else, out loud, in the building.**
