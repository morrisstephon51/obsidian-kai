**Centered on:** SISTER ROSALIND "ROZ" WINBUSH, 48, West Pullman, on Chicago's Far South Side — nineteen years a **certified pharmacy technician** at a chain drugstore, $19.80 an hour, the woman behind the counter who knows which customers are splitting pills and which ones come in on the third of the month, who has counted out other people's medicine with a spatula and a tray five days a week for two decades without one error she can be blamed for. She raised **Marquita**, 22, now in her second year at a community college, and **Trevell**, 14, who plays trombone badly and loudly. In January her mother, **MOTHER ELNORA WINBUSH**, died at seventy-four in the house on 119th Street she had lived in for **forty-one years** — the house where every Winbush Thanksgiving happened, where the good dishes lived in a cabinet nobody was allowed to touch, where a green Charles Chips tin on the closet shelf held every loose photograph the family owned back to 1961. The house had to be sold to settle what the family owed on it. Roz's brother **Dewayne** handled that part from Milwaukee, over the phone, badly. And so eleven days after she buried her mother, Roz Winbush was told she had **eight days** to empty forty-one years out of that house. She did not have a basement. She did not have a garage. She was, at that exact moment, sleeping in her aunt's back room in West Pullman with Trevell on a daybed, because she had given up her own apartment in October to help pay for her mother's last four months of home care. Seven months later, on a Saturday morning at ten minutes after nine, all of it sold **in one lot for $310** to a man with a box truck, and Roz Winbush was at work, in a blue smock, counting somebody's blood-pressure pills, with **$328 in cash in her purse** that she had brought to the office four days earlier and that had not been enough.

**Faith anchor:** DEACON OZELL "OZ" FAIRCHILD, 66, of Beulah Grove Missionary Baptist Church in West Pullman — **twenty-nine years a furniture delivery driver** for a furniture chain that no longer exists on this side of town, and for the last nine years **chairman of the trustee board**, which is the reason he is in this post. It wasn't because I'm smart. It was because it wasn't my money. When it was my own mother's things, I signed it like everybody signs it — standing up, with a pen that wasn't mine, in a hurry."* Twenty-nine years he carried other people's lives up stairs. He can still tell you what a full-size mattress weighs going around a landing, what a china cabinet does to a doorframe, which buildings have the stairs that turn twice. And in 2019, when his mother **MISS ARDELIA FAIRCHILD** went into a nursing home in Dolton, Oz Fairchild emptied her apartment into a 10x10 on a month-to-month, *"just until I could sort it right."* Then the furniture chain closed two stores and Oz was laid off for seven months at fifty-nine years old. He let the unit go three months. Then four. He remembers thinking, in the exact words, *I'll get it out when I get back on my feet.* They sold it. He found out from a man at church who mentioned — kindly, conversationally, with no idea what he was doing — that he'd seen a china cabinet just like Miss Ardelia's in a resale booth off Ashland. Oz drove out there on a Sunday after service in his good clothes. The cabinet was already gone. What he bought back, for **twenty dollars**, was a cracked wooden picture frame with nothing in it — the frame that had held his parents' wedding photograph since 1954. The photograph was gone. *"Somebody buys old pictures for the frames,"* the man told him, not unkindly, *"the pictures mostly get thrown out."* That frame rides on the dashboard of Oz Fairchild's truck to this day, empty, and for **four years he never told one person at that church what it was.** His mother lived two more years. Twice she asked him about her own mother's quilt. Both times he told her it was safe. *"I carried other people's whole lives up three flights of stairs for twenty-nine years,"* he says. *"I know exactly what a house weighs. What I did not know was what a late fee weighs. And I lied to a dying woman about a quilt, and that's the bill I'm still paying on. So when Sister Winbush came in that Sunday and sat in the back and wouldn't look at nobody, I knew. I have known that face since 2019. *(What follows is general navigation for education — not legal, financial, or insurance advice, and not a promise about anybody's property, claim, or outcome. Self-storage in Illinois is governed by the **Illinois Self-Service Storage Facility Act** and, just as much, by **the written rental agreement you personally signed** — and the two of them together set the notice steps, the lien, the deadlines, and what a facility can and cannot do. The steps and the days are real and they are short. Do not take any number in this post as your number: ask the facility in writing, read your own agreement, and call free legal aid. Nothing here is a substitute for either one.)*

**Resources — the real doors, free or low-cost, no fee to a stranger, ever:**

- **THE FREE THING THAT COSTS NOTHING AND CHANGES THE MOST: go today and take out THE ONE BOX.** Not the couch. Not the dining set. One box, and it rides home with you tonight. In it: every piece of paper (birth and death certificates, titles, deeds, benefit letters, discharge papers, immigration papers, insurance policies), **every photograph you cannot buy again**, medicine, spare keys, jewelry that means something, the tools you actually earn money with, and one comfort item per child. This takes an hour, requires nobody's permission, costs zero dollars, and it is the only door in this entire post that **cannot be taken away from you by a calendar.** Everything else here depends on a date, a notice, a manager's mood, or a lawyer. The box depends on you and a Saturday.
- **CHANGE THE ADDRESS AND THE PHONE NUMBER ON THAT ACCOUNT — IN WRITING — AND GET CONFIRMATION.** This is the free step that decided Roz Winbush's whole case. When a facility has to notify you that you are in default and that your property may be sold, **the notice goes to the address and the contact information you gave them.** People sign storage contracts in exactly the weeks their address is changing — after a funeral, a fire, a divorce, an eviction, a move, a deployment — and then never update it, and the single most important letter of the entire transaction gets mailed to a house where a stranger now lives. Update it every single time anything changes. Ask them to confirm it back to you in writing or by email, and keep that. Ask them specifically: *"What address and what phone number and what email do you have on this account right now — read them back to me."*
- **KNOW YOUR NUMBER AND KNOW YOUR DATE. Ask these four questions, and ask for the answers in writing:** (1) *What is the exact dollar amount, today, that stops a sale?* (2) *What date does the default and lien process start, and what is the last day I can pay?* (3) **Does a partial payment stop the sale — yes or no?** (4) *Who is the manager, and what is the name of the person telling me this?* Question 3 is the one that costs people everything. Roz brought $328 in cash. It was applied to her balance. **It did not stop the sale**, and nobody wrote that down for her, and she walked out of that office believing she had bought herself time she had not bought.
- **ASK EARLY, IN PERSON, FOR THE THREE THINGS A MANAGER CAN ACTUALLY DO.** A facility manager is not your enemy and not your friend. She is a person with a calendar, a corporate policy, and **some discretion** — and discretion mostly exists *before* a lien, and mostly evaporates *after* it. The three things worth asking for, in person, early, politely, with a name: **(a)** a written payment arrangement; **(b)** a **smaller, cheaper unit** — you can very often move from a 10x15 to a 5x10 and cut the rent, and a downsize is far easier to survive than a default; **(c)** a **move-out date** where you empty the unit and settle the balance, which for many people is the honest answer and beats paying rent on furniture worth less than the rent. You are far more likely to get all three in person on day nine than on the phone on day fifty-nine.
- **READ FOUR LINES IN THE AGREEMENT YOU ALREADY HAVE IN A DRAWER (or ask them to email you a copy — free):** the **default and lien** clause (what they can do, and when); the **late-fee schedule** (what each miss costs, and what other fees can be added — lien fees, lock fees, advertising or auction-preparation fees); the **access-denial / overlock** clause (the day they put their own lock on the door, which is the day you can no longer walk in and get your box, and it comes *earlier* than the sale); and the **notice provision** (where and how they will send you the default notice). Also read the **insurance requirement**, because most agreements have one.
- **THE RIGHT TO PAY AND GET YOUR PROPERTY BACK BEFORE IT IS SOLD.** Under the Act and your agreement there is generally a window in which you can cure the default — pay what is owed and get your things — up until the sale happens. **The exact amount, the exact deadline, and whether anything less than the full amount will do it are set by the statute and by your contract, not by me.** So ask for it in writing, that day, in those words: *"Tell me in writing the amount and the date that gets my property back."* And if the clock is close, call free legal aid the same day, not next week.
- **IF IT HAS ALREADY BEEN SOLD, YOU ARE NOT AUTOMATICALLY OUT OF OPTIONS — ASK FOR THE PAPER TRAIL, IN WRITING.** Request the account ledger, the copies of the notices they sent, the address they sent them to, the date, the method, and the advertisement. **The notice steps are not optional for the facility.** If a facility did not follow the steps the Act and the agreement require, that is a genuine legal question, and it belongs in front of **free legal aid** the same week — not in front of a company that charges you to ask it. (And if it comes to it, Illinois small claims goes up to $10,000 with no lawyer required and free court forms — that is a different post's territory, and its own honest warning applies: a judgment is not a photograph.)
- **THE DOOR ALMOST NOBODY KNOWS: MOST OF THE PAPER CAN BE RE-ISSUED, AND SOME OF IT IS FREE.** This is the most practical paragraph in this post, and it is the one Roz Winbush wishes somebody had told her in February, because if she had known it, she would have grabbed the **tin** instead of the folder. **A replacement Social Security card is free** (ssa.gov or 1-800-772-1213). **Birth and death certificates** can be re-ordered from the county clerk where the event happened, or from Illinois Vital Records, for a modest fee each. A **duplicate vehicle title** comes from the Illinois Secretary of State for a fee. A veteran's **DD-214** (discharge papers) can be requested free through the National Archives or with free help from a VSO. **Immunization records** can usually be pulled free through the school district or the child's clinic. **IRS tax transcripts are free** at irs.gov. Bank statements, insurance policies, benefit award letters, mortgage and closing documents — all re-issuable, most free or nearly free, all by phone. Roz replaced everything in that manila folder for about a hundred and twenty dollars and four phone calls. **Paper can be re-issued. Photographs cannot.** Which leads to the free ten-minute act that follows from it: **photograph your photographs** with the camera already in your hand, and send them to one other person, so the pictures live in two places instead of one. A picture of a picture is not the picture. It is also not nothing.
- **FREE LEGAL HELP, AND IT IS GENUINELY FREE: Illinois Legal Aid Online (illinoislegalaid.org)** — plain English, 24 hours, no appointment; **CARPLS at 312-738-9200**, the Cook County legal-aid hotline; **Legal Aid Chicago**; and **Land of Lincoln Legal Aid** and **Prairie State Legal Services** for the collar and downstate counties. Call them *before* the sale date if there is any way to do it, and call them after if it has already happened and you think the notice was wrong.
- **TO REPORT THE REST OF IT: the Illinois Attorney General Consumer Fraud Hotline, 1-800-386-5438**, for a facility that will not produce a ledger or that charged fees the contract doesn't authorize; **the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov or 1-877-382-4357**; **the FBI's IC3 at ic3.gov** for a fake online "storage auction," a fake gate code, or a deposit sent by app to a stranger; and **the CFPB at consumerfinance.gov/complaint** if a leftover storage balance shows up on your credit report or in the hands of a collection agency — because it can, and because a letter from a collector **after** the sale is not junk mail and should not be ignored.
- **FOR THE PART NO LAW FIXES: 2-1-1 (call 211 or 211.org)** — township general assistance, St. Vincent de Paul, the Salvation Army, Habitat **ReStore**, furniture banks, household-goods ministries, and church benevolence, for a family that has to build a household back out of nothing. And **988 (call or text), free, 24 hours**, because a woman sitting in a parked car in a drugstore lot on her lunch break, having just found out her mother's Bible sold for part of three hundred and ten dollars, is in a specific kind of trouble that is not about money.

## I. Eight days to empty forty-one years

Here is the thing about the week people sign a storage contract.

Nobody rents a storage unit on a good day. Look at the actual list of reasons a person on our side of town ends up standing at that counter: somebody died. Somebody got sick and went into a nursing home. There was a fire. There was a divorce. The landlord sold the building. The lease ran out and the next place wasn't ready. The kids moved back in. A son came home from prison and needed the back room. Somebody got deployed. The house went into foreclosure. Two households had to become one household by the fifteenth.

**Every single one of those is a week in which a person is exhausted, grieving, ashamed, and out of time.** And that is the week the industry sells into. That is not a conspiracy; it's just the truth about when the product gets bought. The sign said FIRST MONTH $1 and the sign was not lying — it was doing exactly what it was designed to do, which was to be the one number a woman with eight days and a funeral program still in her purse could actually say yes to.

Roz Winbush is not a careless woman. I want to be careful about this, because the whole rest of this post falls apart if you let yourself think *well, she should have kept up with it.* This is a woman who has counted out other people's medication for nineteen years. Nineteen years of double-checking a label against a script against a bottle, five days a week, because a mistake at her job is not a fee, it's a funeral. She has never been late on a rent she could reach. She gave up her own apartment in October to pay for her mother's home care — that is not a woman who can't manage money, that is a woman who spent hers on the right thing.

What she had was eight days, one truck she borrowed, two teenagers, a full-time job, and forty-one years of another woman's life to lift.

And what she signed, standing up, in a hurry, four days after a funeral, was a contract that said a number she did not read, in a clause she did not know existed, about a lien she had never heard the word for.

*"If you had asked me in February what a lien was,"* she says, *"I'd have told you it's something the county puts on a house. I'd have got that half right and it would have cost me my mother's pictures."*

## II. What she thought she was paying, and what she was actually paying

She thought the unit was **$129 a month.** She had seen $129 on the website, and she is not wrong that she saw it — $129 was the online rate, for a smaller unit, at a different location.

What she signed for was **$164 a month** on a 10x15, plus a **$24 administrative fee** at signing, plus **$11 a month** for a required protection plan. First month: one dollar. Second month and every month after: $175 when you add it up, which is **thirty-six percent more per month than the number in her head**, and she did not find that out in February. She found it out in March, when the autopay came out, and she looked at it, and she felt the way we all feel, which is: *I don't have the energy for this today, and I can't move all that furniture again anyway, and it's only for a few months.*

Then in May, her bank reissued her debit card. Something about a breach at a gas station — she didn't even ask, she just got the new card in the mail and activated it like a responsible person and threw the old one away.

**And the autopay quietly died.**

Not with an alarm. Not with a phone call. It just stopped working, the way a card-on-file always stops working when the card changes, on every account you have ever set up and forgotten about. June's rent didn't come out. July's rent didn't come out. Roz Winbush, meanwhile, was living the actual life of a forty-eight-year-old woman with a dead mother, a daughter in community college, a fourteen-year-old, a full-time job on her feet, and a grief that made whole weeks disappear. She did not notice a $175 charge that *stopped* appearing. **Nobody notices the absence of a withdrawal.** That is one of the genuinely cruel small facts of modern money, and it is worth saying out loud in a fellowship hall: *you will notice a charge you didn't make. You will not notice a charge that failed.*

Here is what happened next, and I want to list it as a ledger, because the ledger is the lesson:

- Day six of June: **late fee, $20.**
- Day six of July: **late fee, $20.**
- Late July: **lien fee, $50.**
- Early August: **overlock** — the facility's own red lock, added to her door. **$15.** From that day forward she could not have gotten her own box out even if she had driven over there with a key, and she did not know that, because she had not read the clause and nobody had ever said the word to her.
- Mid-August: **auction preparation and advertising fee, $75.**
- Plus three months of rent at $164, plus a processing charge.

**Total to open that door: $711.**

Seven hundred and eleven dollars, on a unit she had rented in February for one dollar, to protect property with a resale value of maybe eight hundred dollars and a replacement value, as she puts it, *"of my whole childhood."*

And that is the first thing this post exists to teach, so let me put it flat:

**The fees are not the punishment for the miss. The fees are the mechanism that turns a miss into a sale.** A $20 late fee is survivable. A $20 late fee plus a $50 lien fee plus a lock plus a $75 advertising fee plus the two months that went by while you were working up the nerve to go in there — that is a number most people in our churches cannot produce in one piece, and *a number you cannot produce in one piece is the same thing as a door you cannot open.*

## III. The letter that went to a house with a stranger in it

Now the part that decided everything, and the part that is free to fix, and the reason I am writing about this at all.

**They sent her the notice. They sent it to the address on the account. The address on the account was her mother's house on 119th Street.**

The house that had been sold in March.

Think about the shape of this. In February, Roz Winbush did not have an address she believed in. She was in her aunt's back room with a fourteen-year-old on a daybed and a whole life in boxes, and when the clipboard said ADDRESS she wrote down the only address that had meant anything to her for forty-eight years — her mother's. Her mail was going there anyway; the house hadn't closed yet; it was February and she was four days out from a funeral and it was the address her hand already knew how to write.

The phone number on the account was a prepaid number she stopped using in April.

So in July and August, the most important letter of this entire transaction — the letter that says *you are in default and your property may be sold* — traveled a few miles, went into a mailbox at a house that now had new people in it, and did what mail does when it arrives for somebody who doesn't live there anymore. And the phone calls went to a number that rang somewhere for somebody else.

**She did not get the notice because of a mistake she made in the worst week of her life, and the mistake took ninety seconds to make and would have taken ninety seconds to fix.**

Now, hear me carefully: the law has things to say about how a facility has to give notice, and whether they did it right in any given case is a real question for a real legal-aid lawyer, and if you are in this situation **that is exactly the question to ask them.** I am not going to tell you her notice was defective, because I don't know, and neither does anybody who hasn't read her ledger. What I am going to tell you is this:

**You cannot control whether a company follows its steps. You can absolutely control what address and what phone number those steps are aimed at.** That is free. That is a phone call. That is the single highest-return ninety seconds in this entire post, and almost nobody does it, because the week you sign is the week your address is in motion — which is precisely the week the industry meets you.

Two free facts decide a storage case: **a date and an address.** Both of them get given away by silence.

## IV. Nine-ten on a Saturday morning, and $310

She found out on the Tuesday before.

Not from a letter. From a woman at the drugstore counter — a customer, seventy-something, who mentioned that the storage place on Halsted had a bunch of units going up that weekend, they always post the list, her nephew goes to those. Roz Winbush stood there in her blue smock with a bottle of lisinopril in her hand and her stomach went out from under her.

She went on her lunch break. That is when she learned the word **overlock**, standing in an office with her keys in her hand, looking at a computer screen turned halfway toward her.

**$711.**

She had $328. She had gotten $200 of it by picking up a shift, and $128 of it from Marquita, her twenty-two-year-old, over Cash App, and she had not told her daughter what it was for, and she is not over that yet. She put all of it on the counter in cash. The young man behind the counter took it, applied it to the account, printed her a receipt, and was, by every account including hers, perfectly polite.

She drove back to work believing she had bought time.

**She had not bought time. She had bought a smaller balance.** The sale was not stopped, because a partial payment does not necessarily stop a sale, and that sentence — which took me eleven words — was not said to her, and she did not know to ask, and she is a smart woman who reads labels for a living.

*"He didn't lie to me,"* she says. *"I want to be fair. He didn't lie to me one time. He just answered exactly the question I asked. I asked how much I owed. I did not know there was a different question."*

**The different question is: what number stops the sale, and what day does it stop being enough?**

Saturday morning, 9:10 a.m., she was at work. The lot on Halsted had maybe fifteen people in it. Her mother's forty-one years went **in one lot for $310** to a man with a box truck and a dolly, which is roughly what forty-one years of an ordinary Black woman's household goods are worth on the open market at nine in the morning, and if that sentence makes you angry, good, hold onto that too.

The dining set her mother bought on layaway in 1984: gone. Her father's tools: gone. The Singer: gone. The seven quilts: gone, and one of them had been stitched by a woman born in Mississippi in 1911 whose name is on the back of it in thread.

Then a small mercy, and it came from a stranger.

A woman who had been at the sale — Roz knows her only as Miss Pat, who buys and resells — remembered the truck, and told her where the man's booth was, at a swap meet off Ashland. Roz drove out there the next Sunday after church in her good clothes, the same way Oz Fairchild had in 2019 and neither of them knew that about each other yet.

She got back **three things for sixty dollars**: the green Charles Chips tin, with every loose photograph the family owned from 1961 forward. Marquita's baby book. And her mother's Bible, with all three funeral programs still pressed in the back.

The four photo albums were already gone. *"Somebody buys those for the frames and the scrapbooks,"* the man told her. *"The pictures mostly get thrown out."*

She sat in her car in that lot and held a cookie tin with both hands for twenty minutes before she could drive.

## V. What is actually being taken, because it is not the furniture

Every post in this series so far has measured a harm in dollars. Wages not paid. A check washed for $6,240. A washer that cost $3,119.20. Retirement that came to zero. That is the right unit for almost everything, because for working people the dollar is the unit of injury.

**This one does not measure in dollars, and that is what makes it different, and that is why the church needs to hear it.**

If you total up Roz Winbush's loss in money, you get a number that sounds almost manageable: she paid about $1,200 in rent and fees across seven months, lost property with a garage-sale value of maybe eight hundred dollars, and spent about $120 replacing paper. A little over two thousand dollars. There are posts in this series about people who lost five times that in an afternoon.

Now total it the other way.

**Every photograph of her father that was in an album instead of the tin.** Her grandmother's quilt. Her father's tools — not the resale value, the *tools*, the ones she wouldn't let anybody borrow, that had his sweat in the handles. A sewing machine that made her Easter dresses. And the specific, unrepeatable fact that **her mother's house is gone and her mother is gone and now most of the objects that proved either of them existed are gone too**, and the only three that came back came back because a stranger named Miss Pat happened to remember a truck.

There is a post in this series about a mother who lost everything she owned in a house fire, and I want to name it here on purpose, because it is the closest cousin this domain has and it is **not the same thing.** A fire takes it in forty minutes and nobody mailed you a warning first, and there is a Red Cross and a disaster program and a whole architecture of help built on the words *it wasn't your fault.* **This takes seven months, and somewhere in the middle of it a letter with your name on it went to an address, and the architecture of help is almost entirely paperwork you were supposed to have read.** That difference is exactly why nobody brings a casserole for a storage auction, and it is why people go through it silently, and it is the reason for the table at the end of this post.

You cannot buy that. There is no program in this post that restores it. Free legal aid cannot restore it. A judgment cannot restore it. In the best imaginable case, where a facility did everything wrong and a lawyer proves it, **what you get is money — and money is not the quilt.** That is the honest inversion of a lesson this series has taught before about suing people: *a judgment is not cash.* Here it's worse. **Even cash is not the thing.**

Which is why the number-one door in this post is not legal and not financial. It's a cardboard box and one hour on a Saturday.

**If you can't buy it again, it rides up front with you.**

Not in the back of the truck. Not in the unit. Up front, on the seat, where your hand can touch it. Papers, pictures, medicine, keys, the tools you eat off of, one thing per child that they'd cry about. That's it. That's the rule. It costs nothing and it is stronger than every statute cited in this post, because it doesn't depend on anybody's calendar but yours.

## VI. Six predators, and all six need the same one condition

The first two are not scams. They are the business operating as designed, and I am naming them first on purpose, because **the largest harm in this domain is entirely legal**, and if you only warn people about the crooks you will leave them defenseless against the paperwork.

**1) The price that isn't the price.** FIRST MONTH $1, and then a rate that is not the rate you saw online, plus an administrative fee, plus a required monthly protection charge, plus — and this one catches long-term tenants hardest — **a rent increase after you're in.** Once your household is inside a 10x15, the cost of shopping around is a truck and eight men, so the leverage flips completely the day the door closes. *The defense:* before you sign, ask in writing — **"what will this unit cost me per month for the next twelve months, all in, and when can the rate change?"** Then multiply. Twelve months at $175 is $2,100. Three years is $6,300. It is the same multiplication that unmasks a rent-to-own contract, in a different coat: **if the number is bigger than what's inside, the unit isn't storing your things. It's storing a decision you haven't made yet.**

**2) The fee stack and the silent autopay.** A late fee, a lien fee, a lock fee, an advertising or auction-prep fee — each one defensible on its own, and together the escalator from *I'm a little behind* to *my property is for sale on Saturday.* Plus the card-on-file that dies when your bank reissues your card, which is the most common single trigger there is. *The defense:* **look at the charge every month on purpose, with your own eyes, for thirty seconds.** A statement you never open is a smoke detector with the battery out.

**3) The fake auction, and the fake unit.** Storage auctions are real, public, and popular — which is exactly why there are fake ones. Listings on social media for a unit "full of tools, must clear today," a **deposit by CashApp or Zelle**, a gate code that doesn't work, a phone that stops answering. And the mirror version aimed at victims: an outfit that will "buy your unit back for you" if you pay them first. *The tells:* a real facility takes payment at a counter or on the account you already have; nobody legitimate needs a deposit sent to a personal app to a stranger's name; and a gate code is not a receipt. Report it to **IC3 at ic3.gov** and the **FTC**.

**4) The lien-rescue advance fee.** This one found Roz Winbush inside forty-eight hours of her searching the words *storage auction stop* on her phone at eleven at night. **$400 to "halt your auction," "we have relationships with the facilities," "act now, we can file today."** There is nothing to file. What that company sells for $400 is **a phone call you can make for free and a payment you have to make yourself anyway.** Same species as the debt-relief mill, the expungement mill, and the "we recover your losses" outfit: *charging a poor person for a free door, at the exact hour they are most afraid.* And note the arithmetic insult in this particular one — **the $400 they wanted was more than half the $711 that would have actually stopped the sale.**

**5) The imposter text and the imposter "manager."** *"URGENT: Unit 214 is scheduled for auction in 24 hours. Pay $89 now to hold your unit: [link]."* Or a call from a "manager" asking for a gift card, or a Zelle to a personal number, or your card over the phone. *The tells:* **a lien is not cured by a gift card, ever, by anybody**; no legitimate facility holds a sale for $89; and the word "URGENT" plus a 24-hour clock plus a link is the signature of this entire genre. And the same rule that works on every one of these applies here: **don't tap the thing in the message. Look the place up yourself, with your own thumbs, and call the number on the actual door you drove past.**
  — **And the cousin of this, which belongs to the movers, not the facilities:** an outfit that will "move you and store it for you," and the unit turns out to be **rented in their name, not yours.** If your name is not on that lease, you cannot legally get your own belongings out of it, and *nothing in this post helps you fast.* Mover fraud — the hostage load, the truck that won't unload — is its own domain with its own regulators; this one is different. Here the truck arrives fine. Here it's **the door that won't open, seven months later.** Get your name on the agreement or don't sign anything.

**6) The quiet thief, and it isn't a company. It's a sentence.**

***"I'll get it out when I get on my feet."***

Oz Fairchild said it in 2019 about his mother's china cabinet. Roz Winbush said it in April about a dining set. It is not laziness and it is not denial exactly — it's the most reasonable sentence in the world, and it's fatal, because **the unit does not wait for your feet.** It runs on a calendar that has nothing to do with your recovery.

Wrapped around that sentence is the shame that keeps the whole thing sealed: **a storage unit is a confession.** It means you lost the house, or the marriage, or the mother, or the room. People do not tell their church they have a unit for the same reason they don't tell their church about a payday loan. So they don't ask. And here is the structural fact that ties every one of these six together, the same one that has shown up in this series over and over until I can no longer call it a coincidence:

**Every single predator in this post — the fee stack, the rate hike, the fake auction, the $400 rescue, the imposter text — requires exactly one condition to work. That you don't go in and ask.** Every last one of them is priced on silence. Which means the countermeasure to all six is not a product and not a lawyer. It's a behavior: **say it out loud, early, in writing, to a person whose name you get.**

## VII. What she actually did, in order

After the Saturday. In order, because the order is the teaching.

**One.** She went back and asked for everything in writing: the ledger, the fee breakdown, copies of the notices, the address they were sent to, the dates, and the advertisement. Some of it came slowly. She kept asking. *"I wrote down the day and the time every time I called, on a legal pad. Nineteen years in a pharmacy teaches you to document."*

**Two.** She called **CARPLS** and then Illinois Legal Aid Online, and she asked the real question, which was not *can I get my mother's things back* — she already knew that answer — but ***did they follow their steps, and does that mean anything now.*** Which is the correct question, and which she could not have formulated in July, and which cost her nothing to ask in October.

**Three.** She replaced the paper. All of it. Social Security cards, free. Her birth certificate and the death certificates, a fee each. A duplicate title for the Buick from the Secretary of State. Trevell's shot record, free, through his school. About **$120 and four phone calls**, and she says the tears came when the man on the phone at Social Security said *"that'll come in about two weeks, ma'am, no charge,"* because she had spent five weeks believing that folder was a catastrophe. **It was an errand. Nobody had told her the difference between an errand and a catastrophe.**

**Four.** She photographed every single picture in the green tin with her phone — two hundred and six of them, four evenings at her aunt's kitchen table — and sent them to Marquita, to Dewayne in Milwaukee, and to two cousins. *"Now it's in five houses and a cloud. My mother's face is in five houses. It took me four nights."*

**Five.** She told Marquita the truth about the $128, and what it had been for, and that it hadn't been enough. She says that was harder than the auction.

**Six.** She reported the **$400 lien-rescue outfit** and the **$89 text** to the Illinois Attorney General and the FTC, and the fake-deposit listing to IC3. *"They're going to find somebody else. But my report plus somebody else's report is how they get found."*

**Seven.** She went to Oz Fairchild — because he was the trustee who had the church's storage lease, and she thought maybe he'd know how to read the paperwork. She was in that man's kitchen for two hours. He got out a cracked empty picture frame and told a sixty-six-year-old man's secret to a forty-eight-year-old woman he barely knew, and then he said the sentence that started the rest of this: ***"Twenty-three people in this church have got a unit right now, and I'd bet my truck not four of them know what day theirs sells."***

**Eight.** And then the two of them went and did the only genuinely preventive thing in this entire post, which was to set up a folding table in a church parking lot.

## VIII. The honest caveats, because this post cannot get anybody's mother's things back

Five runs of this series have ended with a section like this, and this one is the hardest to write.

**One — sometimes the unit is the right call, and this post shames nobody who has one.** After a fire, a funeral, a divorce, a deployment, a hospital stay, an eviction: a storage unit is sometimes the only thing standing between a family and putting a household on a curb. The rule is not *never*. The rule is: **a unit is a bridge with a date on it, not a basement.** Write the date on your own refrigerator. If the date passes twice, the unit has stopped being a bridge and started being a bill.

**Two — when it's sold, it is generally sold.** Illinois gives a facility a lien on the property inside and a lawful way to sell it. If they followed their steps, there is usually nothing to undo, and I am not going to end this post pretending otherwise. And even in the case where a lawyer proves they did it wrong, **what comes back is money.** Not the quilt.

**Three — a partial payment may not stop a sale.** It is the single most expensive thing Roz Winbush did not know, and it is worth repeating three times in a fellowship hall. **Ask what number stops it and what day it stops being enough, and get it in writing.**

**Four — free legal aid is real, and the auction calendar is faster.** The lawyers in this post are excellent and free and they cannot always beat eleven days. Which is why the free *behavioral* doors — the box, the address, the phone call, the question — outrank every legal door here. **The only reliably winnable part of this fight happens before the lien.**

**Five — insurance pays for value, and most of what matters has none.** Roz paid $11 a month for seven months for a protection plan. Ask what any such plan actually covers and what it requires you to prove — and understand this clearly: **no protection plan anywhere covers your own late payment.** That is not a loophole, that is the whole design. Insurance is for water and fire and theft. **A lien sale is not a loss; it is a contract working.**

**Six — the re-supply doors can furnish a house, and that is not nothing.** 2-1-1, the township, St. Vincent de Paul, the Salvation Army, ReStore, furniture banks, church benevolence: they can get a family beds and a table, and if you are reading this after a sale, use them and do not feel a way about it. **But there is no benevolence fund for a photograph.** Say that plainly and then sit in it a second. And if you are reading this the week after a sale, sitting up at night with a list in your head of what was in there — that is grief, not paperwork, and it is a legitimate reason to call **988**, free, 24 hours, call or text. You are not calling about furniture and everybody on that line knows it.

**Seven — the dignity reframe, and then I'll stop.** Roz Winbush did not lose her mother's things because she is irresponsible. She lost them because **grief and an eight-day deadline signed a contract in a parking lot**, because nobody in her life had ever read one of those agreements out loud, because a bank reissued a card, and because the only number she was ever shown was **one dollar.** She is a woman who has not made a medication error in nineteen years. The deficit was never her judgment. It was that in the worst week of her life, the only person explaining the paperwork to her worked for the company.

## X. The first Saturday, and a folding table in the parking lot

Roz Winbush asked Deacon Fairchild for two things: a folding table and permission.

They set it up in the Beulah Grove parking lot on the first Saturday of the month, next to Oz's truck, with the driver's door open and the cracked empty frame sitting on the dash where people could see it. No sermon. No seminar. Nobody selling anything. On the table: a case of ten bankers boxes the trustee board bought for about thirty dollars, a fistful of permanent markers, a legal pad, Roz's green Charles Chips tin propped open, and a piece of poster board in a fourteen-year-old trombone player's handwriting that said:

**WHAT'S IN THE UNIT — AND WHAT DAY DOES IT SELL?**

**IF YOU CAN'T BUY IT AGAIN, IT RIDES UP FRONT WITH YOU.**

Twenty-three people sat down that first Saturday.

**Nine** of them had a storage unit that day. **Four** of those nine could not tell you their own balance. **Two** did not know the rate had gone up since they moved in. **One** woman, sixty-nine years old, had been paying **$148 a month for six years** — she did that multiplication out loud with Roz's pen in her hand, wrote **$10,656** on the legal pad, looked at it, and said *"on a bedroom set."* She went and got her one box the following Tuesday, gave notice, let the rest of it go, and cried in that parking lot with her hands on the hood of a truck — and every person there understood that what she was crying about was not furniture.

**One man, seventy-one**, found out his unit was scheduled for sale **in eleven days**, and that the number that stopped it was **$214.** The trustee board voted that $214 in ten minutes, because for once in this entire series the number was small and the clock was still running — *"and that is the whole reason for the table,"* Oz says. *"Everything else I ever collect for is four thousand dollars and too late. This one was two hundred and fourteen dollars and eleven days."*

**Three people** changed the address and phone number on their storage account right there in the parking lot, on their phones, in under two minutes each.

**A thirty-four-year-old father** put his kids' birth certificates and his own Social Security card into a bankers box, wrote HOUSE PAPERS on the side in marker, and put it on the front seat of his car instead of back in the unit.

And a woman about Roz's age sat down, didn't say anything for a while, and finally said she'd had a unit for four years and had never told her husband.

It happens the first Saturday of every month now. They call it **THE ONE BOX.** Roz Winbush sits at the end of the table with the green tin open in front of her, *"because women my age come over apologizing, and you can't apologize to a cookie tin."* Oz Fairchild stands by his truck with the door open and tells the 2019 story, all of it, including the lie about the quilt, to anybody who slows down — and he has stopped calling it a testimony. He calls it *"the receipt."*

*"Four years I had that frame on my dash and told everybody it was sentimental,"* he says. *"It is not sentimental. It's a receipt. It's what twenty dollars buys you at a swap meet after the other thing is gone. And I'm through being the deacon who kept a receipt like that to himself, because Sister Winbush's mother's pictures are in five houses today, and my mother's are in none."*

**The Plug AI is connected** — connected to the retired furniture delivery man with an empty cracked picture frame on the dashboard of his truck, who carried other people's whole lives up three flights of stairs for twenty-nine years, who has known exactly how this feels since 2019, who never once said it out loud until it happened to somebody else's mother, and who is now the reason a seventy-one-year-old man's eleven days did not run out.

**The plug is in.**
