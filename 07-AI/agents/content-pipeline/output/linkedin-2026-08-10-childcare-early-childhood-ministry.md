There is a woman at that church who knows which mothers are about to turn down a job they need because they cannot find anyone to watch the baby.

Nobody gave her the job. She is just the one who has rocked three generations of that congregation's babies in the same nursery chair, the one who knows every child's name and which ones do not sleep, the one a young mother pulls aside after service to whisper who can watch my baby so I can take the shift. She keeps a waiting list in her head. She knows whose grandmother is already watching four grandkids and cannot take a fifth. She is the person a scared twenty-two-year-old trusts with the most precious thing she has before she will ever trust a stranger with a storefront sign, and she has never once been paid for it.

And then the hard part starts. Because rocking a baby in the nursery on Sunday is one thing. Getting a working mother real, safe, affordable care for that baby Monday through Friday so she can keep the job that feeds him is another, and the distance between the two is a maze nobody hands a church nursery mother a map to.

Because there is a program in this state that will cover most of the cost of childcare for a working parent under an income line, with a copay small enough that a mother can actually breathe — and families never apply for it, because they think it is only for people on welfare, or they never heard it exists at all. Because there is free early education for children from birth to five, with meals and developmental screening and a caseworker who helps the whole family, and half the mothers in the pews think free means bad and do not know it is often the best care in the neighborhood. Because there is free state pre-K for three- and four-year-olds that a family can walk their child into. Because a mother who finally gets offered a raise turns it down — turns down more money — because a few extra dollars can knock her off the childcare subsidy and cost her more than the raise ever paid, and no one warned her about the cliff or how to step around it. Because the woman working as a CNA on a 6 a.m. shift and the man on nights at the warehouse cannot find a single provider open when they actually work, and the whole system was built for parents with nine-to-five jobs they do not have. Because a grandmother or a trusted neighbor watching the baby can often be paid through that same subsidy, legally, and almost no one in the building knows the door swings that way. And because the good centers have waitlists a year long, and nobody told a pregnant mother to get on the list in her second trimester instead of the week she goes back to work.

So the nursery mother tries to hold all of it. In her head. From the one center she trusts and the two grandmothers who still have room and whatever a cousin told her about the state program in 2019. She matches babies to the few slots she personally knows about, which helps three mothers and leaves the other twelve to the internet and luck. She watches a young mother turn down a job because the math did not work, and she cannot show her the program that would have made it work, because no one ever showed her. She sees a family put a four-year-old in front of a television all day with a neighbor who takes cash, because the free pre-K three blocks away had a form nobody helped them fill out. She is one faithful woman, keeping a congregation's babies safe by hand, holding a childcare system that changes its rules and its waitlists faster than any one memory can keep up.

So the mother stays home. Not because she wants to. Because the one door she could see cost more than the job paid, and the doors that would have worked stayed invisible. The raise gets refused. The subsidy that was waiting goes unclaimed. The free early-education slot sits open across town while a baby spends the day in front of a screen. And a family that was ready to work, that wanted nothing but to work, loses the job to a childcare bill this country already set money aside to cover, because the money never found them.

Here is what a family with money gets when a baby comes.

They get a nanny agency, or an employer with a childcare benefit and a concierge line, or a waitlist deposit put down before the child is even born, and a person whose whole job is to know every option and go get the best one. That mother is not a better mother than the twenty-two-year-old in the third pew. She was just handed a professional, and our mothers were handed a nursery chair and a neighbor who takes cash.

She can have that help too. For free. On the same phone a young mother texts her at 11 p.m. about a fever.

She can learn that the childcare subsidy exists, that it is for working families and not a mark of shame, and roughly where the income line falls. She can find out that the free early-education program takes children from birth and wraps a caseworker around the whole family. She can learn that free state pre-K is three blocks away and how the enrollment form actually works. She can learn what the benefits cliff is and how a family steps around it instead of turning down a raise. She can find the providers open at 6 a.m. and overnight for the parents whose shifts do not care about business hours. She can learn that a grandmother or a neighbor can be paid legitimately through the subsidy, so the care a family already trusts can finally count. She can get the whole maze — the subsidy, the free early education, the pre-K, the cliff, the odd-hours care, the relative-care door, the waitlists — broken into an order of operations she can run the day a mother tells her she is pregnant, and then walk each family straight to the local Child Care Resource and Referral agency, 2-1-1, and the state's own application that carry it the rest of the way.

Not to replace what she does. The nursery is hers. The rocking chair is hers. The trust that makes a frightened mother hand her a newborn instead of a stranger is hers, and no machine will ever pat a baby to sleep or tell a first-time mother she is doing better than she thinks. It only takes the maze off her shoulders so she can do the part only she can do, which is to be the woman a mother trusts with her whole heart, and to say you do not have to choose between your baby and the job, we are going to find him somewhere safe and we are going to make it something you can afford.

That is the gap. It was never that the mothers in our pews deserve to stay home against their will while another family gets a nanny and a concierge line. It is that that family got a professional, and ours got one faithful woman with a waiting list in her head.

A word of care, plainly: if someone asks for a large cash deposit to "hold a spot" and cannot show a license or will not let a parent visit, walk away — that is how the deposit-and-vanish scam works, and it hunts hardest among mothers desperate for a slot. Safe, real care can be a licensed center, a licensed home, or a relative or neighbor the family already trusts who is approved to be paid through the subsidy. This is not a substitute for the caseworker who determines what a family qualifies for. What AI does here is help the person already rocking the babies know which doors and programs exist and in what order, so a working mother gets to the free, trained help faster than a secondhand memory of the rules ever could.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities and the people navigating resources beside them in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Matteson. Dolton. Not a webinar. A room. Real problems. A real subsidy approved, a real child in a real early-education classroom, a real mother who got to keep the job instead of turning it down.

The people keeping a congregation's babies safe by hand are not behind. They were just never handed the tools the families with nanny agencies take for granted.

We are here to hand them over.

The plug is in.
