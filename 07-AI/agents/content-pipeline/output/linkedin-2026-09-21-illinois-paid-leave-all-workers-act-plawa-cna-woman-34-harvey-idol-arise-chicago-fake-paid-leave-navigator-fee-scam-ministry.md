**Key legal and program facts:**

- **Who is covered under PLAWA:** The Illinois Paid Leave for All Workers Act (820 ILCS 192) took effect January 1, 2024. It applies to nearly all employees in Illinois, including part-time, per-diem, seasonal, and variable-hours workers. The law explicitly covers workers who do not have a set weekly schedule. Jasmine — a per-diem CNA averaging twenty-six hours per week — is covered. Her supervisor's claim that per-diem employees "don't qualify" is incorrect and constitutes a violation of the Act.

- **The 40-hour entitlement:** Under PLAWA, employees accrue one hour of paid leave for every forty hours worked, up to a maximum of forty hours per year. Employees may carry over up to forty hours of accrued leave to the next twelve-month period. For a per-diem worker averaging twenty-six hours per week, this means approximately thirty-four hours of accrued paid leave after a full year — more than enough to cover an eight-hour absence.

- **The "any reason" standard:** PLAWA leave may be used for any reason. The worker does not need to provide a medical certification, a doctor's note, or an explanation. A worker may simply request to use accrued leave. The employer may require up to seven calendar days' notice when the need for leave is foreseeable; when it is not foreseeable (a child's sudden illness), notice as soon as practicable is sufficient. A 6:15 a.m. call before a shift is legally sufficient notice for an unforeseeable illness.

- **Prohibited employer responses:** An employer may not deny a worker's use of PLAWA leave, count PLAWA leave usage as an unexcused absence, retaliate against a worker for requesting or using leave, or use attendance policies to penalize PLAWA leave. The attendance reminder Jasmine received three days after her PLAWA leave request is a textbook retaliation marker. Under the Act, retaliatory action — including disciplinary documentation, schedule reduction, or demotion — is an independent violation subject to separate IDOL penalties.

- **Remedies available through IDOL:** If IDOL finds a violation, it may order the employer to pay the worker the value of the denied leave (Jasmine's $148) plus damages equal to three times the amount of the leave denied, plus any attorney's fees. For Jasmine, a successful complaint could result in recovery of $148 + $444 in damages = $592, plus the removal of the unexcused absence from her record.

- **The employer's obligation to inform workers:** PLAWA requires employers to provide written notice to employees of their rights under the Act, including posting in the workplace. An employer who has failed to post the required PLAWA notice is in violation regardless of whether it also denied leave. Nursing homes and long-term care facilities that employ large numbers of per-diem CNAs — a workforce that is predominantly Black and Latina women in southern Cook County — have a particular compliance obligation and a documented history of under-informing variable-hours staff of their leave rights.

- **Coordination with FMLA, sick leave, and other laws:** PLAWA leave is separate from federal FMLA leave (which applies only to employers with fifty or more employees for qualifying serious health conditions). PLAWA's coverage is broader — it applies to smaller employers and any reason. In Cook County, the Cook County Earned Sick Leave Ordinance provides additional protections. Workers may have rights under multiple laws simultaneously; Arise Chicago can help identify which applies.

- **Documentation advice:** Workers who believe their PLAWA rights have been violated should document: the date and time of the leave request, the supervisor's verbal response and exact language, the date the leave was denied or recorded as unexcused, any written attendance documentation received, and any change in scheduling or hours following the request. Text messages, payroll records, and scheduling records are all relevant. IDOL investigators will request this documentation from the employer; having your own records strengthens the complaint.

**Here is what the Illinois Paid Leave for All Workers Act actually says — and what your employer may not have told you:**

**1. You are almost certainly covered — including if you are part-time, per-diem, or variable-hours.**

The PLAWA covers nearly all employees in Illinois, regardless of hours worked or schedule type. The only exceptions are employees covered by a collective bargaining agreement with different leave terms, certain employees of the federal government, and a small category of qualifying seasonal workers. A per-diem CNA averaging twenty-six hours per week at a private nursing home is covered. "Per-diem employees don't qualify" is not a statement of the law. It is either a misunderstanding of the law or a deliberate misrepresentation of it.

**2. You earn paid leave as you work — it is not a gift the employer grants.**

For every forty hours you work, you earn one hour of paid leave. After one full year, a worker averaging twenty-six hours per week has accrued approximately thirty-four hours. The leave belongs to the worker. The employer is required to track accrual and make the balance available. If your employer has not tracked your accrual or has not told you what your balance is, that is a violation.

**3. You do not need to explain why you need the leave.**

PLAWA leave can be used for any reason. You do not need to say your child is sick, that you have a medical appointment, or that you are dealing with a family matter. You need only request use of your accrued leave. For unforeseeable needs — a child's sudden fever, an emergency — you must give notice "as soon as practicable." A 6:15 a.m. call before your shift starts is legally sufficient.

**4. Denying the leave is a violation. Marking it unexcused is a violation. The attendance letter may be retaliation.**

An employer who denies a valid PLAWA request, records the resulting absence as unexcused, or issues attendance discipline for PLAWA leave usage has committed multiple violations. IDOL can investigate each violation separately and order back pay plus damages for each. Retaliation — any adverse action taken because a worker requested or used PLAWA leave — is an independent violation that IDOL takes seriously.

**5. Filing an IDOL complaint is free and does not require an attorney or a paid service.**

Go to labor.illinois.gov. Select the PLAWA complaint form. Describe what happened — what you asked for, what the supervisor said, what was taken from your paycheck, what documentation you received. IDOL will assign an investigator, contact the employer, and conduct an inquiry. If the employer violated the Act, IDOL can order back pay plus triple damages plus any attorney fees you incurred. You do not need a paid claim service, a navigator, or a form preparation company to submit this. The description of what happened is the complaint. Only you can write that description. No one can do it better by charging you $149.

**6. Free help is available if you want it.**

Arise Chicago (arisechicago.org / 312-243-2490) helps workers file PLAWA and other labor complaints at no cost and runs Know Your Rights workshops in South Suburban Cook County. Prairie State Legal Services (prairiestate.org / 800-942-0177) provides free civil legal representation for income-eligible workers in Dolton, Harvey, Calumet City, and across the south suburbs. Illinois Legal Aid Online (illinoislegalaid.org) has plain-language guides and self-help tools for every step of the process.
