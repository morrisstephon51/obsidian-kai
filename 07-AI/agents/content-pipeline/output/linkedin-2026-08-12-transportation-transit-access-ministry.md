There is a woman in that church who drives everybody.

You know the one. When Mother Green can't make it to Sunday service, it is her car in the driveway at nine in the morning. When Sister Ruth has dialysis three times a week and no way to get there, it is her behind the wheel, every time, without being asked twice. When the young mother's stroller won't fold down small enough for the bus, she is the one who says just call me, I'll come get you. She runs the pantry boxes out to the shut-ins. She works as a home health aide, so her whole living is built on her being able to move — three clients, four clients a day, spread out across towns that were never meant to connect, and she gets to every one of them. If that church has wheels, it is her.

And then one Tuesday morning her car makes a sound it has never made before, and it dies in her own driveway, and it does not come back.

The transmission. Three thousand dollars she does not have and cannot get. And in one morning the woman who moved the entire congregation around cannot move herself.

Understand what that actually does to a life like hers, because it is not one problem. It is every problem at once. Her clients are in one town and she is in another and there is no train that runs out here and the bus takes two hours and three transfers and does not even run late enough to bring her home when her shift ends. So she starts being late. Then she starts missing shifts. Then her hours get cut, because a home health agency cannot send an aide who cannot reliably arrive, and she knows it, and it shames her, because being reliable was the one thing she was always sure of. She can't get her own mother to dialysis anymore. She can't get to the store without it being an all-day expedition. The food pantry runs go to somebody else now. And the woman whose entire identity was being the one you could count on to show up is sitting in a house she cannot leave, watching the life she built on movement quietly come apart, over one thing that nobody in a two-car household ever has to think about.

Understand her clearly. She is not disorganized. She is not helpless. She is not making excuses and she is not looking for a handout. She is one of the most reliable, hardest-working human beings that congregation has ever had, and she solved the transportation problem the only way anybody ever told her to solve it, which was to have a car and keep it running on a home health aide's wages until the day it finally asked for more than she had. The problem was never her drive. The problem is that she lives in a county whose entire map was drawn for people who own a car that never breaks, and hers just did.

And so she has quietly decided that this is just her situation now. That folks like her are supposed to figure out a ride or do without. That there is no help for something as ordinary as not being able to get somewhere, and that asking would just make her the burden she has spent her whole life refusing to be. So she does not ask. And the world gets smaller.

Here is the thing nobody has ever laid out plainly for her: getting where you need to go, out here, in a place built without you in mind, is not a personal failing you are supposed to muscle through alone. It is a system — a confusing, scattered, badly-explained system — and there are real doors in it that nobody ever walked her to.

Because Pace runs more than the bus she already knows about — it runs Dial-a-Ride and door-to-door service into the very corners of these suburbs where the fixed route never goes, and paratransit for people who cannot use a regular bus, and she has spent years thinking the bus was her only option because no one ever told her the rest existed. Because the RTA has a travel information line where a real person will help her plan an actual trip from her door to her Tuesday client, and reduced-fare and ride-free programs for seniors and for people with disabilities that could cut what this costs down to almost nothing — and her mother may qualify right now. Because if she or her mother is on Medicaid, there is a benefit almost nobody is ever told about, a ride to covered medical appointments, arranged ahead of time, often at no cost, so that getting to dialysis is not supposed to depend on whose car is running that week. Because her own township — the township office ten minutes from her house — very likely runs a dial-a-ride van for seniors and for folks who cannot drive, and it has been sitting there the whole time. Because 211, three digits on the phone in her pocket, can point her to a program nearby that helps with a bus pass or even a car repair. And because — hear this part, because it is built for exactly her kind of desperation — the moment a woman starts searching "free car" and "help fixing my car" and "transportation grant," the scammers come running: the ones who promise a free car if she just pays a processing fee, the fake government grant that only needs her bank account first, the program that helps nobody and charges everybody. A real ride program does not make you pay a fee up front to get in the door.

So she sits in a house she cannot leave and believes the smallness is permanent. She is not stranded because she failed. She is one of the most dependable people in that building. She is stranded because getting around out here turned out to have a whole set of doors — a phone line, an application, a benefit on a card she already carries, a van from her own township — and nobody ever walked her to a single one of them.

Here is the thing about the family across town. When their car needs work, there is a second car in the driveway. There is a teenager with a license who can run to the store. There is a train station four blocks away that runs all day. And when something like the Medicaid ride benefit or the Pace paratransit application comes up, there is somebody in that house — a spouse, a grown kid, a neighbor who works in an office — who just sits down and figures out the form and makes the call and reads the fine print, as a matter of course. Getting around was never a locked door for that family. It was a second set of keys and a person who handles the paperwork. Their mother is not more capable than the woman who drives our whole church. She just landed in a house that already had the ride and the helper. Nobody handed ours either one.

She can have that second thing. Starting this week. Not a second car in the driveway — but the patient helper the other family always effectively had, the one who reads the confusing form and explains the benefit and figures out the route.

Because this is where a tool like AI actually meets a woman like her, quietly, in the background, doing the part her life never came with. It can sit with her and read the Pace paratransit application line by line and tell her in plain words what each question is really asking. It can explain what "non-emergency medical transportation" even means and how you arrange it, so getting her mother to dialysis stops being a crisis every single week. It can take her three clients and her home address and help her puzzle out which bus and which Pace route actually connect, and roughly when she would need to leave. It can draft the exact questions to ask when she finally calls her township office, so she is not fumbling and does not feel foolish. It is the patient someone who does the reading and the sorting and the figuring — the thing the family across town always had a person in the house to do.

Not to drive the bus. Let that be plain. The machine does not drive her anywhere. It does not show up at her clients' doors. It does not bathe anybody or hold her mother's hand in the dialysis chair or do one minute of the hard, human, holy work she does all day. It does not replace her reliability or her hustle or her heart. It is not a car and it is not a substitute for one. It is only the helper that finds her the ride — the patient translator for a system that was written to keep her out — so that she can go back to being exactly who she already is, the one you can count on to show up.

That is the gap. It was never that the woman who drives our whole church is less capable than the woman across town. It is that one of them was handed a second set of keys and a person at the kitchen table who handles the forms — and the other was handed a home health aide's paycheck, a county with no train, and a job that quietly assumed everybody owns a car that never dies.

A word of care, plainly, and I will say the important parts twice. This is general navigation, not an endorsement of any one transit agency or program or company over another, and it is not legal or financial or medical advice. AI tools can be confidently wrong, so never trust a route, a schedule, or an eligibility rule it gives you without confirming it with the actual agency — Pace, the RTA, your Medicaid plan, your township. Never put private information into a public AI tool: not your Social Security number, not your Medicaid ID, not your bank details. And hear this one twice, because it is built for people in exactly this spot: a real benefit or public ride program does not charge you a fee up front to enroll, so anyone promising a "free car" or a "transportation grant" who first needs a processing fee or your bank account is running a scam, and the harder a person is struggling to get where they need to go, the more of them will come. Route the real help to real, trusted places: Pace and the RTA for buses, Dial-a-Ride, paratransit, and reduced-fare or ride-free programs; the number on your Medicaid card to ask about rides to medical appointments; your local township office, such as Rich Township, Bloom Township, or Thornton Township, to ask about a dial-a-ride van; and 211 or 211.org to find transportation help, a bus pass, or a car-repair program near you.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Dolton. Robbins. Chicago Heights. Matteson. South Holland. The towns the trains forgot. Not a webinar. A room. Real people. A real woman who has driven that whole church for years, taught how to make the same tool the other family always had do the reading and the figuring for her — so the woman everybody counts on can get counted on again.

The most dependable people in our churches are not stuck because they gave up. They are stuck because the map was never drawn with them on it.

We are here to help them find the road anyway.

The plug is in.
