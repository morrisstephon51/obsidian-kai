There is a man in that church who paid off his house two years ago, framed the last mortgage statement, and hung it in the hallway like a diploma. He is sixty-six. And last Tuesday he almost signed a twenty-five-year contract on his own front porch because a young man in a bright vest told him the government was buying his neighborhood solar panels, for free, but only if he signed before the crew left the block.

You know him. He drove a CTA bus for thirty years, or he pulled shifts at the old steel mill until it closed, or he carried mail through every kind of Chicago weather, or he mopped the floors and fixed the boilers at the grade school until his knees gave out. He is retired now, on Social Security and a small pension, and he counts. He knows what the light bill was in June and what it was in July, and he has watched it climb every summer while the check stays the same. He keeps the thermostat higher than he'd like and sits in the dark den to save a few dollars, and he does the math a careful man does on a fixed income — where can I cut, what can I do without, how do I keep this house I finally own free and clear so my kids inherit it clean.

And here is the part that would knock the wind out of him if somebody said it plainly: there really is a way to put solar on that roof and cut that bill, a real one, run by the state, built for a man in exactly his shoes — and nobody ever told him it existed. The people who found him first were not from the state. They were a sales crew working the block with a tablet and a script, and the "free" they were selling was a loan, or a lease, dressed up in a word he wanted to believe.

So when the vest on his porch says "the government is paying for this, sir, it's free, you've been selected, but I need a signature tonight," it doesn't sound like a trap. It sounds like the first good news the light bill has given him in years.

So there it is — the squeeze. A rising bill, a fixed check, a paid-off house he is proud of, and a stranger offering to make the bill disappear if he just signs right now. And that is the exact moment they get him: the contract he doesn't have time to read, the "loan" he thinks is a gift, the lien that quietly attaches to the house behind the word free.

Because a man watching his one bill he can't control climb every year, on money that doesn't grow, who is proud of the house and wants to protect it — that is a man under a squeeze, and a squeeze is what these crews sell to. Say it plainly, and I'll say it again before I'm done: the danger is not that he is greedy, or gullible, or too country to understand solar. The danger is that he has a real, honest need and a real, honest door he was never shown — so he is left to the first person who knocks, and that person's whole job is to get a signature before he can ask a single question. He doesn't know the state has a program with his name on it. He doesn't know a free watchdog will read the contract for him. He doesn't know that ComEd doesn't knock on doors, that "free" is almost always a loan, and that a signature can put a lien on the very house he means to leave his children. None of that is a failing in him. It's a stack of pages nobody put in his hands.

He opened his own front door in good faith. The shame in this story belongs to the vested crew, the fly-by-night installer, and the lien — never to him.

So here are the doors almost no one names for him plainly, none ranked, all worth a look. First and biggest, because it surprises people: Illinois Solar for All — a real state program, at illinoissolarforall.com, that brings solar to income-eligible homeowners with no upfront cost and a written promise that whatever he pays can never be more than half the value of the power the panels make, so by design he cannot lose money on it — and it works only through approved vendors the program vouches for. There's a sister program, Illinois Shines, at illinoisshines.com, with its own approved-vendor list and a consumer-protection team. There is the Citizens Utility Board — CUB, at citizensutilityboard.org — a free nonprofit watchdog that has fought for Illinois utility customers for decades and will look over a solar quote or contract for him free, before he signs a thing. There is the Illinois Commerce Commission for utility questions and complaints. There is ComEd's real net-metering program — real, worth understanding, and never sold by someone knocking on his door.
