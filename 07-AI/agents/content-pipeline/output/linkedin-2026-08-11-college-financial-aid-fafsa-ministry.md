There is a man at that church whose daughter is away at college right now, and every parent of a graduating senior finds a reason to stand next to him after service.

Nobody in his family had ever done it. He never went. He worked with his hands his whole life, good work, honest work, and he was proud of it, but he carried a quiet certainty that college was a thing that happened to other families — families with a savings account for it, a cousin who had gone, somebody at the dinner table who knew the words. He had heard the numbers. Forty, fifty thousand a year. He did the math the way you do math on a thing you have already decided you cannot have, and he stopped. Then his daughter brought home a form and a teacher's note about something called financial aid, and he almost let it sit on the counter, because forms like that had never once come back with good news for a man like him, and he filled it out, and it was free to fill out, and a grant came back that did not have to be paid back, and a state grant on top of it, and eighteen months later he drove her to a dorm and carried her boxes up three flights and sat in his truck in the parking lot afterward and cried. Now a father in the next pew, or a mother whose smart kid has already talked herself out of applying, finds him after service and asks, quiet, like it is a secret they are ashamed to need: how did your girl get to go?

And then the hard part starts. Because getting your own child through that door once is one thing. Holding the whole college-aid maze in your head for every family that is sure the door is bolted shut is another, and nobody hands a working man a map to it.

Because the FAFSA is free — the word free is the first word in its name, and there are websites that charge families to fill out a form the government gives away, and the parent who does not know that either pays a stranger or, worse, never files at all and leaves every dollar of aid on the table. Because the Pell grant is real money that is never, ever paid back, and far more families qualify than ever apply, and the child who assumes her family makes "too much" or "too little" never finds out where she actually falls. Because the state has its own grant — free money, through the Illinois student aid commission — but the funds are limited and run out, so the family that files in the spring gets it and the family that waits until summer, when the money is gone, does not, and nobody told them the calendar was the whole game. Because you do not need perfect grades or a college fund — aid is built on need, and starting at the community college for two years and transferring is not a lesser path, it is a cheaper, smarter, fully respected one that families talk each other out of because they think it does not count. Because a student on free or reduced lunch can get the college application fees waived and the test fees waived, and families pay them anyway or apply to fewer schools to save money they never had to spend. Because the dual-credit class the high school offers is free college credit banked before the kid ever leaves, and half the families do not know to ask for it. Because federal loans, if loans are needed at all, come first and come with protections, and the private loan and the for-profit "get your degree fast" school with the aggressive recruiter are how a family ends up with debt and a credential that does not open the door it promised. Because the scholarship offer that asks for a fee, or "guarantees" you money, or needs your bank account to "hold your award," is a scam, every time, and the family reaching hardest is the one most likely to get reached by it. Because a student without a Social Security number is not automatically shut out in Illinois — there is a separate application that may let her apply for state aid — and a family that assumes the door is closed never asks the one question that would open it. And because underneath all of it is one overworked counselor responsible for three, four hundred seniors, which is the real reason nobody ever sat down with this family and walked them through — not because the family did not care enough, but because there was never a person whose job it was to care about their kid in particular.

So the father tries to hold all of it. In his head. From the one time he got his own daughter through, two years ago, when a couple of the deadlines were on different days and one of the grants went by a different name. He tells a mother the wrong filing month and she misses the state money by weeks. He forgets to mention the fee waiver and a family narrows their kid's whole list down to two schools to save two hundred dollars. He hears about a scholarship and cannot remember if it was the real one or the one that wanted a fee. He is one man, holding the door for other people's children out of his own memory of a system that moves its deadlines and renames its money faster than anyone working a job and raising kids can track.

So the aid the government set aside for a family goes to the family that filed on time instead. The state grant runs dry in April and the kid who would have gotten it never knows it existed. The bright student talks herself out of applying because nobody did the math with her and showed her the real number after aid, which is almost never the sticker price. The fee waiver goes unused and the application list shrinks. The family without the savings account signs up for the for-profit school whose recruiter called back the fastest, and the debt outlives the degree. And the undocumented student, certain she is not allowed, never fills out the one form that might have said otherwise.

Here is the thing about a family with resources: the door was always held open for their kid. They hired someone — a college counselor, an admissions coach, a financial-aid adviser — for a few thousand dollars, and that person knew every deadline, filed the FAFSA flawlessly, found the scholarships, knew which grants ran out and filed early to catch them, and walked the kid from the fall of junior year to the day the boxes went up the dorm stairs. Getting in and getting it paid for was not a maze that child solved alone at a kitchen table, because there was always a professional standing beside her. That family's kid is not smarter than the daughter this father got through by himself. The door was just never locked for them the way it was locked for him.

He can have that adviser too. For free. On the same form he almost left sitting on the counter two years ago.

He can find out the real cost of a school after aid and stop a family from crossing off a college over a sticker price nobody actually pays. He can point a mother to the free FAFSA instead of a site that would charge her. He can learn which state money runs out and get a family to file early enough to catch it. He can flag the Pell grant so a family that assumed it earned too much finds out it qualifies. He can make sure the fee waivers get claimed so a kid can apply to six schools instead of two. He can tell a family which loans are safe and steer them clear of the for-profit recruiter and the "guaranteed scholarship" fee. He can help the undocumented student find the one application that might be open to her. He can have the whole maze — the free form, the Pell, the state grant and its deadline, the fee waivers, the dual credit, the safe order of loans — broken into an order of operations a family can start this month, and then walk each of them straight to the school counselor, the college's financial-aid office, the federal student-aid line, and the state commission that carry it the rest of the way.

Not to replace what he does. The question whispered after service is his. The reason a nervous mother trusts a truck-driving deacon enough to admit her kid has already given up is his, and no machine will ever have sat in a parking lot crying because its own child made it up three flights of dorm stairs. It only takes the maze off his shoulders so he can do the part only he can do, which is to look a tired father in the eye and say the thing he needed somebody to say to him: your kid is not less than theirs, this was never about smart or not smart, this door has your child's name on it too, and we are going to find the handle together.

That is the gap. It was never that the families in our pews want it less or love their children less than the family for whom the door was always open. It is that that family had someone paid to walk their kid through, and ours got one faithful father holding the door with the memory of how he got his own girl through it once, two years ago, when a couple of the deadlines were on different days.

A word of care, plainly: the FAFSA is free — the first word of its name is "Free" — so any website charging you to fill it out is one to close, and any scholarship that asks for a fee, promises "guaranteed" money, or wants your bank login is a scam every time, because real aid never charges you to receive it. Be slow with any for-profit college whose recruiter is calling you back within the hour and promising a fast degree — compare the cost and the graduation rate against your community college first, and take out federal aid before any private loan. And if your student has no Social Security number, do not assume the door is closed — in Illinois there is a separate application that may let her apply for state aid, and the counselor or the state commission can tell you. This is general navigation, not financial-aid, legal, immigration, admissions, or tax advice — helping someone learn that a grant exists is not the same as filing their FAFSA or deciding what they qualify for — so always route the actual application, the current rules, and the eligibility itself to the high school counselor or college-and-career center, to the college's own financial-aid office, to the free FAFSA at studentaid.gov (or the Federal Student Aid Information Center at 1-800-433-3243), and to the Illinois Student Assistance Commission at isac.org for the state MAP grant. The local community college's admissions office and 2-1-1 can point families to more help nearby.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities and the people navigating resources beside them in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Matteson. Dolton. Not a webinar. A room. Real problems. A real family that stopped believing college was for other people's kids, a real grant that got claimed before it ran out instead of expiring unused, a real first-generation student carrying her own boxes up the stairs.

The people holding the door open for other families' children by hand are not behind. They were just never handed the tools the resourced never even have to reach for.

We are here to hand them over.
