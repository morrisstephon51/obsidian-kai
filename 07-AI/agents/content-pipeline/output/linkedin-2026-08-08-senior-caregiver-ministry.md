There is a woman at that church who knows which elders live alone, which ones stopped driving, and which ones will not tell anybody when something is wrong.

Nobody put her in charge of it. She just started calling. One widow on Sunday afternoons, then two, then a list she keeps in a spiral notebook by the phone. She is the one who notices when Mother So-and-So misses two services in a row. She is the one who drives to the pharmacy, who sits at the kitchen table and reads the mail out loud, who catches that the milk in the refrigerator went bad a week ago and understands what that actually means.

And then the hard part starts. Because getting old in this country is not just a body slowing down. It is a maze of paperwork that never stops moving, and most families walk into it blind on the worst day of their lives.

There is a Medicare enrollment window that opens once a year and closes, and choosing wrong in it can cost a family thousands they will never get back. There is a drug plan that covered the medication all year and then hits a gap, and suddenly the pill that keeps a man's heart steady costs more than his Social Security check. There is a Medicaid long-term-care application thicker than a phone book, with a lookback period and a spend-down rule that no grieving daughter should have to decode alone. There are in-home care hours that get quietly cut because a renewal form came in the mail and nobody knew it was the one that mattered. And underneath all of it is a caregiver, usually a daughter, sometimes a granddaughter, who is holding down a job and raising her own kids and slowly going under.

So the woman with the notebook tries to hold all of it. For a dozen families at once. She reads the letters. She keeps the appointments in her head. She drives across town. And she is one person, with her own aging body and her own family, and a whole congregation of elders who all outlived the people who used to help them.

So something slips. An enrollment window closes and a widow is locked into a plan that does not cover her cancer doctor for a year. A drug plan gap goes unnoticed until a man is cutting his pills in half. A Medicaid application sits half-finished on a table because no one could face page forty. A daughter finally breaks and puts her mother somewhere neither of them wanted, because the home-care hours ran out and nobody ever told her there was another option.

Here is what a family with money gets when a parent starts to fail.

They get a geriatric care manager who maps the whole thing. They get an elder-law attorney who structures the Medicaid application so a lifetime of savings is not wiped out by a paperwork mistake. They get a benefits counselor who reads every notice the week it comes and never misses a window. They get a team paid to keep an old man in his own home, with his own coffee cup, near the people who love him, for as long as it is safely possible. That team is not more devoted than she is. It is just resourced.

She can have that help too. For free. On the same phone the families call her on.

She can hold up a Medicare notice and get it explained in plain language, with the window and the deadline made unmistakable. She can describe a person's medications and income and get back a clear read on whether they qualify for Extra Help, for a Medicare Savings Program, for the drug-cost relief that is sitting there unclaimed. She can get the Medicaid long-term-care maze broken into an order of operations, which document first, which one unlocks the next, what the lookback actually means for this family. She can have every renewal date and enrollment window held for every elder at once, so none of them slips past while she is sitting on somebody's porch. And she can ask what help exists for the daughter who is drowning, and get a real map of respite care and caregiver support instead of silence.

Not to replace what she does. The visits are hers. She is the one who notices the bad milk, the one who hears the loneliness under a "I'm fine, baby," the one who shows up on the porch when the paperwork is done. No machine has ever held an old woman's hand through a Tuesday afternoon. It just takes the maze off her shoulders so she can do the part only she can do, which is show up.

That is the gap. It was never that the elders in our pews deserve less dignity in their last years than the ones whose families could afford a care manager. It is that those families got handed a whole team, and ours got handed one woman with a spiral notebook and a heart that could not stand to let an elder disappear alone.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities and the people navigating resources beside them in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Matteson. Dolton. Not a webinar. A room. Real problems. A real elder's stack of Medicare letters on the table.

The people carrying our elders through the end of their lives by hand are not behind. They were just never handed the tools the funded agencies take for granted.

We are here to hand them over.

The plug is in.
