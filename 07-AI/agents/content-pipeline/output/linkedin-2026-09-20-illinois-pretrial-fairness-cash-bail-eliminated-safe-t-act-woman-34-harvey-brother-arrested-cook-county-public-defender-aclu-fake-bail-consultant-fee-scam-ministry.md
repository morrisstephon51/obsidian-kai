**Scam spine:** Within hours of an arrest in Cook County, family members sometimes receive calls from individuals identifying themselves as "bail bondsmen," "bail bond agents," "pretrial coordinators," or "licensed release consultants" — offering to expedite release in exchange for $200 to $600, often payable by Zelle, Cash App, or money order. These callers exploit a gap in public knowledge: most families in Illinois still operate on the mental model of "cash bail" — where posting money secures release — that governed the system for decades. What they do not know is that Illinois eliminated that system entirely. The Pretrial Fairness Act, which took effect September 18, 2023 as part of the SAFE-T Act, made Illinois the first state in the nation to abolish cash bail entirely. No bail bond company, coordinator, or consultant can collect money to secure a loved one's release — because money no longer determines whether someone is detained. That decision now belongs entirely to a judge. No private payment changes it. The $350 to a pretrial "coordinator" does nothing except take $350 from a family that was already frightened and already poor.

**Key legal facts:**
- **Illinois Pretrial Fairness Act (725 ILCS 5/110):** effective September 18, 2023; eliminated monetary bail in all Illinois criminal proceedings in all Illinois circuit courts; Illinois became the first state to abolish cash bail statewide
- **How detention now works:** after an arrest, the prosecutor may file a petition for pretrial detention; a judge conducts a detention hearing — typically within 24 to 48 hours; the judge may detain the person if they find clear and convincing evidence the person poses a specific, real threat to an identifiable person or the community, or presents a willful flight risk; for most misdemeanors, the state rarely seeks detention; if no petition for detention is filed or the petition is denied, the person is released, typically with conditions (check-ins, GPS monitoring, substance use evaluation) monitored by pretrial services
- **No money secures release:** under the new law, monetary bail — paying cash or a bond to a court to secure release — no longer exists in Illinois state court; there is no "bail amount" set at arrest; there is no cash payment to the court, a bondsman, or anyone else that gets someone out
- **Bail bond agents no longer operate in Illinois state courts:** the profession of bail bondsman — which existed to post bond money on behalf of defendants for a percentage fee — has no legal function in the Illinois state criminal justice system since September 18, 2023; any individual claiming to be a "bail bondsman" for an Illinois state court case after that date cannot perform what they are claiming to perform
- **Public Defender right:** the Sixth Amendment guarantees the right to appointed counsel for anyone facing incarceration who cannot afford private representation; in Cook County, the Public Defender's office is assigned at the first appearance; the process is handled by the court; there is no fee and no application; no private coordinator can "expedite" the assignment of a public defender or substitute for one
- **Pretrial services monitoring:** when a person is released under the new system, they are typically assigned to Illinois Pretrial Services — a court-supervised program that may require check-ins, electronic monitoring, and compliance with conditions set by the judge; this is not a private company and it does not charge the defendant or the family
- **Misdemeanor vs. felony:** under the Pretrial Fairness Act, for most low-level misdemeanors, detention petitions are rare; a person arrested for a Class A or Class B misdemeanor in Cook County is very likely to be released to pretrial services conditions without detention, often within 24–48 hours; a "coordinator" offering to speed this up by collecting a fee is describing work that either happens automatically through the court or cannot happen regardless of what anyone charges
- **Illinois AG authority:** persons soliciting fees for "bail services" or "pretrial release assistance" in connection with Illinois state court proceedings, where those services cannot legally be performed, may be subject to consumer fraud enforcement; report at illinoisattorneygeneral.gov or 1-800-243-0618

**Distinct from:**
- Federal court proceedings — the federal system (U.S. District Courts, federal charges) still uses monetary conditions of release in some cases; the Pretrial Fairness Act applies only to Illinois state courts; if a loved one is arrested on a federal charge, the system is different
- Other states' bail systems — Illinois is the only state to have abolished cash bail statewide; families who moved from other states may carry an older mental model that does not apply here
- Expungement / record sealing (prior posts) — those are court processes for clearing a conviction from a person's record after the case is resolved; pretrial detention is the period between arrest and case resolution
- Criminal record expungement mills (prior posts) — those scams target people seeking to clear past convictions; pretrial consultant scams target families in the immediate hours after an arrest, before any conviction exists

**Here is what changed — and what tens of thousands of Cook County families do not know:**

**1. Illinois abolished cash bail on September 18, 2023. It is completely gone.**

The Illinois Pretrial Fairness Act — part of the SAFE-T Act — made Illinois the first state in the nation to eliminate monetary bail entirely.

Under the old system: after arrest, a judge set a dollar amount as "bail"; if you could afford to pay it (or pay a bail bondsman ten percent of it), you went home; if you couldn't, you sat in jail waiting for your case to resolve. The same charge, on the same night, meant freedom for one family and incarceration for another based entirely on their bank accounts.

That system ended three years ago. Your ability to pay no longer determines whether your loved one comes home tonight. A judge decides — based on risk, not money.

No bail bondsman, no pretrial coordinator, no "release specialist" can collect money to change that outcome. The system they are describing — the one they are selling you — does not exist anymore.

**2. Here is how the new system actually works.**

After an arrest in Cook County, a person is processed and a first court appearance is scheduled — typically within 24 to 48 hours.

At that hearing, the prosecution may or may not file a petition asking the court to detain the person pretrial. For most misdemeanor charges — retail theft, most traffic offenses, disorderly conduct — the state rarely files for detention.

If no detention petition is filed, or if the judge denies it, the person is released — usually with conditions set by the court, which may include regular check-ins with Illinois Pretrial Services, electronic monitoring, no-contact orders, or other requirements. A pretrial services officer — a court employee, not a private company — supervises compliance.

If detention is ordered, it is because a judge found clear and convincing evidence that the person presents a specific danger to another person or a willful flight risk. That decision cannot be changed by a payment to a private coordinator.

In either direction, no private fee changes the outcome.

**3. There is no application to expedite. There is no paperwork to "submit." The public defender is free.**

The caller told Dominique she needed to pay to "submit the pretrial release application." There is no such application. The court process is initiated automatically upon arrest. Nothing is submitted by a private party.

The right to a public defender is guaranteed under the Sixth Amendment. In Cook County, the Public Defender's office is one of the largest in the country. When someone who cannot afford private counsel appears before a judge, the Public Defender is assigned at that hearing — at no cost to the family.

There is no fee to request the Public Defender. There is no form to fill out in advance. There is no process to accelerate by paying someone. Anyone claiming to provide this service is collecting money for nothing.

The Cook County Public Defender: publicdefender.info / (312) 603-0600.

**4. The call to Dominique followed a recognizable pattern. Know it.**

These calls often come within hours of an arrest — before the family has had time to research anything, before the shock has worn off, at the exact moment when fear is highest and judgment is lowest.

The caller knows the name of the person arrested. They know the charge. They know the facility. This information is available through public court filings and sometimes through data available to aggregators — or, in some cases, through informants. The caller's knowledge does not mean they are official. It does not mean they have access to anything. It means they have a name, a number, and a story that sounds plausible to someone who doesn't know the law has changed.

Verify before you pay anything. Call the Cook County Sheriff's Inmate Lookup at cookcountysheriff.org. Call CARPLS at 312-738-9200. Call the Cook County Public Defender at (312) 603-0600.

If anyone asks for money to secure someone's release from Cook County Jail after a state criminal arrest — hang up. Report to the Illinois Attorney General: 1-800-243-0618 / illinoisattorneygeneral.gov.
