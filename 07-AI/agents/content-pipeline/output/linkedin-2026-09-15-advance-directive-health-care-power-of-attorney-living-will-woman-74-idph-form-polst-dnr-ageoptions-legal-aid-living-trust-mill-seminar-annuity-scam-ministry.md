There is a woman in that church who has been to more hospital rooms than anyone in the building, and last month she sat in one that changed her mind about a stack of papers she'd been putting off for years.

You know her. Seventy-four years old, retired now after a lifetime as a nurse's aide — thirty-some years of turning patients, holding hands, wiping brows, standing quietly against the wall while doctors said the hard things to families who weren't ready. She still teaches the Sunday school class for the little ones, still drives herself to Bible study on Wednesday nights, still cooks for every repast the church puts on. She is not fragile. She is not sick. She is one of the strongest, clearest-headed people in that sanctuary, and everybody knows it.

What put her in that hospital room last month was another family from the congregation. Their mother — a woman she'd sung next to in the choir for twenty years — had a massive stroke, no warning, and ended up on a machine in the ICU, unable to speak for herself. And in the hallway outside that room, our nurse's aide watched something she had seen a hundred times from the working side of the badge and never once from the pew: three grown children standing in a huddle, screaming at each other. One said Mama would never want to be kept alive like this. One said taking her off the machine was as good as killing her. One just cried. And a young doctor stood there, patient and helpless, asking them to decide something their mother had never told them — because there was nothing written down, nowhere, and now the weight of it, the guilt of it, the *rupture* of it, was going to sit on that family forever, no matter which way they chose.

She drove home from that hospital and sat at her own kitchen table and said out loud, to an empty house: *Not my children. Not like that.* And here is the part that would let her — and half that congregation — breathe if somebody said it plainly: in Illinois, she can settle this for her family, right now, while she is healthy and clear and fully herself, on the state's own forms, for free, without a lawyer and without a dime to anybody. She can name the one person who will speak for her if she ever can't — a health care power of attorney. She can write down, in her own words, what she does and does not want done — a living will. She can keep the decision where it belongs: between her, the people she loves, her doctor, and her God — and out of a hospital hallway. Nobody handed her those doors. The only people who ever tried to "help" her with this showed up with a free dinner and a briefcase.

Because a strong, responsible seventy-four-year-old woman who has finally decided to "get her affairs in order" is exactly who a certain kind of salesman is built to find. So when the flyer comes for the "free estate-planning dinner at the nice restaurant off the interstate," and the warm man in the good suit stands up between the salad and the entrée and says, *"If you love your family, you cannot leave them what my last client left his — a mess, a court battle, taxes that ate the whole estate. Tonight, for our guests only, we can set you up with a complete living trust and all your directives — normally thousands, but for you..."* — it does not sound like a robbery. It sounds like the responsible thing. It sounds like exactly what she came here to do.

So there it is — the squeeze. A wise, careful woman who has finally worked up the resolve to plan for the end of her life, sitting at a white tablecloth with a free plate in front of her, and a confident stranger telling her that love itself requires her to write him a check for three thousand dollars tonight. That is the moment they get her: the "living trust mill" selling a package she mostly doesn't need; the annuity quietly folded inside it, moving her savings into a product that pays *him* a fat commission and locks up *her* money for years; the "just sign here" that makes a smooth new "advisor" the agent over not only her care but her checkbook; the letter, weeks later, saying she must "register" or "activate" her living will with the state — for a fee — before it will "count." And the fear behind all of it — *do you want to be the mother who left her children a mess?* — that keeps her from calling her own doctor, or the hospital, or legal aid, anybody who would have told her the real forms are free.

Say it plainly, and I'll say it again before I'm done: the danger is not that she's foolish, or naive, or "should know better at her age." The danger is that she has a real, loving duty — spare her children an impossible choice, keep her own wishes in her own hands, put her house in order the way a good and faithful steward does — and real, honest doors, nearly all of them free, that nobody ever walked her through. So she is left to the first voice that offers to "handle it," and that voice's whole business is to get a signature and a check out of a careful woman before she thinks to call her doctor or the state herself. She doesn't know the Illinois health care power of attorney is a free form. She doesn't know a living will costs nothing. She doesn't know that naming who speaks for her *care* is a completely different thing from handing someone her *money* — and that anyone bundling the two, over dinner, for three thousand dollars, is selling, not serving. None of that is a failing in her. It's a subject nobody teaches, written in words nobody explains, aimed at her in the one season she finally felt ready to face it.

And there are doors. Real ones — that let her name her voice, record her wishes, and spare her family that hallway — and almost nobody names them for a seventy-four-year-old woman who has decided, bravely, to plan.

Start with the first thing she needs to hear, before a single door: deciding these things now, while she is well, is not "giving up," and it is not inviting death, and it is not a failure of faith. It is the opposite. It is love. It is stewardship. It is a strong woman putting her house in order so that, if the worst ever comes, the decision stays between her, her family, and her God — and never falls to a stranger, a courtroom, or three grieving children arguing in a hallway. Not knowing that the free forms exist isn't ignorance; nobody is ever taught this, it's written on purpose to feel complicated, and the people who do understand it mostly charge to explain it. She does not have to be sick to do this — a stroke, a wreck, a bad fall can come to anyone at any age, and the youngest, healthiest people are exactly the ones caught with nothing written. And the paper is only half of it — the real work is the *conversation*, sitting down with the person she trusts most and telling them, out loud, what she wants, so they can carry it without guilt. The shame in this story belongs to the man in the good suit who scares elders into a package they never needed. Never, ever to her.

So here are the doors almost no one names for her plainly, none ranked, all worth a look. Before she pays a seminar or a soul: there is the **Illinois statutory health care power of attorney** — the state's own free form that lets her name the person (and a backup) who will speak for her medical care if she ever can't — and the **Illinois living will**, where she writes down her wishes about life-sustaining treatment. Both are **free**, published by the state and laid out in the **Illinois Attorney General's own consumer guide**, and Illinois generally asks for a **witness, not a lawyer** — though she should check the current signing rules, because they do change. There is the **IDPH Uniform POLST / DNR form** for someone already living with a serious illness — but that one is a **medical order she signs *with* her doctor**, which is different from a directive she writes on her own, and her physician is the right door for it. There is her own **hospital or clinic** — the **palliative-care team, the social worker, the chaplain** — and **hospice**, all of whom help people with exactly this, for free, every single day. There is her **doctor**, who should get a copy for her chart so it's actually there when it's needed. There is **AgeOptions**, the Area Agency on Aging for suburban Cook County, and the **Illinois Department on Aging Senior HelpLine at 1-800-252-8966**. There is free **legal aid** — CARPLS, Legal Aid Chicago, Prairie State — if she wants a lawyer's eyes on it at no cost. There is the **Illinois Mental Health Treatment Preference Declaration** if a psychiatric directive fits her situation. There is the plain and freeing truth that she can **change or revoke any of it, anytime**, as long as she's able. And once it's done, the last real step: **give copies** to her agent, her backup, her doctor, and the hospital — because a perfect form locked in a drawer helps no one — and maybe a small card in her wallet noting she has a directive and who her agent is. And there is **2-1-1**, the free call that opens the rest. That's a whole row of doors — a free state form instead of a three-thousand-dollar package, a hospital team that does this for a living, a state helpline, real help she can afford — and she's about to hand a stranger her savings and her signature because nobody ever told her the simple ones were already hers.

But hear the warning before she signs anything or pays anyone, because these sellers work a church directory and a senior center the way the storm crews work a torn-up block. Hear it plainly, and I'll say it again at the end. There is the **"living trust mill"** — the free-dinner seminar that frightens seniors into an overpriced "trust package," thousands of dollars, for what free state forms and a conversation with her doctor would largely cover. There is the **annuity or insurance product buried inside that package** — the real point of the whole evening, moving her savings into something that pays the salesman a fat commission and ties up her money for years. There is the **notario or unlicensed "planner"** charging big money to "do your directives" and quietly harvesting her personal information. There is the darkest one — the **abused power of attorney itself**: the wrong agent, a smooth relative or a "helpful" new friend, who uses a signed POA to drain her — which is why she must choose that person with enormous care, name a backup, and never let anyone talk her into making her *health-care* voice and her *money* voice the same untrustworthy hand. There is the **"register / activate / file your living will with the state or Medicare — for a fee"** letter, when no such paid registration is required for her directive to count. There is the **"free online directive" website** that really wants her Social Security number, her Medicare number, and her medical history. And there is the **imposter** who says *the hospital, or Medicare, requires this on file — pay to file it today.* The rule is flat: the **real forms are free**, and **no lawyer is required** to make a valid Illinois health care directive; she pays **no one** a fee to "register," "activate," or "file" a directive to make it count; she never signs a "trust package" or moves a dollar into an annuity **at a dinner seminar**; she keeps her **health-care agent and her money separate** unless she fully trusts one person with both; and she confirms **everything** through numbers *she* looks up — her own doctor's office, her hospital, the Illinois Attorney General's official guide, a legal-aid line — never a number from a flyer, a salesman, or a text.

So she almost signed. Not because she's a fool — a fool doesn't spend thirty years easing dying strangers and their families through the worst nights of their lives. She almost signed because she was finally, bravely ready to face the one thing everybody avoids, and a confident man said *this is what a loving mother does, tonight,* and in that moment it felt like the responsible thing.

Here's the thing about the woman across town. Same age, more or less. Same resolve — watched her own friend's family come apart in an ICU hallway and went home saying *not my children.* Same flyer for the same free dinner landed in her mailbox the same week. But somebody had already sat with her once and said: *"When you're ready to do this — and good for you for doing it — you do not need a lawyer and you do not need to pay anybody. The Illinois health care power of attorney and the living will are free forms; the Attorney General's office lays it all out. Your own doctor and the hospital social worker will help you for nothing. And whatever you do, do not sign a 'trust package' or buy anything at a dinner — that dinner is a sales floor."* So she went to the free dinner, ate the free plate, smiled, took the man's card, and threw it away in the parking lot. The next week she asked her doctor's office for the forms, named her most level-headed niece as her health care agent, sat that niece down at the kitchen table and told her exactly what she wanted, gave copies to her doctor and the hospital, and put a little card in her wallet. She wasn't smarter than the woman in our pew. She wasn't sharper, and she didn't love her family more. She had one person who knew the doors were there and named them before a salesman got a signature. One woman has her wishes written, her voice named, her children spared — for free. The other almost handed three thousand dollars and her power of attorney to a man she'd known for one dinner.

The woman in our pew can be met before she signs that package or pays that fee. Not with a lecture about how she should have done this twenty years ago. With a hand on the shoulder and the plain truth that she has real doors, nearly all of them free, and that being the responsible woman who finally decided to plan does not make her a mark for the first man who offers a free dinner.

Because this is where a tool like AI actually meets a woman like her — quietly, at that same kitchen table, the forms in front of her instead of a salesman. It can even help her make the little wallet card.

Not to decide her wishes for her, and not to be her lawyer or her doctor. Let that be plain. The machine is not the state, not her physician, not her attorney; it cannot be her health care agent, cannot witness her form, cannot file a thing — and it can be confidently *wrong* about the current Illinois signing rules, so the real forms and the real requirements come from IDPH, the Attorney General's guide, her doctor, and legal aid, not from a chatbot.
