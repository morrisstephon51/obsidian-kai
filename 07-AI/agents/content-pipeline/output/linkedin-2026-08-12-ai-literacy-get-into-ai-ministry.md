There is a woman in that church who knows everything.

Not the way the world means it. She could not tell you what a large language model is and she would wave you off if you tried. But she knows who is sick before the pastor does. She knows which young mother is one bad week from losing the lights, and she knows it without being told, because she has been reading people in that building for forty years. She keeps the prayer list in her own hand. She ran the kitchen and the benevolence fund on a legal pad and a shoebox of receipts and never once lost a dime, and she raised her own children and she is helping raise her grandchildren, and if that church has a backbone it is her. She is somewhere in her late sixties or her seventies and she has more real intelligence in her than a whole building full of people with letters after their names.

And she is afraid of something she will not say out loud.

Her granddaughter gave her a smartphone and showed her how to answer it and how to see the pictures, and she is grateful, and she uses maybe a tenth of it. But lately the world has started speaking a language she was never taught. Everyone is talking about this AI thing. Her grandson does his schoolwork with it. The young people at work — the ones young enough to be her children — throw the word around like it is nothing. The news says it is going to change every job there is. And she sits in the back of the sanctuary holding a phone that costs more than her first car, and she feels, quietly, in a place she would never admit to, that the future has become a room she was not invited into, and that the door is closing, and that a woman like her is simply meant to be left behind by it and be gracious about it.

Understand her clearly. She is not slow. She is not too old. She is not behind. She is one of the sharpest, wisest, most discerning human beings that congregation has ever produced. She learned a new phone. She learned to text her grandchildren so they would answer her. She has adapted to more change in one lifetime than most people will ever be asked to survive. The problem was never her mind. The problem is that every single time the world changed its language — the computer, the internet, the smartphone, and now this — somebody sat down patiently with the family across town and taught it to them, and nobody ever once sat down patiently with her.

And her family has quietly decided that this is just how it is. That AI is for young people. That it is for the college kids and the tech people and the folks with money across town. That a woman her age has no business with it and should just be glad it leaves her alone. So they never teach her. They do it for her when she asks, quickly, half paying attention, and she feels like a burden, so she stops asking. And the door closes a little more.

Here is the thing nobody has ever said to her plainly: it is not a room she was locked out of. It is a room nobody ever walked her into, and then let her believe the walk was too far for her legs.

Because this thing everyone is so afraid of is, at the bottom, a tool — and she has been mastering tools her whole life, a stove, a sewing machine, a checkbook, a phone she was sure she would never learn and now cannot live without — and this one is more forgiving than any of them, because you talk to it in plain words, her words, the words she already has. Because there are free versions of these AI helpers, free to start, sitting right there on the phone in her purse, and no one ever showed her that the same tool the young people brag about is one she is allowed to touch. Because the public library ten minutes from her house — a place she already trusts, a place that already feels like hers — runs free classes and free help for exactly this, and a real human librarian will sit next to her and go slow and never make her feel foolish, and she has never known to ask. Because there are free courses, real ones, online and at the community college down the road in its continuing-education programs, built for grown adults starting from the very beginning, not for teenagers who already know. Because there is a ministry — this one, the one writing these words — whose entire reason for existing is to walk a woman exactly like her into that room and turn the lights on. Because 211, three digits on that same phone, can point her to a free class nearby she never knew was there. And because — hear this part, because it is the part that hunts her — the moment a woman like her starts reaching toward this, the scammers come. The "get rich with AI this weekend" schemes. The "pay us and get certified" mills that charge a fee for something a library gives away free. And the cruelest one, the one built for a grandmother's heart: a phone call in what sounds exactly like her grandbaby's voice, crying, in trouble, needing money right now — a voice a machine can fake now from a few seconds of sound — and the woman who loves the hardest is the exact woman that trap was built to catch.

So she sits in the back and holds the phone and says nothing. Grateful. Gracious. Quietly certain the future belongs to someone else. She is not being left behind because she is less. She is one of the wisest people in that building. She is being left behind because getting into this new world turned out to have a door, and a light switch, and a patient someone to walk you in — and her family was never handed a single one of them.

Here is the thing about the family across town. When the world changed its language again, nobody in that house treated the grandmother like she was finished. Her grandson sat with her on a Sunday and showed her, slowly, and laughed with her, not at her. Somebody in that family already used these tools at work and brought them home like it was nothing. There was always a person — a grandchild, a neighbor, a nephew who worked in an office — who could just show her, patiently, again and again, until it was hers. Getting into the new world was never a locked room for that family. It was a lamp somebody turned on for the elder as a matter of course. Their grandmother is not sharper than the woman keeping the prayer list in our pews. She is not wiser. Somebody simply walked her into the room, and nobody ever walked ours.

She can have that too. Starting this month. With a free class at a library she already trusts and a patient someone who will never once make her feel slow.

She can open the free helper already sitting on her phone and ask it a question in her own plain words and watch it answer, and feel, maybe for the first time, that this thing is not above her. She can walk into her public library and sit with a librarian who will go at her pace. She can find a free beginner's course built for a grown woman starting at the very start. She can walk into a room The Plug AI is running and be taught, gently, by people who came expecting her, not tolerating her. She can learn the two things that keep her safe — that this tool can be confidently wrong, so you check it, and that you never, ever hand it your Social Security number or your passwords or your bank — and she can learn the sound of a scam so the fake voice on the phone does not empty her savings. She can have the whole overwhelming thing broken into one small step she can take this week.

And once she starts, she has the thing the family across town always had: a patient, tireless helper that meets her exactly where she is. One that answers the same question five times without a sigh. One that explains the word she did not know without making her feel small for not knowing it. One that lets her ask the thing she was too embarrassed to ask a grandchild. It waited for the family across town on the strength of a house full of people who already knew. Ours never had that person. Now she can.

Not to replace her wisdom. Let that be plain, plainer than anything. Not to think for her, not to pray for her, not to discern for her, not to take the place of forty years of reading a room and knowing which young mother needs a casserole before she asks. No machine gets to touch that. Her wisdom is hers. Her discernment is hers. The heart that keeps the prayer list is not something a tool improves on. It is only a translator for a world that started moving fast, a lamp in a room that got dark, a patient hand that walks her to the same starting line the woman across town started from. The knowing stays hers. It always was the point.

The Plug AI says three things, in this order. Get Connected. Get Skilled. Get into AI. We walked the first word, and we walked the second, and this is the third. This is the one the whole name was pointing at the whole time. Because it was never enough for our people to just get online, and it was never enough to just get a credential, if the actual language of the future — this one, the one being written right now — stayed a room our elders and our young and our tired were quietly told they did not belong in.

That is the gap. It was never that the woman in our pews is less capable than the woman across town. It is that one of them was handed the tools, a house where someone already used them, and a grandchild who sat down and turned on the light — and the other was handed a smartphone and a soft, gracious lie that the future was a room she was not invited into.

A word of care, plainly. There are many of these AI tools, and I am not telling you to use one company over another, and this is general navigation, not an endorsement of any product, and not tech or legal or financial advice. These tools can be confidently wrong, so check what they tell you, especially anything that matters. Never put private information into them — not your Social Security number, not your passwords, not your bank or your full medical details. A real free class does not charge you a fee, so anyone demanding money up front for "AI certification" or promising you will get rich with AI in a weekend is running a scam, and the harder a person is reaching for a better life, the more of them will come. And hear this one twice, for our elders especially: a machine can now fake a loved one's voice from a few seconds of sound, so if a call comes saying your grandchild is in trouble and needs money right now, hang up and call that child back on the number you know — the people who love the most are the ones these traps are built for. Route the real learning to real, trusted places: the free versions of the well-known AI assistants, used carefully; your local public library — the Matteson Area Public Library and the South Holland Public Library are two near us — for free classes and a patient librarian; free beginner courses online and your local community college's continuing-education programs, such as Prairie State College in Chicago Heights and South Suburban College in South Holland; The Plug AI's own workshops; and 211 or 211.org to find a free class nearby.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Matteson. Dolton. Chicago Heights. South Holland. Not a webinar. A room. Real people. A real woman who keeps the prayer list in her own hand, who was sure this was not for her, taught gently by people who came expecting her — turning on the light in a room she was told she was never invited into, and watching her walk in like she owned it, because she does.

The wisest people in our churches are not behind. They were just never walked into the room the way the family across town always was.

We are here to open the door.

The plug is in.
