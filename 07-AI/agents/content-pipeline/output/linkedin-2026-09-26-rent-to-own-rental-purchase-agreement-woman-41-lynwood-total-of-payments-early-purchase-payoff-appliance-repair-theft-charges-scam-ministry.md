**Centered on:** SISTER TAMEKA PINKSTON, 41, Lynwood, IL — everybody at Macedonia calls her **Meka.** Fourteen years a **certified nursing assistant** on the three-to-eleven shift at a rehabilitation and skilled-nursing facility off Torrence, $23.60 an hour, the one they call when Room 14 won't let anybody else near her, the one who learned every resident's people by name so she could say *your daughter called* in a way that landed. Three children, raised by herself since their father took a job in Georgia six years ago and slowly stopped calling: **KENDRICK, 16**, a junior who wants people over on Fridays; **ARIELLE, 11**, who reads at the kitchen table; and **TRE', 4**, who is four. She has never missed rent. She has never had a credit card. She has, at any given moment, somewhere between forty and two hundred dollars, and a work uniform requirement that does not care which. In February her washing machine made a sound like a rock in a coffee can and then made no sound at all — and eight weeks later she signed three pieces of paper at a storefront on a Thursday evening, and a truck came Saturday, and for fourteen months she paid $39.99 a week, on time, never late, not once — and at the end of those fourteen months she had paid **two thousand nine hundred and eleven dollars** and she did not own a washer, a dryer, or a television. Not because she was cheated. Because nobody in her entire life had ever taught her to turn the paper over and find the one line that says what the *total* is.

**Faith anchor:** DEACON WARDELL HOLLINS, 69, of Macedonia Missionary Baptist Church in Lynwood — **thirty-four years an appliance repair technician**, the last nineteen of them running his own one-truck operation out of a garage on the 200 block, the man four generations of women on the south end called before they called anybody. He can tell you what's wrong with a washer from the hallway. He keeps a coffee can of **drain pumps** on a shelf because a drain pump is the single most common reason a working woman throws away a machine that has nine years left in it. And for two years — **1994 to 1996** — before the repair work got steady enough to feed his kids, Wardell Hollins drove a delivery truck for a rent-to-own store. *He delivered the Saturday. And he came back for the Monday.* That is the thing he carries, and it is the reason he runs the ministry he runs, and it is why the question he asks every single person who tells him about a new couch is not *how much a week.* He runs the **SECOND BLESSING** table at Macedonia — a garage bay behind the fellowship hall where he and two young men he is teaching repair donated washers, dryers, stoves, and refrigerators and match them, free, to families in the pews and families who have never once been in the pews.

Rental-purchase agreements are governed by Illinois law and by the specific written agreement a person signed, and the terms, dollar amounts, disclosure requirements, notice periods, and reinstatement rules are set by that law and that contract — not by this post, and they change. Nothing here speaks for any store, lender, lease company, the Illinois Attorney General's office, or any court. Read your own written agreement, get the numbers in writing from the company that holds it, and use the free legal help listed below before you sign anything, return anything, or stop paying anything. If any of this has you somewhere dark — and money shame is heaviest at 2 a.m. — **988** is free, 24 hours, call or text.)*

**Resources — the real doors, free or low-cost, no fee to a stranger, ever:**

- **THE WRITTEN AGREEMENT YOU ALREADY HAVE — and the line on it called the TOTAL. This is the first door, it is free, and it is in your own house right now.** Illinois has a law specifically about these contracts: the **Rental-Purchase Agreement Act (815 ILCS 655)**. You do not need to read the statute. You need to know what it exists to make them tell you. A rental-purchase agreement is required to disclose things in writing — including **the cash price of the item, the amount and number and timing of the payments, and the total amount you will have paid if you make every payment until you own it** — and whether the merchandise is **new or used.** That total is the number the advertisement never says out loud. It is on your paper. If you cannot find it, call the store and say these exact words: *"I want the total amount to acquire ownership on my agreement, in writing."* They have that number. It is not a favor. Ask for it on every agreement separately.
- **THE EARLY PURCHASE OPTION — the lever almost nobody knows they are holding, and the single most valuable door in this post for a family already inside a contract.** Under Illinois rental-purchase law these agreements generally must give you a way to **buy the item out early for less than the remaining scheduled payments.** The exact formula is set by the law and by your contract, and it changes depending on how far in you are — so do not guess and do not let anybody on the phone estimate. Say: *"I want my early purchase payoff amount, today's number, in writing."* People who are fourteen months into a lease and about to give up and walk away are very often a few hundred dollars from **owning the thing outright.** Get the number before you make any decision. Get it before you return anything.
- **THE RIGHT TO REINSTATE AFTER YOU MISS — because the worst mistake is hiding.** Illinois rental-purchase law generally provides that if you fall behind and the goods go back, you have a **window to reinstate the agreement** and pick up where you were rather than losing everything you have paid — and if you are past that, you may still be able to negotiate. The number of days and the conditions are set by the law and by your written contract. **Call them before the truck comes, not after.** A missed payment handled out loud is a smaller thing than a missed payment handled by not answering the phone for five weeks.
- **A REPAIRMAN, BEFORE A CONTRACT. A dead appliance is very often a $12 part or a $90 part.** This is not folk wisdom, it is the trade: drain pumps, lid switches, door latches, igniters, thermal fuses, drive belts, a clogged pump full of a child's sock. Before you finance $2,400 worth of laundry at "$39.99 a week," get somebody to *look at it.* Ask at church, ask at the barbershop, ask a neighbor who fixes things, call an independent repair shop and ask what a diagnostic costs. Many dead machines are not dead.
- **FREE AND LOW-COST HOUSEHOLD GOODS ARE REAL, AND MOST PEOPLE HAVE NEVER LOOKED.** Ask your church whether anybody runs a household-goods or appliance ministry (they are everywhere and they are almost never advertised). Then: **furniture banks** and household-goods programs in the Chicago region; **St. Vincent de Paul** and **Salvation Army** family stores and voucher programs; **Habitat for Humanity ReStore** for appliances and furniture at a fraction of retail; church and community **benevolence** funds; your **township office**, which in Illinois often has emergency household help; Buy-Nothing and neighborhood giveaway groups; and scratch-and-dent or floor-model appliances from a local dealer, which is the same machine with a scar on the side. **Call 2-1-1 (or 211.org) and say: "My washer died, I have three kids and a work uniform, what is in my area?"** A live local person will tell you. It is free.
- **A SMALL LOAN FROM A CREDIT UNION OR CDFI BEATS A LEASE, AND LAYAWAY BEATS BOTH.** A community credit union or a CDFI will sometimes make a small-dollar loan to a member with no credit history and a steady check — at a real interest rate that is a fraction of what a lease costs you, with a payment that *ends.* And if you can wait eight weeks, **layaway and saving in a jar are not beneath you**; they are the cheapest financing that exists.
- **FREE OR LOW-COST NONPROFIT CREDIT COUNSELING — nfcc.org** (a National Foundation for Credit Counseling member agency). Free or low-cost, and they will look at the whole picture rather than one contract — which matters, because a rent-to-own agreement is almost never the only leak in the boat.
- **FREE LEGAL HELP ON A CONTRACT, A THREAT, OR A COLLECTOR: Illinois Legal Aid Online (illinoislegalaid.org) — free, plain-English, 24 hours; CARPLS at 312-738-9200 (Cook County legal-aid hotline); Land of Lincoln Legal Aid for the collar and downstate counties.** If somebody is threatening you with **criminal charges** over a rental-purchase agreement, this is the call. Free.
- **TO REPORT THE PREDATORS: the Illinois Attorney General Consumer Fraud Hotline at 1-800-386-5438 (and ag.state.il.us) — this is the office for deceptive rent-to-own practices, illegal threats, and abusive collection; the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov or 1-877-382-4357; the CFPB at consumerfinance.gov/complaint for lease and financing companies; and the FBI's IC3 at ic3.gov for a fake website or a CashApp/Zelle "deposit" that vanished.**
- **2-1-1 (call 211 or 211.org)** for the gap under all of it — food, utilities, rent help, a free tax site. And **988 (call or text)**, free, 24 hours, because a woman doing this arithmetic at her own kitchen table at two in the morning sometimes needs a human being more than she needs a number.

Let me start with the sentence that is the whole post, and it came out of the mouth of a sixty-nine-year-old deacon in a church garage with a screwdriver in his hand:

**"They were never selling her a washing machine. They were selling her Saturday."**

Hold onto that, because everything else here is arithmetic, and the arithmetic is the easy part.

## I. February

The machine died on a Tuesday.

Tameka Pinkston was on the three-to-eleven, which means the laundry happens between midnight and one in the morning, which is how it has happened for fourteen years. She came in at 11:40, made a plate, put a load in, and heard a sound she will describe to you as "a rock in a coffee can, and then nothing." She unplugged it and plugged it back in, which is what everybody does, and it filled with water and sat there full of water and her son's basketball uniform, and it has been sitting in that basement ever since.

Here is what a dead washer costs a certified nursing assistant, and I want to put it in front of the church in the actual units, because we say "she needs a washer" like it's a comfort item.

She wears **scrubs.** They are a work requirement. She cannot show up on a unit in yesterday's scrubs. There is no version of her job where laundry is optional, and there is no version of her week with a spare two and a half hours in it.

The laundromat on the state line is $18 to $22 for her family's four loads, depending on the dryers. Add soap. That is roughly **eighty to ninety dollars a month** she did not have a line for, and it is two and a half hours on a Saturday, and the cart she uses has a wheel that grabs, and Tre' is four, which means Tre' is either asleep on a folding table or he is inside a dryer. Kendrick is sixteen and will help but hates it in a way she understands and does not have room to accommodate. Arielle reads.

For eight weeks she did that. Eight Saturdays.

And on the eighth one, a woman from Macedonia — a good woman, a woman who meant nothing by it, a woman who said *"Girl, you doing laundry here? Y'all all right?"* — walked into that laundromat and saw her.

That is the moment. Not the broken washer. **The being seen.**

I need the church to understand something about that moment, because we get this wrong constantly. Tameka Pinkston did not go to a rent-to-own store because she was financially illiterate. She went eight weeks later, on a Thursday evening, because a woman she sits four rows from saw her family's dirty clothes in a public building, and because Kendrick had stopped asking to have people over, and because she was exhausted in a way that has nothing to do with money.

The store was not competing with a better appliance deal. The store was competing with **shame and a Saturday**, and it knew that, and it won.

## II. The Thursday

Let me describe the transaction honestly, because nothing dramatic happened. There was no con.

The sign said **$39.99 A WEEK. NO CREDIT CHECK. DELIVERED SATURDAY.** Premier Home Rentals, in a strip with a currency exchange and a wing place. The salesman was kind. He was genuinely kind — I want to say that plainly, because the story where the villain is a sneering man in a bad suit lets all of us off the hook. He was kind, he was fast, he did not ask for a credit report, he did not make her explain anything, and he did not once use the word *interest,* because legally he did not have to. **This is not a loan.** That is the entire architecture. She was not borrowing money. She was **leasing**, with an option to own at the end — which means the language of a loan does not apply to it, which means the number a loan would have to shout at her does not have to be shouted.

He asked what she needed. She said a washer. He showed her a washer and a dryer, and said the pair was the same as the washer plus a little, which was true. She said okay.

And then — because he was good at his job, and because she was standing in a showroom at 7 p.m. after a fourteen-hour day with a four-year-old on her hip — he walked her past a sixty-five-inch television.

She thought about Kendrick. Sixteen. Friends. Fridays. A living room a boy would want to bring people to instead of a living room he apologizes for.

She signed three agreements. Washer. Dryer. TV.

The truck came Saturday at 10:15. Two men, polite, shoes off in the house without being asked. They hooked up the washer, they hauled the dead one to the basement, they set the TV on the stand and showed Tre' how the remote worked, and they left.

**And Tameka Pinkston sat down in her own clean living room and cried, because for the first time in eight weeks her children had a normal house on a Saturday.**

I will not let anybody skip that part. That relief was real. The store delivered exactly what it promised, on time, and it changed her family's week.

For the next fourteen months she paid $39.99 a week on the pair and a separate weekly on the TV. She paid it in the store, in person, with cash, most Fridays after the bank. Fourteen months. **Never once late.** The woman does not know how to be late.

## III. The question nobody had ever asked her

Fourteen months later, in April, Deacon Wardell Hollins was loading a rebuilt dryer into a Buick behind the fellowship hall — Second Blessing, last Saturday of the month — and Tameka was helping because that is what she does, and she mentioned, easy and proud, that she had a good set at home now and it was almost paid off.

And Wardell asked her one question.

**"What's the total on the paper?"**

She said what everybody says. *"Thirty-nine ninety-nine a week."*

"No, baby," he said. "That's the payment. What's the **total**?"

And she stood there in a church parking lot and realized she did not know. Not *couldn't remember* — had never known. Had never been told, had never asked, had never once in her life been taught that there was a different number than the one on the sign.

She went home and got the papers out of the drawer where the papers live, under the school forms, and she sat at her own kitchen table, and Arielle got her a pen because Arielle is eleven and knows what her mother's face means.

Here is what it said.

**Washer and dryer: cash price $1,048. Total to acquire ownership: $3,119.20.**

**Television: cash price $478. Total to acquire ownership: $1,068.60.**

Total on all three, if she paid it out the way she had been paying it out faithfully for fourteen months: **four thousand one hundred eighty-seven dollars and eighty cents,** for merchandise the same store said, on the same paper, was worth **fifteen hundred twenty-six dollars.**

She had already paid **twenty-nine hundred and eleven dollars.** She owned nothing. She owned nothing at all. She had paid nearly twice the cash price of every item in her house and if she stopped paying that night, a truck would come and take all three, and the twenty-nine hundred dollars would be gone the way rain is gone.

She said the number out loud at her table. She said "four thousand, one hundred, eighty-seven dollars" out loud in her own kitchen at eleven-forty at night, and she had to sit down, and then she got angry, and then — and this is the part that matters, this is the part I need the whole church to hear — **she got ashamed.**

Because the first thing an intelligent, hardworking, faithful woman does when she finally sees that number is not *they robbed me.* It is **what is wrong with me that I didn't know.**

## IV. Deacon Hollins, 1995

Now let me tell you why that old man asks that question.

Wardell Hollins drove a rent-to-own delivery truck from 1994 to 1996. He was thirty-eight, he had three kids, the repair business wasn't steady enough yet, and a route job with a steady check was a route job with a steady check. He is not apologetic about taking it. He is something else about what he did in it.

Because the truck goes two directions.

"Saturday, I'm the best man you ever met," he says. "I'm in your house, I'm carrying a sofa, your babies are running around me, your mama's telling me about her knee, I'm the man that made your house a house. Monday I'm the same man with the same truck and I'm taking it back out the door."

In the spring of 1995 they sent him to a two-flat to recover a washer from a woman he calls **Miss Odetta**, because he never knew her last name and he has never once forgotten her first.

She had been paying on it for about two years. She was, he thinks, three or four payments from the end — he never saw the contract, drivers don't — and something had happened, a hospital thing, a hurt hand, he doesn't know, and she had missed, and she had missed again, and the office cut a recovery ticket.

She did not fight him. She stood in the doorway of her own basement and she asked him one thing. She asked him to wait, because **there were clothes in it,** and they were wet.

He waited. He let her wring out her family's wet clothes into a laundry basket on the concrete floor while he stood there with the dolly. Then he took the washer out to the truck and drove it back to the warehouse, where — and he knows this, because he worked there — it was cleaned up and put back on the floor and rented again to somebody else on a Saturday.

She had paid, by his rough math, close to **eighteen hundred dollars** on a machine the store would have sold anybody for about six hundred.

"I did that," Wardell Hollins says. He says it every time, the same way, and he does not let his voice do anything with it. "Wasn't a scam. Wasn't against the law. I had a ticket and a dolly and three kids at home and I did it. Four months later I quit and went to fixing them instead of hauling them, and I have been fixing them for thirty-four years, and I still see that woman's hands."

Then he says the sentence that is the reason he is the anchor of this post:

**"I have been the man on the other end of that knock. So don't you ever ask me how much a week. Ask me what's the total."**

## V. The thing that makes this different from every other money post

Let me draw the lines hard, because this domain gets confused with four others constantly and the confusion is expensive.

**This is not payday lending or a title loan.** In that world — and we have sat with that in this series, with a twenty-nine-year-old man and a 300% APR — you **borrow cash** and pay back more, and the fix runs through interest rates, credit unions, CDFIs, and the language of a loan. Rent-to-own is structured as a **lease**, so the borrowing never legally happens. No money is lent. No APR has to be printed on the wall. That is not an accident; it is the design. Everything in this post exists because the harm had to be measured in a different unit: **the total.**

**This is not "rent-to-own a house."** We sat with a forty-nine-year-old man in University Park and a contract for deed — an installment land sale, deeds, title, forfeiture, a recorder's office. Same phrase in the street, completely unrelated law, completely different door. Do not let the words collapse. One is your house. This one is the washer in your basement.

**This is not the wheelchair.** When we walked through durable medical equipment with a fifty-eight-year-old woman, rent-to-own showed up as exactly one warning line: never lease a $2,400 scooter that Medicaid will cover for nothing. That warning was right, and it does not apply here, and I want to be honest about why: **nobody covers a washing machine.** There is no benefit program that hands a working mother a dryer. That is precisely what makes this lane hard and precisely why the doors in this post are different in kind — they are *repair, reuse, benevolence, a furniture bank, a credit union, and arithmetic,* not a covered benefit. If this post promised her a free appliance from a program, it would be lying to her.

**This is not a car.** Auto loans, repossession, title, liens, used-car fraud — all sat with, all separate, all governed by other law.

**And this is not the collection lawsuit.** Everything we have walked through about a summons, an appearance, the FDCPA, a default judgment, garnishment exemptions, bankruptcy — that all begins *after* a default, mostly in a courtroom. This post lives in two places a courtroom never sees: **the thirty seconds before a signature, and the phone call in month fourteen.** If you are already past that, the free legal-aid numbers above are the same ones, and they are the right call.

One more, and it's the one that pairs: last post in this series was about a fifty-four-year-old man whose job never offered him a retirement plan — money that **never got captured.** This one is the opposite failure in the same kitchen. **This is money that got captured and then poured out through a hole nobody showed her.** You cannot save thirty dollars a week in a house that is leaking a hundred and sixty.

## VI. Six predators, and five of them showed up *after* she started asking

Here is the part that shocked me, and it is the structural truth of this domain:

**The expensive thing was legal. The criminals arrived the week she started asking questions.**

**1. The store that tells you the payment and lets you not hear the total.** This is the big one and mostly it is legal. The advertisement is the payment. The window is the payment. The salesman is the payment. The total is on page two in a box, and the law requires it to be there, and nobody in the building is going to read it to you unless you ask, and the entire business model depends on the fact that almost nobody ever asks. The tell is not a lie. **The tell is an omission you can end with one sentence:** *"Read me the total to acquire ownership out loud before I sign."* If a salesman gets strange when you ask that, you have learned everything.

**2. The "no credit needed" button at checkout — the phone-app cousin.** This one is newer and it is coming for her children, not her. Tameka's cousin bought four tires online, and at checkout there was a button: *no credit needed, get approved in seconds, four easy payments.* It was not the tire store's financing. It was a third-party lease company — the kind with a name like QuikLease — and here is the trap inside it: there is usually a **short early-payoff window** (often advertised as ninety days or similar) where the thing costs you close to retail, **and the clock frequently starts at delivery, not at your first payment,** and if you drift past it the cost multiplies. It auto-debits a card he entered once, which means the leak is silent. **Rule for a young person: if the button says "no credit needed," find the total and the payoff deadline before you click, and put the deadline in your phone as an alarm.** Same law, same math, smaller screen, no salesman to be kind to you.

**3. The store that doesn't exist.** A Facebook Marketplace or a slick site advertising rent-to-own appliances, no credit, delivery this week — asking for a **delivery deposit by CashApp, Zelle, or a gift card** before anybody comes. Nobody comes. This one is not expensive-but-legal; this one is theft, and it is IC3 and the FTC. **A real store does not need a wired deposit from a stranger to put a washer on a truck.** If you cannot walk into the building, do not send the money.

**4. The "we're pressing charges Monday" call.** This is the one that put Tameka Pinkston on the floor of her own bathroom, and it came four days after she started asking for her numbers. A man called — not the store, or maybe the store, she will never know — and told her that she was in possession of **stolen rental property**, that a **criminal complaint** for theft was being filed Monday, that she should think about her children and her CNA license, and that all of it went away if she paid a settlement figure **today, by CashApp.**

Hear me on this. **A bill you cannot pay is not a crime.** A collector on a phone does not file criminal charges; that is not a thing a collections office decides on a Thursday afternoon. Threats like that are exactly what the Illinois Attorney General's Consumer Fraud line and the free legal-aid numbers exist for. And notice the tell that gives the whole game away: **a legitimate company does not settle a criminal matter by CashApp.** The demand for a payment app *is* the confession. Write down the time, the number, and what was said, and make the free call.

**5. "It builds your credit" — and the $499 man who fixes what it didn't.** She was told the lease would build her credit. Fourteen months of perfect payments later, there was nothing on her credit report at all — because many of these agreements simply are not reported as credit, since legally they are not credit. The cruelty is the asymmetry: **on-time payments often don't help you, and a default can still find its way to a collector.** And then, right on schedule, a man at a barbershop with a folder offered to "repair" her credit and "delete" the account for **$499 up front.** Charging up front for credit repair is a red flag in itself, and nobody can delete accurate information. She had already learned that lesson in this church, from another post, and she walked.

**6. The quiet thief — and it is not a man with a folder. It is Saturday.**

This is the one I want the church to sit in.

Rent-to-own's actual product is not merchandise. It is **speed and yes.** It is the ability of a tired mother at 7 p.m. on a Thursday to say *yes* to her own children by Saturday morning, with no credit check, no application, no explaining herself to anybody, no being told no by one more institution.

Every free door in this post is *slower* than that. The furniture bank has a process. The credit union has a membership. The repairman comes Tuesday. Layaway takes eight weeks. **The predator's one unbeatable advantage is that it never makes you wait and it never makes you ask.**

And underneath the speed is the thing the speed is covering, which is being seen in a laundromat by a woman from your own church. Shame is what sets the price. Tameka Pinkston did not overpay $2,661 for laundry. She paid $2,661 to not be the mother whose kids have dirty clothes, and there is no interest rate on earth that captures that, and any post that calls her foolish for it has understood nothing.

**Which means the fix cannot be a lecture. It has to be a faster, warmer door that gets to her before Thursday.** That is a job a store cannot do and a church can.

## VII. What she actually did, in order

Nothing here is heroic and all of it is repeatable.

**One. She got the total in writing, on all three agreements, separately.** She called the store — the number she looked up herself, not one from a text — and asked for the total to acquire ownership on each. They gave it to her. It took nine minutes. That is the whole first step, and it is free, and anybody reading this can do it tonight.

**Two. Then Wardell taught her the lever, and this is the most valuable paragraph in this post.** She was ready to stop paying and let them take everything, because that is what humiliation wants you to do. He told her: **do not walk away. Ask for your early purchase payoff.** Not an estimate. Today's number, in writing.

The payoff on the washer and dryer — fourteen months in, with roughly thirteen hundred dollars of scheduled payments still ahead of her — was **four hundred and twelve dollars.**

Four hundred and twelve dollars stood between Tameka Pinkston and **owning her own washer and dryer.** She had been three months from a thousand dollars she did not need to spend, and nobody had told her the option existed, and it was printed in her own contract in her own drawer.

She paid it with her tax refund and one hundred and fifty dollars from Macedonia's benevolence fund, which the trustees voted in eleven minutes because Wardell brought the paper with the number on it and did not editorialize.

She owns them. There is a receipt in the drawer that says so.

**Three. The television she gave back, and it cost her, and I am not going to soften it.** She looked at the TV's payoff and the TV's total and she made the hard adult decision: she returned it voluntarily, in person, with a receipt, and she got it in writing that the agreement was closed. Everything she had paid on that television is **gone.** Several hundred dollars, gone, and she is not getting it back, and there is no undo button in this post and no program that reimburses her. I want that sitting in the middle of this thing, because a post that ends with everybody whole is a post that is lying, and because the honest version is the one that actually helps the next woman: **the money you already paid is usually not recoverable. The only thing you can still control is the number going forward.** She told Kendrick why, at the table, straight. He was sixteen about it for two days. Then he wasn't.

**Four. She reported the threat.** Illinois Attorney General consumer fraud line, and the FTC. Free, about twenty minutes, and the thing she got back from it was bigger than the outcome: she found out that an unpayable bill is not a crime, and she stopped being afraid of her own phone.

**Five. She took the basement washer to the church garage.** Deacon Hollins put it on the concrete, pulled the pump, and held up a **drain pump** with a child's sock and a hair tie wound into the impeller.

**Ninety dollars and an hour.**

That is what stood between Tameka Pinkston and never walking into that store at all. A ninety-dollar part on a machine with years left in it.

And here is how a real elder handles a moment like that, because this is craft and I want it copied: he did not let her go where she was going. He said, **"Don't you dare sit there feeling stupid. You didn't fail a test. Nobody in your life ever had one reason to teach you what I know — I learned it because I got paid to. That's not a character flaw, baby. That's a missing uncle."**

*That's a missing uncle.* The gap was never her intelligence. It was **access to somebody who knew.** And a church is, structurally, an uncle.

She donated the repaired machine to Second Blessing. It went to a family in Sauk Village in June. She asked Wardell to let her ride along on the delivery, and she did, and she says it fixed something in her that the $412 didn't.

**Six. She taught Kendrick the arithmetic.** He turns seventeen this year. He has a phone. The "no credit needed" button is going to find him at a checkout within eighteen months, and it will be polite, and it will be fast, and it will not mention a total.

So she sat him at the table with her own three contracts spread out — the actual papers, the actual numbers, her own mistake on display in front of her son, which is a specific kind of courage — and she taught him the one move:

**Multiply it. Before you sign anything, multiply the payment by the number of payments, and say that number out loud. And if saying it out loud makes you feel some kind of way — that feeling is information.**

"The button's coming for him," she said. "I wanted the number to get there first."

## VIII. The honest caveats, because this post is not a sermon against a store

Three things, and they cost me something to write.

**Sometimes it is the only door on a Tuesday, and this post does not exist to shame one single person who used it.** If a family is sleeping on a floor tonight, and there is no ministry, no furniture bank, no township office open, no credit union that will have them, and a store will put beds in that apartment on Saturday — then a bed on Saturday is worth something real, and a mother who chooses it is not a fool, she is a mother. **What this post asks is that she go in knowing the total, and that she get out through the early-purchase option as fast as humanly possible.** Know the number, and treat the contract like a fire you're walking through, not a house you're living in.

**The math is not the whole story, and anybody who thinks it is has never been broke.** *"Just save up for eight weeks"* is a sentence with an invisible assumption inside it — that there are eight weeks in which nothing else breaks. In Tameka Pinkston's life, something else always breaks. That is not poor planning. That is what being one person with three children and no cushion actually is.

**And the free doors are slower, which means the church has to be faster.** This is the part that is on us and not on her. The store got to her in eight weeks. Second Blessing had been forty feet behind the fellowship hall the entire time and she did not know it was for her, because the sign on it said it was for people in need, and Tameka Pinkston does not consider herself a person in need. She has a job. **A ministry that only serves people who will call themselves needy will never reach the working poor, who are most of the room.**

## X. The last Saturday of the month

Macedonia does Second Blessing on the last Saturday. Wardell has done it for eleven years: donated machines, a garage bay, two young men learning a trade that will feed them, families driving off with a working dryer in a trunk tied down with rope.

In May, Tameka Pinkston asked him for a folding table next to the garage door.

No sermon. No seminar. No advisor, nobody selling anything, no sign-up sheet. A card table, two chairs, a calculator from the church office, and a legal pad with one thing written across the top of the first page in marker:

**WHAT IS THE TOTAL?**

Fourteen people sat down at that table that Saturday.

**Nine of them had a rent-to-own or lease-to-own agreement live in their house right then. Not one of the nine could say the total.** Two had brought the contracts in a grocery bag, unopened since the day they signed, because opening mail about money is its own kind of dread. One young mother had a payoff on a bedroom set that was two hundred and sixty dollars and she had been about to let them take it. One man, sixty-one, was fourteen months into paying weekly on a **laptop that had been broken for nine months**, still paying, because he did not know he could stop, and did not know what stopping would cost, and had never asked anybody, and had assumed — this is the sentence that should end every argument about whether this work matters — **"I figured it was my own fault, so it was my own business."**

They do it every last Saturday now. Deacon Hollins calls it **Multiply It.**

And Tameka Pinkston keeps that drain pump — the ninety-dollar part, with the sock fibers still wound in the impeller — on the windowsill over her kitchen sink, where she can see it while she's standing there. Arielle asked her why she doesn't throw it out.

"Because I paid twenty-nine hundred dollars for that ninety-dollar piece of plastic," she said. "And because I'm the uncle now."
