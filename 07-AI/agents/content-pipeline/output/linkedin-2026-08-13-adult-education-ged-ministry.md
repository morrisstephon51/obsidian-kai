There is a man in that church who keeps everything running.

Not the church building. A whole school. He is the one they call when the boiler quits in January and the second graders are still in their coats. He is the one who knows which breaker, which valve, which door has stuck since 2019 and how to lift it just so. He can walk a hallway and tell you what is wrong before anybody files the work order. He does the math in his head faster than the young cashier can ring it up. Twenty years he has kept that place standing, quietly, before the first bell and long after the last child goes home. If that school has heat and light and a floor that is safe to run on, it is because of him.

And every single day, he keeps up a building full of children learning the very thing he never got to finish himself.

Because he left in eleventh grade. Not because he was slow. Not because he was lazy. His own father got hurt and could not work, and there were younger ones at home, and somebody had to bring money in the door that week — not next semester, that week. So at sixteen he became the grown man of the house, and the paycheck came, and the diploma did not. He always told himself he would go back. Then one year became five became twenty, and he never told a soul.

He has hidden it for two decades. He hides it well. But it is there on every application he has ever been afraid to fill out, one line, the same line, the one that stops him cold before he even starts: high school diploma or GED required.

So he watches. He trains the new young guys — shows them the building, the systems, the shortcuts it took him years to learn — and then he watches those same young men get moved up over him, into the supervisor's title, the better pay, the benefits, because they have a piece of paper he does not. And then, most days, one of them comes back down the hall to quietly ask him how to actually do the job. He answers. Every time. And he swallows the thing that rises up in his throat, and he goes back to work.

Understand what this really is, because it is not a knowledge problem. This is one of the sharpest, most dependable men that congregation has, sitting under a ceiling he can see but has never been able to reach. Every promotion has that line. Every real training program has that line. The workforce classes that could double his pay have that line. He is more than smart enough for all of it. He is locked out of all of it by a decision he made at sixteen to keep his family fed.

And there is a newer wound now, and it is the one that actually keeps him up at night. His daughter is in middle school, and she brings home homework, and she comes to him because he is her daddy and he knows everything — and there are nights he cannot help her, and he has to make an excuse, and go to the other room, so she will not see his face. He tells both his kids the same thing every morning. Stay in school. Finish. Do not do what — and then he stops himself, because he has never told them the rest of that sentence.

So he has quietly decided that this is just who he is now. That the ship sailed twenty years ago. That a grown man in his late thirties does not go sit in a classroom, does not admit at this age what he never finished, is too old and too far behind and too proud and too tired. That men like him get the ceiling, and that is simply the deal. So he does not ask. And the ceiling stays exactly where it is.

Here is the thing nobody has ever laid out plainly for him: finishing what got interrupted, out here, as a working adult, is not a door that closes at eighteen. It is a system — a real, free, quiet, badly-advertised system — and there are doors in it built for exactly a man like him, and nobody ever walked him to a single one.

Because the community college he drives past on the way to work every single day very likely runs adult-education classes designed for grown people with jobs and kids — the ones out here have done it for years, places like Prairie State College over in Chicago Heights and South Suburban College down in South Holland, named only so he knows this is real and near, not ranked and not the only ones. Because those classes are, very often, free — not college-loan free, actually free — and that alone undoes the thing he assumed, which was that going back would cost money he does not have. Because the credential at the end of it is real: the GED, or another state-approved high-school-equivalency test, is recognized by employers and by colleges the same as a diploma, and it is the exact key that fits every locked line he has ever stared at. Because many of these programs run at night, on weekends, or online, and some help with childcare or a ride, precisely so a working father does not have to quit his job to get them — he can keep the lights on and still go. Because some of them now let you do both at once, earn the equivalency while you train for a trade in the same program, so the thing he thought he had to do first before he could ever "get skilled" turns out to be something he can do at the same time. Because the public library down the street runs free tutoring and study groups and has the quiet room and the computer he might not have at home. Because 211, three digits on the phone in his pocket, can point him to the nearest free class this week. And because — hear this part, because it hunts men in exactly his spot — the moment he starts searching online, the diploma mills come running: the sites that promise an "instant GED" or a "high school diploma today," no class, no test, just a fee. That paper is worthless. No employer takes it, no college takes it, and it is a scam built to sell shame a fake cure. A real credential comes from real study and a real test, and the honest version of it is usually free.

So he sits under a ceiling and believes it is the roof of the world. He is not stuck because he is not smart enough. He is one of the smartest men in that building. He is stuck because finishing turned out to have a whole set of doors — a free class ten minutes away, an evening schedule, a real test that opens every locked line, a library, a helper — and nobody ever walked him to one of them, and the one time he looked, a scammer got to him first.

Here is the thing about the family across town. When their son was sixteen and stumbled in eleventh grade — and their son did stumble, plenty of them do — there was a household that could absorb it. Nobody needed his paycheck that week. There was a parent who called the counselor, who found the tutor, who sat in the meeting, who built the summer-school plan, who simply would not let him fall all the way through. So that boy graduated, maybe by a thread, and got the diploma and every locked line it opens. And twenty years later, when that same man needs to level up, there is still somebody in the house — a spouse, a sibling, a friend who works in an office — who reads the program page and drafts the email and figures out the form as a matter of course. He was never smarter than the man who keeps the school running. He just had a household that could catch him at sixteen, and a person who handles the paperwork at thirty-eight. Ours had neither. He was the person who caught everyone else.

He can have that second thing now. Starting this week. Not a household that could have caught him at sixteen — that time is real and it is gone. But the patient helper the other man always effectively had, the one who reads the confusing page and explains the hard part and never once makes him feel like the slowest person in the room.

Because this is where a tool like AI actually meets a man like him, quietly, in the background, doing the part his life never came with. It can be the study partner at eleven o'clock at night, after the shift, after the kids are down, when he would rather cut off his own arm than raise his hand in front of strangers — the one that explains a fraction, or how to build a paragraph, or what the question is really asking, as many times as he needs, and never sighs, and never makes a face. It can break the whole thing into small pieces so it stops looking like a mountain. It can quiz him on the drive to work. It can find the free program nearest his house and draft the exact three questions to ask when he calls — does it cost anything, are there evening classes, how long does it take — so he does not have to walk in cold. It is the patient someone who sits with him in the part that always felt lonely and shameful, so it stops being either.

Not to take the test for him. Let that be plain, because it matters more here than anywhere. The machine does not sit the exam. It cannot, and it must not, and a credential he did not actually earn would be the diploma-mill lie all over again — worthless paper and a fresh shame. It does not replace the teacher or the tutor or the classroom full of other grown people quietly doing the same brave thing. It does not do the learning for him. And it most certainly does not give him the intelligence — he has had that the whole time; it is what has kept that school standing for twenty years. It is only the patient helper who makes the door less lonely and less frightening, so that a man who was always smart enough can finally go get the paper that proves it.

That is the gap. It was never that the man who keeps the whole school running is less capable than the man across town. It is that one of them, at sixteen, had a household that could absorb a crisis and a person to call the counselor — and the other, at sixteen, was the crisis-absorber, the one holding his family up, and there was no one to catch him and no one to handle the forms. One got a diploma and a helper. The other got a job and a ceiling. And he has spent twenty years being the helper for everybody else while never having one of his own.

A word of care, plainly, and I will say the important parts twice. This is general navigation, not an endorsement of any one school, program, or company over another, and it is not legal or financial or educational advice. AI tools can be confidently wrong, so never trust a program, a cost, a schedule, or a test detail it gives you without confirming it with the actual community college, testing program, or library — call them, or walk in. Never put private information into a public AI tool: not your Social Security number, not any ID number. And hear this one twice, because it is built for men in exactly this spot: a real high-school-equivalency credential comes from real study and a real test, and legitimate prep is usually free or very low-cost through a community college or a library, so any site promising an "instant GED" or a "diploma today" for a fee, with no class and no test, is a diploma-mill scam selling worthless paper — and it hunts the people carrying the most shame. Route the real help to real, trusted places: your local community college's adult-education office, such as Prairie State College or South Suburban College, to ask about free HSE classes and evening or online schedules; the Illinois Community College Board and that same adult-ed office for how the equivalency test actually works; your local public library for free tutoring and a quiet place to study; and 211 or 211.org to find a free class near you.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Dolton. Robbins. Chicago Heights. Matteson. South Holland. Not a webinar. A room. Real people. A real man who has kept a whole school standing for twenty years, taught how to make the same tool the other family always had sit with him at night and help him finish the thing he was always smart enough to finish — so the man everybody leans on can finally reach the ceiling he has been holding up the whole time.

The smartest people in our churches are not stuck because they gave up. They are stuck because at sixteen, somebody had to hold the family up, and it was them.

We are here to help them finish anyway.

The plug is in.
