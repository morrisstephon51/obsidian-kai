There is a man on that block everybody knows.

He has had his own chair for thirty years. He cut your hair, and your father's hair, and now he is cutting your son's — three generations in the same seat, the same steady hands, the same low voice that has heard every secret on that street and kept every one of them. When somebody passes, he knows before the news does. When a young man is about to do something stupid, he is the one who talks him down over the clippers. He opens when he says he will open. He is the corner of that block that does not move. Thirty years, one chair, and a whole neighborhood that trusts him with the back of its neck.

And in thirty years he has never once had health insurance.

Not because he was reckless. Because it never felt like it was for him. Insurance was an HR thing. A boss thing. A form somebody hands you on your first day at a company, in a building, with a badge. He never had a first day like that. He built the thing himself, from a borrowed chair and a hand mirror, and nobody ever handed him a card, and so somewhere along the way he decided — the way you decide something without ever saying it out loud — that coverage was simply for other people. People with a boss. People with a badge. Not people like him.

So for thirty years he just did not go. A cold, he waited out. A cut, he handled himself. The rare time something scared him enough, he paid cash at the counter and did not ask what the next visit would cost, because he already knew he would not be coming back for it.

But the body keeps its own books, and lately it has been sending him notices. The blood-pressure machine at the drugstore, the one by the pharmacy counter — he pumps the cuff while he waits for nothing, and the number comes up red, and he tells himself the machine is off. His knees talk to him now on the days he stands from open to close. There is a tired that sleep does not touch. And 65 — Medicare, the one card he knows is coming, the one the country finally hands a man his age — is still a few years out. A few years is a long time to hold your breath.

So here is what he actually does, this steady man, this pillar. He lies awake and types his symptoms into his phone at two in the morning, and the phone tells him it is nothing or it is everything, and he cannot tell which, and there is no one to ask. He rations himself. He decides, quietly, that he will simply not find out — because in his math, the worst thing is not being sick. The worst thing is a diagnosis with a price tag on it that takes the shop, and the little he managed to put away, and the one thing he built that was his. So he does not look. Not looking feels like protecting it. Not looking is how he is losing it.

Understand what this really is, because it is not carelessness. This is one of the most responsible men on that block — thirty years of showing up, of keeping his word, of holding a corner steady for a whole neighborhood — sitting on a health problem he can feel, because the system that hands other people a card at their first day of work never issued him one, and he came to believe that meant he was not allowed to have it. He is not uncovered because he is foolish with his money. He has been careful with his money his entire life. He is uncovered because everything about how this country does coverage assumes a boss, a badge, a W-2 — and he had a chair.

And here is the thing nobody has ever laid out plainly for him: coverage for a man like him — self-employed, careful, a few years short of Medicare — is not a door that only opens for people with a boss. It is a system, a real, quiet, free-to-navigate system with actual help in it, built in part for exactly the person who works for himself, and nobody ever walked him to a single door in it.

Because the state has an official place to get covered — Get Covered Illinois — and, more than a website, there are free navigators, real trained people, who will sit with you and help you enroll and never charge you a dime, and that alone undoes the first thing he assumed, which was that figuring this out would cost money he does not have. Because the marketplace where a self-employed man buys his own plan very often comes with help paying for it — the subsidy — and a barber with a modest, up-and-down income is frequently exactly the person who qualifies for real help on the premium and never once checks, because he assumed the sticker price was the price. Because depending on what a year actually brings in, he might qualify for Medicaid — free or very low-cost coverage — and never know it, because he never asked. Because there are community health centers out here — some people call them federally qualified health centers — that will see him now, this month, on a sliding scale, regardless of whether he has a card or a dollar, places like Aunt Martha's that have served these south suburbs, named only so he knows this is real and near, not ranked and not the only one. Because the help has windows and timing to it, so the answer is not "the door is closed," the answer is "ask now, because there may be a window open you cannot see from where you are standing." Because 211, three digits on the phone in his shirt pocket, can point him to free enrollment help and low-cost care this week. And because — hear this part, because it hunts men in exactly his spot — the minute a man starts looking for coverage, the vultures come: the robocalls and the slick cold-callers selling a "health plan," pressing him to pay right now and read off his Social Security number right now, and the "junk" plans that look like insurance in the ad and turn out to cover almost nothing the day he actually needs them. Real help does not cold-call you. Real help does not rush you. Real help — the navigator — is free.

So he sits on a corner he has held for thirty years, believing the card is for other people. He is not uncovered because he was careless. He is one of the most careful men on that block. He is uncovered because coverage turned out to have a whole set of doors — a free navigator, a subsidy he probably qualifies for, a Medicaid door he never tried, a clinic that would see him tomorrow — and nobody ever walked him to one of them, and the one time the phone rang with an offer, it was a scammer.

Here is the thing about the man across town. He has had an insurance card in his wallet since he was twenty-four. Not because he is wiser about health, or more careful with his body — he is not; he skips checkups too. He has it because a job handed it to him on his first day, and an HR portal quietly renews it every single autumn while he clicks "keep my plan" without reading it, and if his ever lapsed there is a spouse's plan behind it, and if he got a scary number at the drugstore he would simply make an appointment, because the appointment is free and the card is just there. He never had to go find coverage. It found him, on day one, and it has followed him around ever since like a shadow he does not think about. He is not smarter than the barber. He never had to be. Coverage was the background of his life. For the barber it was a blank space where the card should be, and the difference between them was never responsibility. It was a badge one of them was handed and the other one never was.

The barber can have that same protection now. Not the badge — he built a chair instead of a career at a company, and that was a good and honorable life. But the help the other man effectively always had — someone who reads the confusing page, who knows which door, who makes sure he does not overpay and does not get robbed — he can have that, starting this week.

Because this is where a tool like AI actually meets a man like him, quietly, in the background, doing the part his life never came with. It can be the patient someone at eleven o'clock at night, after the last cut, who explains what a premium is, and a deductible, and a subsidy, and a "network," in plain words, as many times as he needs, and never sighs and never makes him feel like the only grown man in the room who did not already know. It can help him get a rough sense of whether he might qualify for help paying, so he walks in with his shoulders back instead of bracing to be embarrassed. It can find the nearest free navigator, and the nearest community health center, and put the address in his hand. It can draft the exact three questions to ask when he calls — is the help free, what might I actually pay, and is there a window open now — so he is not walking in cold. It can sit with him in the part that always felt like a locked door for people with a boss, so it stops feeling that way.

Not to sign him up. Let that be plain, because it matters. The machine does not enroll him in a plan — a human navigator or the official marketplace does that, and it must be a real one. It does not diagnose him, and it does not tell him what that red number at the drugstore means; that is the doctor's to say, once he has a door to walk through to reach one. It does not replace the navigator or the clinic or the physician. And it must never, ever be handed his Social Security number or his income papers — that is exactly what the scam on the phone is fishing for. It is only the patient someone who makes the door less confusing and less frightening, so that a man who spent thirty years covering everyone else can finally go get himself covered.

That is the gap. It was never that the man who has held that corner for thirty years is less responsible than the man across town. It is that one of them was handed a coverage card on his first day of work and has clicked "renew" every autumn since without thinking about it, and the other built something real with his own hands and got no card because the whole machine is wired for a boss he never had. One got coverage as the automatic background of his life. The other got a business and a blank space, and a quiet decision at two in the morning not to find out what is wrong. One never had to look. The other was simply never shown where to look.

A word of care, plainly, and I will say the important parts twice. This is general navigation, not an endorsement of any one plan, insurer, program, or health center over another, and it is not legal, financial, medical, or insurance advice. AI tools can be confidently wrong, so never trust a plan, a price, a subsidy amount, an eligibility rule, or an enrollment deadline it gives you without confirming it with an actual navigator, the official marketplace, or the health center — call them, or sit down with them in person. Never put private information into a public AI tool: not your Social Security number, not your income documents, not a member or Medicaid ID. And hear this one twice, because it is built for men in exactly this spot: legitimate enrollment help — a navigator — is always free, and nobody legitimate cold-calls you to sell a "health plan," rushes you, or demands your Social Security number and a payment on the spot, so any call or ad promising instant coverage that pressures you to pay and hand over your number is a scam, and a "plan" you cannot look up and verify through the official marketplace is not real coverage. Route the real help to real, trusted places: Get Covered Illinois and a free local navigator to see what you qualify for and to actually enroll; the official marketplace and that same navigator for how the subsidy and the plans really work; Illinois Medicaid, through the navigator, in case free or low-cost coverage is open to you; a community health center near you, such as Aunt Martha's, to be seen now on a sliding scale regardless of insurance; and 211 or 211.org to find free enrollment help and low-cost care nearby.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Dolton. Robbins. Chicago Heights. Matteson. South Holland. Not a webinar. A room. Real people. A real man who has held a corner of that block steady for thirty years, taught how to make the same tool the family across town always had sit with him at night and help him find the door to coverage — so the man everybody trusts with the back of their neck can finally take care of his own.

The most dependable people in our churches are not uncovered because they were careless. They are uncovered because the card was handed to somebody else on day one, and nobody ever walked them to the door that was theirs too.

We are here to walk them to it.

The plug is in.
