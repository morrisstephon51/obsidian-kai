There is a man in that church who almost drove to the store one afternoon to buy seven hundred dollars in gift cards, because a voice on the phone told him it was the only way to keep the lights on for his kids that night.

You know him. Forty-one. Runs a forklift at the warehouse, first one in, quiet in the back pew, the kind of man who fixes everybody else's car and never asks for a thing. His hours got cut when the season went slow, and across one brutal summer the electric bill quietly doubled — because you do not turn off the air conditioning when there's a baby in the house on breathing treatments and it is ninety-eight degrees outside. So he fell behind. Seven hundred dollars behind, with a yellow disconnection notice taped to the door. And he told no one, because in his mind being behind on the light bill is the same as failing his family, and a man does not say that out loud. Then the phone rang. The caller ID said it was the electric company. The voice knew his address, sounded official, and said the power would be shut off in thirty minutes unless he paid the full balance right now, with a prepaid card, from the gas station down the street. And a good father, panicked, with a baby who needs the AC, grabbed his keys.

He was thirty minutes from being robbed. That is the part that has to be said plainly, and I will say it again before I am done: no real utility company takes gift cards, and no real utility gives you thirty minutes. A power company sends its notices by mail, with due process and time to respond and real ways to get help — it does not call in a panic demanding a prepaid card in the next half hour. That call was a thief who knows exactly how a proud father reacts when you threaten his children's comfort. The only thing standing between this man and an empty bank account was one piece of information nobody handed him: that the call was a lie, and there are real doors.

And there are doors. Real ones. Doors nobody names for a working family the week the bill comes due, because who wants to admit at coffee hour that they're behind on the light bill.

Start with the first thing he needs to hear: falling behind on a utility bill after your hours got cut is not a moral failure. Running the AC so a baby can breathe in a heat wave is not irresponsible — it is a father doing his job. He is not bad with money. He is a working man whose income dropped through no fault of his own, in the exact season the bill was always going to spike. Wanting to keep the lights on without asking for help is not pride to be ashamed of — but there is no version of protecting his kids that requires him to face this alone, in the dark, with a thief on the phone.

So here is the door almost no one names for him plainly: there is real help for the energy bill, and there are legal protections for the shutoff. There is a program — LIHEAP, the energy assistance program — that helps working families with the heating bill and the summer cooling bill and the past-due balance, and you apply for it through the local community action agency, for free. In a heat wave there are often protections that restrict when the power can be shut off at all. And this is the one that matters most in his house: if someone in the home relies on electric medical equipment — a baby on a nebulizer counts — a doctor's certificate can protect the power from being disconnected. He does not know any of that. He thinks the notice on the door is the end of the conversation. It is not. It is the beginning of one.

And there is help underneath the bill itself. The electric company has programs it does not advertise — a deferred-payment plan that spreads the past-due over months, budget billing that levels the bill across the whole year so July doesn't crush him, hardship and arrearage-forgiveness programs, a low-income rate discount — but you have to call and ask for them by name, because they are not offered at the door. There is weatherization assistance that will seal and insulate the apartment for free and drop the bill permanently. There is township general assistance and the community action agency and very likely an energy or benevolence fund in his own church. He is standing in front of a whole row of doors — energy assistance, cooling protections, a medical certificate, the utility's own payment plans, weatherization, township and church help, and one call to 211 that maps it all — and he is reaching for his car keys to go pay a scammer, because nobody ever told him a single one of those doors was his to open.

But hear this warning before he pays anyone a dime, because the thieves hunt the people who fall behind. The disconnection scam is one of the most common there is, and it works because it targets panic. Hear it plainly, and I will say it again at the end: a real utility never demands payment by gift card, prepaid card, crypto, or a wire transfer, and never gives you thirty minutes to decide. Anyone who does is a thief wearing the power company's name. Hang up, and call the number printed on your actual paper bill — never the number the caller gives you. There are fake "government energy relief" texts and websites that exist only to harvest a Social Security number or a bank account, or to charge a fee for an application that is free — LIHEAP costs nothing to apply for, and nobody legitimate charges you to fill it out. There are the door-to-door and robocall pitches to "switch your energy supplier and lock in a lower rate" that quietly raise the bill instead, and the stranger who asks to "see your bill" so he can copy the account number — you never have to switch suppliers to get help, and your account number is not for a man at your door. The rule is flat: no gift cards, no thirty-minute deadline, the real application is free, never wire money or pay a "balance" to anyone who called you, and always call the number on your paper bill.

So he almost bought the gift cards. Not because he is foolish — foolish is not the word for a man who works every shift they'll give him and fixes the whole block's cars for free. Not because he is bad with money — a cut in hours during a heat wave is not a budgeting mistake, it is arithmetic that stopped working. He almost paid a thief because nobody ever walked him to the doors, and the one voice that finally spoke up about his light bill was the one built to rob him.

Here is the thing about the man across town. Same story. Hours cut, same heat wave, same yellow notice on the door, and yes — the same scam call, spoofing the same company, demanding the same gift cards. But a friend had already sat with him on the porch a week earlier and said: "Listen. There's an energy assistance program — apply through the community action agency, it's free. The electric company has a payment plan and a hardship program, but you have to call and ask for them. Get a note from the baby's doctor — they can't shut off the power on a child with a nebulizer. And if anybody ever calls demanding gift cards in thirty minutes, that's a scam every time — hang up and call the number on your bill." So when that call came, he laughed and hung up. He applied for the help. He got on a payment plan. He kept the lights and the air on through the worst of August, and he never gave a thief a dime. He was not smarter than the man in our pew. He did not love his kids more. He had one friend who knew the doors were there and named them before the crisis hit. One father kept his money and his lights. The other almost lost both in a gas-station parking lot. The whole difference was somebody who said "that call is a lie, and here are the doors," and a silence where nobody ever did.

The man in our pew can be met before he grabs his keys. Not with a lecture about how he should have budgeted. With a hand on the shoulder and the plain truth that he has protections, and there are doors, and keeping the lights on does not require handing his family's money to a stranger on the phone.

Because this is where a tool like AI actually meets a man like him — quietly, at the kitchen table after the kids are down, when he finally admits to himself how far behind he is — doing the part that the shame and the strange words and the not-knowing-where-to-start have been blocking. It can be the patient someone that explains what "LIHEAP" and "the cooling program" and "budget billing" and "a deferred-payment plan" and "arrearage forgiveness" and "a medical shutoff certificate" actually mean, in plain words, as many times as he needs, and never once makes a working father feel like less of a man for asking. It can help him figure out which programs might exist and where to apply in the morning. It can help him put words to the ask, so when he calls the electric company he knows to say "hardship program" and "payment plan" and "medical certificate" instead of just apologizing. And it can help him recognize the gift-card call and the fake-supplier pitch for exactly what they are, before they take one more thing from him.

Not to be the one who decides anything. Let that be plain. The machine does not approve him for assistance, and it does not get to promise the bill will be paid or forgiven — that belongs to the agency and the utility, on their forms, not a chatbot's guess. It does not set his rate and it is not the power company, not the community action agency, not the caseworker. And it must never, ever be fed his Social Security number, his utility account number, or a bank or card number — a public AI tool is not the place for a single line of a family's private information. It is only the patient someone that takes a scary, word-thick, where-do-I-even-start thing and makes it walkable, so that a working father in a heat wave does not hand seven hundred dollars to a thief for no reason at all.

That is the gap. It was never that the man across town loved his kids more, or was better with money, or was less proud. Same cut hours. Same heat wave. Same notice on the same door. Same scam call. It is that one father had a friend who said "you have protections, there are doors, and that call is a lie," and the other was left alone on the porch with a threat and a set of car keys. Not less of a provider. Not less faithful. Just never told the doors were his.

A word of care, plainly, and I will say the important parts twice. This is general navigation, not financial or legal advice, not an endorsement of any utility or program, and not a decision about who qualifies or what any program pays. What LIHEAP and the electric company actually cover, and who is eligible, belong to the community action agency and the utility — not to a machine and not to a guess. AI tools can be confidently wrong, so never trust an amount or an eligibility rule one gives you without confirming it with the official agency (the community action agency, and the utility at the number on your paper bill) and 211. And never put a Social Security number, a utility account number, or a bank or card number into a public AI tool. Hear the heart of it twice, because it is built for a family in exactly this spot: a real utility never demands payment by gift card and never gives you thirty minutes — that call is always a scam — and the real assistance application is free. Route the real help to real, trusted places: the community action agency for the energy assistance program; the electric and gas company at the number on your paper bill for a payment plan, budget billing, and a hardship program; the baby's doctor for a medical certificate; township general assistance and your own church's benevolence fund for the gap; and 211 or 211.org to find all of it in one call.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Dolton. Robbins. Chicago Heights. Matteson. South Holland. Not a webinar. A room. Real people. A real working father, taught how to make the same tool the family across town already knew how to use sit with him at the kitchen table, explain the words nobody explained, help him find the assistance and the payment plan and the medical protection, and name the gift-card scam for the lie it is — so that he can keep the lights and the air on for his kids and hold onto every dollar he has, instead of driving to a gas station in a panic while a thief waits on the line.

The faithful men in our churches are not falling into utility debt because they are irresponsible. They are falling into it because the summer bill doubled when their hours got cut, and nobody ever told them there were protections and doors — and panic is the worst possible time to answer the phone.

We are here to sit with them before they grab the keys.
