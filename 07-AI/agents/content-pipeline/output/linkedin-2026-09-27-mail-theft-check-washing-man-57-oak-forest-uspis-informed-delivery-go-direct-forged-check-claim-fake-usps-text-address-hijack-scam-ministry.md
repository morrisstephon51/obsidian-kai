# Post #394 — WHOLLY NEW DOMAIN: *THE LITTLE RED FLAG ON THE MAILBOX — AND THE FOUR MONTHS A MAN SPENT BELIEVING HIS OLDEST CUSTOMERS HAD TURNED ON HIM* — HOW A 57-YEAR-OLD ONE-MAN LAWN-AND-SNOW OPERATION WITH FORTY ACCOUNTS AND A PAPER LEDGER LOST $264 THAT CAME BACK AS $6,240, HOW A $45 CHECK FROM A SEVENTY-NINE-YEAR-OLD WIDOW BECAME $1,900, WHAT MAIL THEFT AND CHECK WASHING ACTUALLY ARE, THE FREE DOORS NOBODY HANDED HIM (THE POSTAL INSPECTORS, INFORMED DELIVERY, DIRECT DEPOSIT, A WRITTEN CLAIM TO HIS OWN BANK), THE SIX PREDATORS WHO ALL NEED THE SAME ONE CONDITION — AND WHY THE REAL THING THEY STOLE WAS NOT THE MONEY

**Centered on:** BROTHER LOVELL SANDIFER, 57, Oak Forest, IL — twenty-two years a one-man lawn-care and snow-removal operation, a truck, a trailer, two mowers, a plow blade he rebuilds every October, and **forty accounts** he has mostly never advertised for, because in twenty-two years his business has grown exactly one way: somebody's sister told somebody's mother. He cuts, edges, hauls, mulches in spring, does leaves in November, and plows all winter at four in the morning so that a woman who works at the hospital can get her car out of her own driveway at five-thirty. He is not on an app. He does not take cards. He runs the whole enterprise the way his father ran a business and the way most of his customers still prefer: **he mails a paper invoice, and they mail him back a paper check.** He has a folding table in his basement, a metal box of index cards — one card per account, in pencil — and a bank account he has had at the same branch since 1996. He has never bounced a check on purpose in his life. In April he mailed a check for **$264.18** to cover his truck note, dropped it in the blue collection box outside the strip mall on a Sunday evening on his way home from service, and eleven days later that check cleared his account for **six thousand two hundred forty dollars.** Same handwriting. His handwriting. And that was the smaller half of what happened to him.

**Faith anchor:** SISTER WILLENE McCLENDON, 68, of Zion Hill Missionary Baptist Church in Oak Forest — **thirty-one years the church's financial secretary.** She is the one who counts it, records it, reconciles it, and mails it. Every light bill, every gas bill, every insurance premium, the roof loan, the van payment, the flowers, the musician, the check to the association — thirty-one years of Zion Hill's money has left that building in an envelope with Willene McClendon's handwriting on it. She keeps ledgers nobody has ever asked her to keep. She can tell you what the church paid for heat in February 2009. She is not an accountant and has never called herself one; she is a retired school-district payroll clerk who came home, joined the finance committee, and never left, and there are three generations of Zion Hill members who have never once wondered whether the money was right, because it always was. **And three years ago a check she mailed for nine thousand four hundred twelve dollars and sixty-six cents came back with somebody else's name on it** — and for the seven weeks it took to sort out, some good people in her own church looked at her a half-second too long. That is the thing she carries. It is why she recognized what was happening to Lovell Sandifer before he did.

**Resources — the real doors, free or low-cost, no fee to a stranger, ever:**

- **THE FREE FACT THAT COSTS NOTHING AND CHANGES THE MOST: the little red flag on your mailbox is an advertisement.** Raising that flag tells your carrier there is outgoing mail in the box. It also tells anybody driving slowly down your street at eleven in the morning that **there is probably a check in that box.** You do not need a program, a form, or a dollar to stop doing that. Hand outgoing mail to your carrier, or take it inside a post office. That is the whole first door and it is free.
- **THE U.S. POSTAL INSPECTION SERVICE — the federal police for mail crime. Free to report, and this is the office that actually works these cases: uspis.gov or 1-877-876-2455 (1-877-876-2455 is the USPIS hotline).** Stealing mail is a **federal crime**, and a stolen check taken out of the mail is their jurisdiction, not just your bank's problem. Report it even when you think it is too small, even when you have already been made whole, and even when you are embarrassed. Patterns on a route are how boxes get moved, locks get changed, and people get caught — and your one report is somebody else's pattern. Also tell your **local police** so you have a report number, and tell **your own post office** which box you mailed it from and roughly when.
- **USPS INFORMED DELIVERY — free, and there are two reasons to sign up, and the second one is the one nobody tells you (informeddelivery.usps.com).** First: it emails you images of the outside of letter-size mail coming to your address, so you know what *should* have arrived, which is how you find out a statement or a benefit letter never made it. Second, and this is the part that matters: **enrolling claims your own address.** If you never sign up, somebody else can try to. Sign up for your address before a stranger does, and help your elders sign up for theirs. Free.
- **KILL THE PAPER ON EVERY DOLLAR YOU CAN — this is the structural fix, not a tip.** A check that never travels cannot be stolen. Federal benefits (Social Security, SSI, VA, and others) should arrive by **direct deposit into your own account, or on a Direct Express card — never to anybody else's account.** The Treasury program for making that switch is **Go Direct: godirect.gov or 1-800-333-1795**, and the rules on paper benefit checks have been tightening for years, so confirm the current process with SSA at **1-800-772-1213** or ssa.gov. Then do the same on the way out: pay bills by direct debit, your bank's own bill-pay, or in person. And ask your bank to switch you to **electronic statements**, because a paper bank statement in an unlocked box is a map of everything you own.
- **IF A CHECK OF YOURS WAS ALTERED OR FORGED: tell your bank IMMEDIATELY, IN PERSON, AND IN WRITING — and understand that the clock is the enemy.** Under commercial law a bank generally may not charge your account for a check bearing a **forged signature** or for an amount that has been **materially altered** — but your rights depend on **reporting promptly**, and the deadlines run from when your statement was made available to you, and they are short. Do not wait for next month. Go in, ask for a **written check-fraud claim form and a forgery/alteration affidavit**, ask for a **copy of the front and back of the cancelled check** (the back shows where it was deposited, and that is the evidence), get the name of the person you spoke to and the date, and **ask for a provisional credit timeline in writing.** If they tell you no, that is not the end — that is when you call free legal aid.
- **CLOSE THAT ACCOUNT NUMBER AND GET A NEW ONE. Yes, it is a genuine pain. Do it anyway.** A washed check is a permanent photograph of your bank's routing number, your account number, your address, and your signature, and it is now in somebody's hands forever. Changing the account number is the only thing that makes that photograph worthless. Ask your banker to walk the autopay list with you so nothing breaks, and ask what protections they offer on the new account — some banks offer alerts on every check that clears, some offer check-image review, and some services (like positive pay) may only be available on business accounts. **Ask what is available to you specifically.**
- **FREEZE YOUR CREDIT AT ALL THREE BUREAUS — free, by federal law — and start the identity-theft file if any personal mail is missing.** Equifax, Experian, TransUnion; and **IdentityTheft.gov or 1-877-438-4338 (FTC)** to get a recovery plan and an Identity Theft Report. Stolen mail is not only about one check; it is raw material. A statement, a new debit card, a pre-approved offer, and a tax document out of one box is enough to become you.
- **WATCH FOR THE TWO LETTERS PEOPLE THROW AWAY.** If anyone files a **change of address** on your address, USPS generally mails a **validation or customer notification letter** to that address — so an unexpected letter about an address change you did not request is an alarm, not junk. Same with any letter confirming a new online account in your name. Open the boring mail. And when you travel, use **free USPS Hold Mail** rather than letting five days pile up in a box.

## I. Forty accounts, forty index cards

You have to understand how the business actually works, because the business is the reason the theft was invisible for four months.

Lovell Sandifer has forty accounts. Most of them are people. A few of them are a small property manager and a dentist's office and a church. He does not have a website. He has a laminated sign on the trailer with a phone number on it, and that number has been the same since flip phones.

At the end of each month he sits at the folding table in the basement with his metal box of index cards and he writes out invoices by hand — not because he can't use a computer, but because his customers are, on average, sixty-eight years old, and a paper invoice in a mailbox is a thing they trust and a thing they remember. He mails forty envelopes. And over the following three weeks, thirty-odd envelopes come back to his curbside mailbox with checks in them, and he writes the date and the amount on the index card in pencil, and he drives the deposits to the branch on Cicero once a week.

That is a good business. It has fed three children. It paid for a used Silverado twice and a transmission once and it put his oldest daughter's dorm deposit down in August.

It is also, as of about three years ago, a business built entirely on top of a system that quietly stopped being safe, and nobody told him, because who would tell him? There is no letter that comes to your house explaining that the mail is different now. There is no form. His customers didn't know. His bank never said a word. His carrier knew — his carrier knew a great deal — but a carrier is not allowed to be your financial advisor and has a route to finish.

So here is the deficit, and I want to name it the way this series always names it, because it is never what people assume. **Lovell Sandifer is not a careless man.** He is a man who has never in his life been late on an obligation and who reconciles a metal box of index cards by hand. What he did not have was one free fact.

The fact is this: **the little red flag on his mailbox is an advertisement.**

## II. The four months he thought his people had turned on him

It started as a trickle and he read it wrong, the way anybody would.

January, a customer on the 153rd block didn't pay. February, two more. By March, the pencil column on five index cards had gone quiet, and Lovell did what a small businessman does — he assumed the thing that small businessmen have every reason to assume. **Money's tight. People are stretched. The gas bill came in high this winter and the lawn man waits.**

He is not a hard man about it. He carried people through 2020 without a word. So he waited, and he sent second notices, and he put a little handwritten line at the bottom — *no rush, just checking my records* — and he kept cutting.

But by April it was nine accounts and roughly **twenty-eight hundred and eighty dollars**, and something had shifted in him that he is not proud of and told me about with his hands on his knees.

He had started to think differently about his own people.

He caught himself doing arithmetic about a woman whose leaves he had raked for eleven years. He noticed that he pulled onto a certain street with his jaw set. He stopped servicing one account entirely — a man on Kostner who was four months out — and he did it by just not showing up, which is not how Lovell Sandifer does anything, and he knew it, and he did it anyway because he did not want to have the conversation.

And in the third week of April he had the conversation. He stood on a screen porch and said to a seventy-nine-year-old widow, in a voice he says he can still hear, *"Miss Cleotha, I gotta ask you about November and February."*

Now hold that image, because this is the part of mail theft that no pamphlet anywhere describes, and it is the reason this post exists.

**A thief who steals your money takes one thing. A thief who steals your mail takes your money and then hands you a reason to distrust the people who love you.**

For four months Lovell Sandifer believed his oldest customers had turned on him. He was wrong about every single one of them. Nine people had written him nine checks, sealed nine envelopes, put nine stamps on them, and dropped them in the mail, and every one of those checks had been pulled out of a box — some out of *their* boxes with the flag up, at least two out of the blue collection box outside the strip mall, and some, he eventually learned, straight out of **his own curbside box**, which sat at the end of a driveway on a corner lot, a hundred feet from a street anybody could roll down at eleven in the morning.

He was not owed money by his people. He was being robbed. And the robbery came with a free gift: a completely false story about the character of thirty years of neighbors.

That is the real payload. Not the $2,880. **The suspicion.**

## III. Miss Cleotha Lumpkin's screen door

Here is what Miss Cleotha Lumpkin, 79, was living inside of while Lovell was building his case against her.

She had written him a check for forty-five dollars for the fall cleanup. She wrote it at her kitchen table on a Tuesday, in the good pen, and she recorded it in the paper register she has kept in the same drawer since 1979 — the one with the entries in that careful slanted hand that older women were taught and nobody teaches anymore. She put it in the box and put the flag up.

Weeks later the money hadn't come out. So she assumed she had made a mistake. She looked at her register and found the entry and couldn't square it, and she wrote him **another** check, and she made a small note to herself, and she started, in the quietest way, **to wonder about her own mind.**

That is what she was carrying when Lovell came up on the porch. She was seventy-nine years old and she was afraid she was slipping.

She wasn't slipping. Her second check had been stolen out of the mail and **washed**, and it cleared somewhere for **nineteen hundred dollars.** Her first one was simply gone — never cashed, probably pitched, because a forty-five-dollar check to a lawn man is a poor score and thieves sort their haul like anybody else. And the only reason anybody ever found out is that the bank statement she does not really read had a number on it she couldn't explain, and her daughter was at the house in May for a birthday and looked at it.

So she lost forty-five dollars, then nineteen hundred dollars, then four months of trusting her own memory — and then a man she had known for eleven years stood at her screen door and asked her about it in a voice with an edge on it.

She told him about the register. She got it out of the drawer and showed him. And Lovell Sandifer stood in that doorway and understood two things in the same second: that he had just accused an innocent woman, and that if it had happened to her it had happened to all nine of them, and therefore it had happened to **him**, and then he remembered a check for $264.18 and a truck note and a Sunday evening and a blue box outside a strip mall.

He drove home and pulled his own statement and found it. **$6,240.00.** Eleven days after he mailed it.

## IV. Sister McClendon, and the $9,412.66 the church mailed on a Sunday night

He did not go to the bank first. He went to Willene McClendon, because at Zion Hill if it has a number on it you go to Sister McClendon, and because he needed somebody to tell him he wasn't crazy.

She listened for about ninety seconds and then she said, *"Baby, sit down. I know exactly what this is."*

Three years earlier, Willene had mailed the gas bill. Nine thousand four hundred twelve dollars and sixty-six cents — a winter catch-up on a building with a fellowship hall and a sanctuary and bad windows. She wrote the check, she recorded it in the ledger, she put it in the envelope, and on her way home from Sunday evening service she dropped it in the blue collection box outside the Jewel, the way she had dropped a thousand pieces of church mail for thirty-one years.

In January a shutoff notice came. The gas company said they had never been paid.

Willene McClendon, who does not make mistakes about money, pulled the ledger and the statement and found that the check **had** cleared, for **exactly the right amount**, on time. The amount matched. The date was reasonable. Her ledger and her statement agreed with each other perfectly.

**The payee didn't.** The thief had left the amount alone and changed only the name — which is a real and common variant and is the single most instructive detail in this entire post, so let me say it plainly:

**A washed check does not have to be for the wrong amount. When only the payee's name is changed, the number in your register matches the number on your statement, everything reconciles, and you will not find it by balancing your account. You find it when the company you were paying says you never paid them.**

Now here is why Willene is the anchor of this post and not just a witness.

The bank made the church whole. It took **seven weeks.** And in those seven weeks, a trustee asked — politely, reasonably, in a meeting, in front of people — whether he could see her records "just to be thorough." Another member asked twice whether the check had been "handled by anybody else." Nobody accused her of anything. Nobody had to. She went home after that meeting and typed a resignation letter, and she had it in an envelope, and the only reason Zion Hill still has the finance committee it has is that the pastor put his hand flat on the table and said, *"Sit down, Sister. You didn't do this. Somebody did this to you."*

She kept the letter. She showed it to Lovell. It is three sentences long and it is the saddest document in this story, because the church lost nothing in the end and she lost something the bank has no procedure for.

*"They took nine thousand dollars from the church, and the church got it back. What they took from me, the bank could not put back — because it wasn't money. It was being trusted."*

And then she said the thing that made her the right person in the room:

*"So when you came in here talking about how your people had stopped paying you, I already knew what I was listening to. I've heard it from the other side."*

## V. What they are actually stealing, because it is not the money

Everybody thinks the check is the loot. The check is not the loot. **The check is a photograph of your bank account.**

Look at one. On a single piece of paper, in your own handwriting, traveling through a system you do not control, you have printed: your full name, your home address, the name of your bank, your **routing number**, your **account number**, the current check number so a thief knows your sequence, and — at the bottom right — **your signature.** A cancelled check also shows the back, which is why a cancelled check is evidence.

That is why the fix is not a better envelope, and it is why this post cannot end with "be careful." Once one of your checks has been washed, **your account number is compromised forever**, and it stays compromised after the bank refunds you, and it stays compromised after the case closes. There is no un-ringing it. There is only a new account number.

The washing itself is chemistry and it is depressingly simple: most ballpoint ink sits on the surface of the paper and a common solvent lifts it, while the printed parts of the check — your name, your account number, the bank's name — are done differently and stay. So the thief keeps the parts that make it real and rewrites the parts that make it theirs. The countermeasure is almost insulting in its cheapness: **write checks in a gel pen or a permanent black ink**, because gel ink soaks into the fibers and does not lift the same way. That is a two-dollar fix for a nineteen-hundred-dollar problem, and not one person in this story had ever heard it.

And the harvesting is not sophisticated either. Some of it is a hand in an unlocked curbside box with the flag up. Some of it is **fishing** — a weighted line lowered into a blue collection box. And some of it is a stolen **arrow key**, the key that opens collection boxes and apartment cluster boxes across a whole route area; those keys get stolen, they get sold, and the Postal Service has been working on replacing them with electronic locks for exactly this reason. Which means the sentence people say to comfort themselves — *"but I put it in the official blue box"* — is the one sentence that does not help.

**The blue box is not a safe. It is a mailbox.**

Mail dropped in after the last posted collection time sits there overnight. A blue box on a quiet corner after dark, with a church's gas bill in it, is not a vault; it is a container with a slot in it. The free fix, the one Lovell Sandifer now says to strangers in the Jewel parking lot like a man handing out tracts:

**If it's got your account number on it, it doesn't go in a box. It goes to a counter, or it doesn't go at all.**

Hand it to your carrier. Or walk it inside a post office and hand it to a human being at the retail window. That is the whole rule. It costs nothing, it takes ten extra minutes, and it is the difference between the two halves of this story.

And say the rest of it out loud too, because the dignity in this post lives here: **he did not get robbed because he's old-fashioned.** He got robbed because the mail is a two-hundred-year-old system that we keep asking to carry twenty-first-century money, and nobody sent out a notice when the arithmetic on that changed.

## VI. Six predators, and all six need the same one condition

The first two are not really cons. They are the engine. The last four are the people who come for you after, and one of them is not a person at all.

**1. THE WASH ITSELF.** Your check, lifted out of the mail, solvent, new payee, new amount, deposited somewhere far away. Tells and countermeasures: gel pen or permanent ink; never leave blank space in the amount line or the payee line; don't raise the flag; don't use the blue box after the last collection; **and reconcile your statement every single month, looking at the payee and not only the amount** — because Willene McClendon's ledger balanced perfectly while the church was being robbed.

**2. MAIL FISHING AND THE ARROW KEY.** The reason "I used the official box" is not a defense. If your outgoing mail has to leave from a box, put it in before the last collection of the day, not after. Better: the inside counter.

**3. THE "USPS" TEXT.** *"USPS: your package could not be delivered due to an incomplete address. Update here to avoid return — $1.99."* You tap it, you enter your address and your card to pay a fee smaller than a soda, and you have just handed a stranger your card number and confirmed that a real person lives at a real address. **Tells: USPS is not going to text you about a package you never shipped and never tracked; a real delivery problem does not get solved by a $1.99 link; the web address is always subtly wrong.** And let me carve this carefully, because this series has already covered its cousins: this is **not** the Illinois Tollway "unpaid toll, pay today or lose your plates" text and it is **not** the Secretary of State "your license is revoked, pay now" text that earlier posts walked through. Same genus — a mass text impersonating a government agency and charging a small fee — but a different agency, a different pretext, and a completely different set of doors, because the fix here runs through the **Postal Inspection Service and your card issuer**, not the Tollway. Forward it to the FTC and IC3 and delete it. Never tap.

**4. THE CHANGE-OF-ADDRESS HIJACK.** A thief files a forwarding order on your address, and then your whole mail stream — bank statements, the replacement debit card, the SSA letter, the tax form — walks into their hands legally, carried by a federal employee, for months. The free countermeasure is a letter you are currently in the habit of throwing away: USPS generally mails a **validation letter** to an address when a change is filed on it. **Open the boring mail.** A letter confirming an address change you did not request is not junk; it is an alarm going off.

**5. THE INFORMED DELIVERY HIJACK.** Informed Delivery is free and it emails you pictures of your incoming letter mail. Which means if you have not claimed your own address, somebody else can try to — and then a stranger gets a daily photographic preview of everything arriving at your house, and knows exactly which morning to come by. **Sign up for your own address. Then sit down with your mother and sign up for hers.** This is the most underused free door in this entire post and it takes about ten minutes.

**6. THE SECOND WAVE — and then the quiet one.** Within days of a real theft, the vultures arrive. There is the **"check fraud recovery specialist"** who wants four hundred dollars up front to "pursue your funds," which is a fee for filing a claim you can file yourself for free. There is the **bank-imposter call** — the most dangerous call in this whole post — *"Sir, we've detected fraud on your account, we need to move your money to a secure account while we investigate,"* which is not a rescue, it is the second robbery wearing the first robbery's uniform. **Your bank will never call you and ask you to move your money somewhere. Hang up and call the number on the back of your own card or walk into the branch.** There is the fake "USPIS" website that wants a fee to file a mail-theft report that is free. And there is the sympathetic stranger on social media who saw your post about it.

And then there is the sixth one, which took more from these three people than the other five combined.

**THE QUIET THIEF IS SUSPICION.** Read back what actually happened. Lovell spent four months believing nine good people had stopped paying him, and acted on it, and hurt one of them at her own screen door. Miss Cleotha Lumpkin spent four months believing she was losing her mind. Willene McClendon spent seven weeks being looked at, and typed a resignation letter, and has kept it in a drawer for three years.

Nobody stole those months. Those months were a **byproduct** — and they were free to the thief.

Now notice the structure, because it is the same structure this series finds every time: **every one of the first five predators requires exactly one condition to work, and it is that you don't say anything.** The recovery-fee man needs you to be too ashamed to walk into your own branch. The bank-imposter needs you alone on the phone at seven in the evening. The change-of-address thief needs you to throw away a boring letter without reading it. And the wash itself needs you to not report it, so that a route with a soft box on it stays soft.

Which means the countermeasure to all six is one behavior, and it is not a product: **say it out loud, early, to somebody, in writing.**

## VII. What he actually did, in order

This is the part to photocopy. Order matters, and the reason order matters is that some of these doors close.

**1. He went to his bank in person, that week, and put it in writing.** Not a phone call. He walked into the branch on Cicero with the statement, asked for the **front and back copy of the cancelled check**, filled out a written **forgery and alteration affidavit**, wrote down the name of the person who took it and the date, and asked one question that turned out to be the important one: *"What is your deadline for me, in writing?"* Because the deadlines in check fraud run from when your statement was made available, and they are short, and "I'll come back next month" is exactly how people lose a claim they would have won. **Five weeks later the $6,240 was reversed.** The $105 in overdraft fees that the theft caused behind it took a second, separate fight, and he got $70 of it back.

**2. He closed the account number and opened a new one.** He hated it. It broke four autopays and he spent a Saturday on hold. He did it because the photograph of his account was already out there and always will be. His banker sat with him and walked the list so nothing bounced twice.

**3. He reported it to the Postal Inspection Service — and to his own post office, and to the village police.** He did it even though the money was already coming back, because by then he knew about nine other people. He told them which blue box, which curbside boxes, and roughly what times. He is a man with a route-based knowledge of forty streets, and he wrote it out like a work order.

**4. He froze his credit at all three bureaus and started a file at IdentityTheft.gov.** Free. Because a stolen envelope is never just one check.

**5. He signed up for Informed Delivery — and then he did it for eleven other people.** Miss Cleotha first, at her kitchen table, on his phone, in about nine minutes.

**6. He called all nine customers, and this is the step most people skip.** Not to collect. To tell them what had actually happened and to ask them to check their own statements — which is how two more washed checks got found, one of them for eleven hundred dollars belonging to a man who had no idea. **He un-did the story.** He called the man on Kostner he had quietly stopped servicing and said the true sentence, which was *I thought something about you that wasn't true, and I'm sorry,* and then he went and cut that man's grass.

**7. He went back to Miss Cleotha Lumpkin's porch.** He did not bring a form. He brought an apology, and then he sat at her table and helped her write to her bank about the nineteen hundred dollars, and drove her to the branch, and stood there while she said it out loud to a stranger, which at seventy-nine is not a small thing. **The nineteen hundred came back. The forty-five never did** — reported far too late, nothing to work with.

**8. He changed how the business runs.** The invoices still go out on paper, because his customers are his customers. But now the bottom of every invoice has three lines on it that he wrote himself: *you can pay by check, but please hand it to your mail carrier or take it inside the post office — not the blue box, and please don't put your flag up;* *write your check in a dark gel pen;* and *if this invoice is your second notice, call me, because it may mean your mail is being stolen and not that you forgot.* He also set up his own bills on direct payment and stopped mailing checks entirely. **Forty people got a free fact printed at the bottom of a lawn bill.** That is a public health campaign run by one man with a folding table.

**9. He told the church.** Which is how there came to be a table.

## VIII. The honest caveats, because this post cannot promise anybody their money back

This series does not get to skip this part.

**The bank usually makes you whole on a forged or altered check — usually, not always, and not fast.** Lovell waited five weeks with a hole in his account. The law gives real protection here and that is genuinely good news, but it is conditioned on prompt reporting, the deadlines are shorter than people assume, and a bank can and sometimes does deny a claim. If you are denied, that is not the end of the road — that is the moment you call CARPLS or Illinois Legal Aid Online, free. But nobody reading this should walk away thinking a stolen check is automatically refunded. **Miss Cleotha's first check is gone and it is gone because of time.**

**The money came back. The eleven days did not.** When the $6,240 cleared, Lovell's account went negative, and the auto-payment for his **commercial liability insurance** bounced behind it, and his policy lapsed for **eleven days**. His two largest accounts require proof of insurance. For eleven days, a twenty-two-year-old business could not legally do the work it exists to do, and reinstating the policy cost him a fee of about three hundred and forty dollars that nobody has ever refunded and nobody is going to. **That is the part of check fraud that no reimbursement covers: the things that fell over while your own money was missing.** If that is where you are right now — a bill that went past due, a utility shutoff notice, a lapse — call **2-1-1** and say it plainly, because that specific gap is what 2-1-1 is for.

**Informed Delivery is a smoke detector, not a lock.** It shows you the outside of letter-size mail. It does not show you everything, it does not stop anybody, and it will not tell you who took it. What it does is turn "I wonder if something got lost" into "something was taken on Tuesday," and in a case where deadlines decide everything, knowing on Tuesday is worth a great deal. Do not mistake it for protection.

**Direct deposit fixes incoming money and does nothing about outgoing checks** — and there is a harder version of that sentence. This post keeps saying *stop mailing checks*, and for some people that advice assumes a bank account they do not have. If you are unbanked, the door for you is the one an earlier post in this series walked through at length — a second-chance, Bank On-certified account with no overdraft — and it is a real door, but it is a different door, and I am not going to pretend a man cashing checks at a currency exchange can simply switch to bill-pay this afternoon.

**And the apology.** Let me be straight, the way this series has been straight in every one of the last several posts. There is no program in this post, no agency, no hotline, and no reimbursement that un-says what Lovell Sandifer said to a seventy-nine-year-old widow in her own doorway in April. He got the nineteen hundred dollars back for her. He cuts her grass now for nothing, which she fusses about every single time. The bank reversed $6,240 and the postal inspectors have a report on a route. **Nobody has a procedure for the four months.** The only thing that was ever in his control was whether the next forty people got the fact for free, and that is what he did with it.

## X. The first Saturday, and a folding table by the inside counter

Lovell Sandifer asked Willene McClendon for two things: a folding table and permission.

They set it up in the vestibule at Zion Hill on a Saturday morning, next to the door everybody comes through. No sermon. No seminar. Nobody selling anything. On the table: a box of dark gel pens he bought himself for about nine dollars, a laptop open to Informed Delivery, a legal pad, and a piece of poster board with one sentence on it in marker, which is the rule and also the name:

**THE INSIDE COUNTER — if it's got your account number on it, it doesn't go in a box.**

Nineteen people sat down that first Saturday.

Eleven of them had put a check in a blue box in the last thirty days. **Four** of them, once somebody said the words out loud, went home and found something wrong on a statement — one a payee name that wasn't right, one a missing benefit check she had assumed was a government mix-up, and two who simply had never opened the envelope from the bank at all. A man of seventy-three said he had been waiting on a Social Security paper check for five weeks and had concluded that *they must have cut him off* — and Willene McClendon sat down with him and made two calls, and here is the sentence she said to him afterward that Lovell has now repeated in three other churches:

**"Nobody forgot you. Somebody stole you. There's a difference, and the difference is that this one we can do something about."**

Fourteen people signed up for Informed Delivery at that table. Six of them were signing up on behalf of a mother or an aunt. Two women took a gel pen, looked at it, and laughed in a way that was not really laughing, because a two-dollar pen against nineteen hundred dollars is funny until you do the arithmetic.

It is the first Saturday of every month now. Lovell runs it in the winter after he's done plowing, which means sometimes he shows up at ten in the morning having been awake since three, and Willene brings the ledger she doesn't need to bring and sits next to him, and when somebody comes in with that particular face — the one that has already decided this is their own fault — she is the one who talks to them.

She still has the resignation letter. She keeps it in the drawer with the ledgers, and she has started doing something with it: she shows it to people. Three sentences, typed, three years old, never sent. She puts it on the table in front of a person who is sitting there certain they are the only one this has ever happened to, and she says:

*"That's what it did to me, and I hadn't done one thing wrong in thirty-one years. Now. Tell me what they took."*

Miss Cleotha Lumpkin comes to the table too. She does not need anything. She sits at the end of it with her paper register from 1979 open on the tablecloth, and when a woman her age sits down and starts apologizing for being confused, Miss Cleotha turns the register around so she can see the careful slanted handwriting and says the thing that took her four months and nineteen hundred dollars to earn the right to say:

**"Baby, there was never a thing wrong with your mind. There was something wrong with your mailbox."**

Lovell keeps the cancelled check. The real one, the copy the bank gave him, front and back — $264.18 in his own handwriting, turned into $6,240.00 by a stranger who used his signature to do it. It is taped to the inside of the lid of the metal box with the forty index cards in it, so he sees it every month when he does the invoices.

He was asked why he keeps a thing like that where he has to look at it.

*"Because I want to remember that the handwriting is mine,"* he said. *"Everything true on that check, I wrote. And they still turned it into a lie. That's not carelessness, that's just paper. Paper can't protect you."*

And then, after a minute:

*"And because for four months I thought my people had turned on me, and they hadn't. Not one of them. That's the part I can't let myself forget — because that was free for him, and it damn near cost me everything."*

**The gap was never intelligence, and it was never character.** It was access — one free fact, held by a mail carrier and a bank's fraud department and nobody else, about a red flag on a box at the end of a driveway. A church is structurally able to close that gap in about nine minutes at a folding table, for the price of a pack of gel pens, and almost none of them are doing it.
