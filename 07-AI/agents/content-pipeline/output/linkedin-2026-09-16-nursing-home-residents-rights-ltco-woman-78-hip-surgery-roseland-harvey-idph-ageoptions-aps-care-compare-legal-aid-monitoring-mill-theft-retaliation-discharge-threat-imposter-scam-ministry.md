# Post #336

---

Her mother's call light stayed on for fifty-one minutes.

Lorraine is 78. Retired Roseland school cafeteria worker — thirty-one years at Fenger High School, every kid in that building knew her by name and her peach cobbler by reputation, and she was the kind of woman who stayed after her shift to make sure the cleanup was right. She had a hip replacement nine months ago, and complications from the surgery — infection, then a second procedure, then a slow recovery that the family tried to manage at home before admitting, in late spring, that they could not — put her in a skilled nursing facility in Harvey.

Patricia, her daughter, visits three times a week. She drives forty-five minutes each way after her shift at the postal sorting facility in Dolton and brings her mother the things she asks for: a specific brand of hard candy, the crossword puzzle books from the dollar store, a phone charger long enough to reach the bed. She does not tell her mother everything she has noticed since the move.

The call light. The aide who spoke to Lorraine the way you do not speak to a woman who raised four children and ran a cafeteria kitchen for three decades. The morning Lorraine was told, without any notice at all, that she was being moved to a different room — a smaller one, on a noisier hallway — because the facility needed her room for another patient. The care plan meeting that happened without Patricia in the room, where decisions were made about Lorraine's physical therapy schedule that Lorraine herself did not understand. The evening Lorraine's reading glasses went missing from her nightstand.

Patricia mentioned all of this to her supervisor at work — a woman named Gwen who had gone through something similar with her father three years earlier. Gwen asked one question: "Do you know about the ombudsman?"

Patricia did not know about the ombudsman.

---

**What Patricia learned:**

There is a federal law — the Nursing Home Reform Act, passed in 1987, built into the Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act, OBRA for short — that gives every resident of a certified nursing facility a set of enforceable rights. Not suggestions. Rights, with a grievance process, a designated advocacy program, and regulatory teeth behind them. Most residents — and most families — have never been told these rights exist.

**The right to be treated with dignity and respect.** Not as a clinical body to be turned and medicated on a schedule, but as a person with a name and a history and preferences about how they are spoken to. The aide who spoke harshly to Lorraine violated a federal standard. It can be reported. It must be documented.

**The right to know about care and treatment.** Every nursing facility must give residents and their family representatives advance notice of changes to their care plan — medications, therapy schedules, treatment approaches — and must involve the resident in the decisions. The care plan meeting that happened without Lorraine or Patricia was not legally sound. They could have requested to be present. They can request a new care plan meeting.

**The right to file a grievance.** Lorraine has the right to complain — about the call light, the aide, the room move, the glasses — without being punished for complaining. Retaliation is prohibited under federal and state law. A resident who is threatened or penalized for raising a concern has a reportable claim.

**The right to 30 days' notice before a room transfer.** The room move Lorraine was told about without warning was improper. Except in a narrow set of emergency circumstances, a nursing facility must give the resident at least 30 days' written notice before transferring her to a different room in the facility, and the notice must include the reason, the anticipated effective date, the right to appeal, and information about the ombudsman. Lorraine received none of that.

**The right to manage or control her own finances**, or to designate someone she trusts to do so — not the facility. If anyone at the facility is asking Lorraine to sign anything financial, Patricia needs to be present and needs to understand what is being signed.

**The right to visitors.** Any person Lorraine names — a pastor, a neighbor, a friend, a representative from an advocacy group — has the right to visit her in the facility during reasonable hours. That includes the ombudsman.

---

**What the Illinois Long-Term Care Ombudsman (LTCO) actually does — free:**

Every state is required by federal law to operate a Long-Term Care Ombudsman program. Illinois runs its through the Illinois Department on Aging. The ombudsman is an independent advocate — not employed by the facility, not contracted by the facility, not paid by the facility — whose entire job is to represent the interests of residents in nursing homes, assisted living facilities, and similar settings.

An ombudsman can visit Lorraine — in person, privately, without the facility's permission. Lorraine can request a visit herself, or Patricia can request one. The conversation is confidential. The ombudsman investigates complaints, helps residents and families understand their rights, works with facility administration to resolve problems, and escalates to the Illinois Department of Public Health or the Attorney General when a facility does not respond.

The ombudsman charges nothing. There is no monthly fee. There is no intake charge. There is no "evaluation" to purchase.

**State LTCO line (Illinois Department on Aging):** 1-800-252-8966 — available statewide.

**AgeOptions**, the Area Agency on Aging for suburban Cook County, manages the regional LTCO for the communities around Harvey. Their main line is 708-383-0258.

**The Illinois Department of Public Health Nursing Home Hotline** takes complaints about nursing facility conditions, staffing, care quality, and licensing violations: 800-252-4343. IDPH inspects certified nursing facilities and makes those inspection records public. Serious complaints can trigger an unannounced inspection.

**CMS Care Compare** at medicare.gov/care-compare shows the federal Five-Star Quality Rating for every Medicare-and-Medicaid-certified nursing facility in the country — overall rating, staffing rating, quality measures rating, and health inspection summaries. The inspection reports are posted in full. Lorraine's facility has a rating there. Patricia had not looked at it before moving her mother in. She looked the night after she talked to Gwen.

**Adult Protective Services for institutional settings** handles allegations of abuse, neglect, or financial exploitation of vulnerable adults in nursing facilities: 866-800-1409 (Illinois IDHS). If the reading glasses are a sign of something larger — if money has gone missing, if anyone is pressuring Lorraine about financial documents — this is the line.

**Legal Aid** organizations — Legal Assistance Foundation (LAF), the Center on Halsted Elder Law Project, CARPLS — provide free legal help to low-income seniors on nursing home issues, including discharge disputes, grievance procedures, and financial exploitation claims. Most do not charge an intake fee.

**The thing Patricia did not know, and the thing the facility did not tell her:**

When a nursing facility notifies a resident that she will be discharged or transferred — to a lower level of care, to the hospital, or out of the facility entirely — the resident has an automatic right to appeal. The facility must provide written notice that includes the specific reason for the discharge, the effective date, the appeals process, and contact information for the state LTCO. The discharge cannot happen — except in an emergency — while an appeal is pending.

Facilities do not always tell residents or families that this right exists. They sometimes communicate the decision in a way that sounds final and mandatory, when it is not. If Lorraine is ever told she must leave the facility, Patricia's first call is to the LTCO. Not to a moving company. Not to a fee-charging discharge coordinator. To the ombudsman.

This matters because some facilities use discharge threats as a pressure tactic when residents or families complain too much. The word for that is retaliation, it is explicitly prohibited under federal regulations at 42 CFR 483.10(j), and it is reportable to IDPH and the LTCO simultaneously.

**The warning before you pay $89 a month to anyone:**

The weeks after a parent moves into a nursing home are disorienting for families. You feel relieved and guilty in equal measure, you are watching for signs that you made the right call, and you are very willing to believe someone who tells you they can help you monitor what is happening when you are not there. That is the opening that fee-charging "nursing home monitoring services" walk through.

A company called Patricia around week three. It introduced itself as "Nursing Home Watch Partners" and said it offered a "family monitoring membership" — $89 a month — that included a dedicated advocate who would "check on" Lorraine, attend care plan meetings on the family's behalf, and "report back" on facility conditions. It sounded professional. It sounded like exactly what Patricia needed.

**Here is what the $89 a month would have bought her:** access to a process that already exists for free. The Long-Term Care Ombudsman visits residents at no charge. The LTCO attends care plan meetings as a resident's representative at no charge. IDPH inspects facilities and makes the reports public at no charge. CMS Care Compare publishes Five-Star ratings at no charge. Legal Aid attends discharge hearings on behalf of eligible clients at no charge. Nothing this company was offering was a service Patricia could not already get without paying a monthly fee.

This type of company is not illegal. It may do something — phone calls, a periodic checklist. But it is charging a family in a vulnerable moment for a layer of advocacy that the state has already built and funded and made available to every resident and family in Illinois, without a fee.

Do not pay a monthly monitoring fee before you have called the ombudsman.

**Beware any company that implies the ombudsman is difficult to access, slow, or ineffective** — and offers itself as a faster, more attentive alternative at a monthly price. The LTCO program in Illinois has staff and volunteers who visit facilities, know the administrators by name, and have regulatory standing to escalate complaints. A fee-charging monitoring service does not.

**Beware any "quality rating service"** that charges you to run a report on a nursing facility. CMS makes the full inspection records and Five-Star ratings available at no cost at medicare.gov/care-compare. Illinois IDPH publishes facility inspection reports. Any company charging you $25 or $50 to pull a "nursing home quality report" is reselling public information at a markup.

**Beware anyone at the facility** — staff, administration, or a "patient advocate" employed by the facility — who tells Lorraine what she can and cannot discuss with outside visitors, who attempts to be present during ombudsman visits, or who discourages Lorraine from calling family members after a complaint is raised. The ombudsman has the right to visit Lorraine privately. Lorraine has the right to make and receive phone calls privately. Interference with either of those is a reportable violation.

**Beware financial exploitation.** Missing personal items — glasses, jewelry, cash — are among the most common forms of nursing home theft, and they are underreported because residents are embarrassed, unsure whether they misplaced the item, or afraid of retaliation. If something of Lorraine's disappears, Patricia reports it: to the facility's administrator in writing, to IDPH, and to APS at 866-800-1409. She keeps a log with dates and descriptions.

**Beware the signature request.** If anyone at a nursing facility presents Lorraine with a document — a financial agreement, a change to her beneficiary designations, a power of attorney, an arbitration clause buried in an admission agreement — and asks her to sign without her family present and without time to review, the answer is to slow down. Residents can request time to have a family member, a lawyer, or the LTCO review any document before signing.

So if you are reading this and you are Patricia — if your mother is in a facility right now and you are driving forty-five minutes each way and lying awake wondering whether what you observed is something you are allowed to say out loud —

Her rights did not end at the door.

Lorraine has the right to be spoken to with dignity. She has the right to know what is in her care plan and to have a say in it. She has the right to a 30-day written notice before her room is moved. She has the right to file a grievance and not be punished for it.
