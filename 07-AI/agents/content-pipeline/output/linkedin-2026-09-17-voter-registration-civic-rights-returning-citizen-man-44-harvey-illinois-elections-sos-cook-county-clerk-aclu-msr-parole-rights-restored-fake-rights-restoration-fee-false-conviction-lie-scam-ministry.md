Sister Yvette has been doing voter registration drives at Greater Harvest for eleven years.

She sets up a folding table in the lobby after second service, the same way every time — green tablecloth, Illinois State Board of Elections forms fanned out in a neat row, a pen tied to the table with a piece of yarn because pens always walk away. She has registered, by her own count, close to four hundred people in that lobby. She knows how to answer the questions that make people hesitate. She knows which questions she cannot answer.

The one she keeps hearing, the one that made her stop and stand in the aisle for a moment the Sunday Marcus asked it:

*"I did time. Can I even do this?"*

Marcus is 44. He grew up in Harvey, graduated from Thornton, went into warehouse logistics — the kind of work that does not stop moving, inventory in and inventory out, the kind of job where you learn to solve problems fast because there is always another truck. He made a decision in his mid-thirties that cost him four years of his life, and he came home from IDOC on a spring morning in 2023 and has been rebuilding, steadily, since. He has a foreman's position now at a distribution center in Dolton. His supervisor nominated him for employee of the quarter in August. His oldest daughter is starting her first semester at Prairie State in January.

He has been out for going on three years. He still does not believe he is allowed to vote.

He is wrong. And the fact that he believes it — that a man who served his time, completed his supervision, reintegrated into his community by every measure that matters, still walks past a voter registration table assuming the table is not for him — is not a failure of Marcus. It is a failure of information, the same gap that turns into a scam the moment someone is willing to sell him the wrong answer.

**What Illinois actually says:**

Every state has its own laws governing voting rights for people with felony convictions. Illinois's law changed substantially in the last few years, and most people — including most returning citizens — do not know the current version.

Under Illinois law, as it stands now, you **cannot** vote only while you are physically incarcerated in a state or federal correctional facility serving a felony sentence.

That is the only disqualification.

If you are on **parole or mandatory supervised release (MSR)** from an Illinois state conviction, you **can vote**. Illinois restored voting rights to people on parole through Public Act 102-0648, which took effect on July 1, 2023. If Marcus had tried to register on June 30, 2023 — his first day home — the old law would have said no. The day he actually came home, the new law already applied.

If you are on **probation** — a sentence served in the community rather than incarcerated — you have always been able to vote in Illinois. The probation restriction never existed here.

If you are **fully off supervision** — done with parole, MSR, probation, all of it — you absolutely can vote. There is no waiting period. There is no additional form to file. There is no rights-restoration process. The rights are already there.

If you have a **federal felony conviction** and were released from federal custody into the community in Illinois, Illinois law still applies for state and local elections. For federal elections, the analysis is more nuanced, and the ACLU of Illinois can walk through the specific situation.

Marcus served his MSR. He has been fully off supervision for over a year. He has the right to vote in every election — federal, state, and local — and has had that right since the day his supervision ended, without any application or payment or process to complete.

Sister Yvette cannot answer every question at the folding table. This is one she should be able to answer.

**How to register, and how to vote:**

**Online registration:** Illinois has online voter registration at **ova.elections.il.gov**. You need an Illinois driver's license or state ID number, or the last four digits of your Social Security number. The online system takes about five minutes. The deadline to register online for an upcoming election is 28 days before Election Day.

**Automatic registration:** Illinois automatically registers eligible residents to vote when they interact with the Secretary of State — getting or renewing a driver's license or state ID, and at other state agencies. If Marcus has renewed his Illinois ID since completing his supervision, he may already be registered without knowing it. He can check his registration status at the Illinois State Board of Elections: **elections.illinois.gov**.

**Same-day registration:** Illinois allows voters to register on Election Day, and during the early voting period, at authorized sites. You need proof of current address — a utility bill, a bank statement, a piece of official mail with your name and address. If the 28-day online deadline has passed, same-day registration is the path.

**Checking your registration:** Before showing up to vote, check your registration status and polling place at **elections.illinois.gov** or by calling the Cook County Clerk at **312-603-0906**. Registration can lapse if you have moved, or if you have not voted in several election cycles. Better to check ahead of time than to find a problem at the polling place.

**Early voting:** Cook County offers early voting sites across the county, typically opening around 40 days before Election Day. Sites and hours are listed at **cookcountyclerkil.gov**. Early voting requires no reason or excuse — you walk in, show ID, vote, walk out.

**Voting by mail:** Illinois allows any registered voter to request a mail ballot. Applications are available through the Cook County Clerk and can be submitted starting 90 days before an election. The ballot is mailed to your home and must be returned by Election Day.

**Where to go with questions:**

**Illinois State Board of Elections:** elections.illinois.gov / **217-782-4141**. The authoritative source for registration status, polling places, ballot information, and election calendars. Nonpartisan. Free.

**Cook County Clerk — Elections Division:** cookcountyclerkil.gov / **312-603-0906**. Manages voter registration, early voting sites, and mail ballot applications for Cook County. Free.

**Illinois Secretary of State:** ilsos.gov / **800-252-8980**. Automatic voter registration at driver's license facilities. If you have recently gotten or renewed an Illinois ID or driver's license, the SOS can confirm whether you were registered through that process.

**ACLU of Illinois — Voting Rights:** aclu-il.org / **312-201-9740**. The ACLU's Voting Rights Project handles specific questions about rights restoration, felon disenfranchisement, voter intimidation, and registration problems that the county clerk cannot resolve. Free.

**Illinois Prison Policy Initiative:** illinoisprisonpolicy.org. Publishes plain-English guides to voting rights for returning citizens in Illinois, updated as the law changes. Useful for churches running reentry ministries that want accurate, current information to share.

**VOTE411.org** (League of Women Voters): vote411.org. Nonpartisan voter information tool — look up your current registration status, find your polling place, and see sample ballots for your specific address. Works nationwide. Free.

**Illinois Voter Registration:** You can also register in person at your local election authority — in Cook County, that is the Cook County Clerk's office at 69 W. Washington, Chicago — and at public libraries, Secretary of State facilities, and military recruitment offices.

**What the church already does — and what it can do:**

Greater Harvest's voter registration table has been in that lobby for eleven years. Sister Yvette can look a person in the eye and tell them their vote matters, and she has done it four hundred times. That is not something an app does.

What she does not always have — and what the people sitting across that green tablecloth sometimes need — is an answer to the specific question they are carrying. *My record. My supervision. My situation.* The general resource list does not always reach all the way to that question.

AI is useful for exactly this. A faith community health worker, a reentry navigator, or a voter registration volunteer can use it the way a well-resourced nonprofit would use a staff attorney on call: to look up the current Illinois statute, to find the ACLU voting rights page, to draft a plain-English explanation of what the 2023 parole-voting-rights change actually says, to help prepare the two-sentence answer Sister Yvette can give with confidence the next time Marcus asks.

It does not replace the fold-out table, the pen tied with yarn, the deaconess who has been showing up for eleven years. It just fills in the gaps she has been filling with her best guess.
