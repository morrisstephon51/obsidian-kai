She was raised to believe that unemployment is welfare, a handout, something for people who don't want to work — and she has always, always wanted to work. She showed up early. She covered other people's shifts. She was the one they called when they were short. So the word itself — *unemployment* — sits on her like an accusation, and underneath it is a second, quieter fear she would never say out loud: that at fifty-six she is simply too old now, that nobody is going to hire a woman her age, and that she may as well not even try. Here is what nobody has sat her down and told her plainly, and what somebody has to: the money she is so ashamed to claim is not a handout. It is insurance her own work paid for, set aside by law for exactly this week — and being laid off at fifty-six is an economic event that happened to her, not a verdict on her worth.

You know this woman. She is the one who never called in unless she was truly down, who trained the young ones without being asked, who took pride in a job other people looked past, and who has carried her household on her own two feet for longer than most people could. And somewhere in these last few days she swallowed two lies that are quietly costing her the very help she earned: that filing for unemployment is admitting she failed, and that her age has already closed every door in front of her. Both are false, and the truth stands on far firmer ground than the shame will let her feel it. A woman who worked twenty years and paid into unemployment insurance with every single paycheck is not taking a handout — she is collecting an insurance benefit she already bought with her labor. A layoff is not a character flaw; it is a line that got cut, a contract that was lost, a store that closed. The law wrote unemployment insurance into being on purpose, because a society decided that a working person knocked out of a job should have a bridge to the next one instead of a cliff — and it wrote free job-training and reemployment help into being right alongside it, because it decided a skilled worker of fifty-six is worth rehiring, not writing off.

And that has to be said plainly, and I will say it again before I'm done: claiming unemployment is not welfare and it is not weakness — it is stewardship of the household that leans on her. It is one of the most ordinary things in the world for a good, hardworking woman to be laid off through no fault of her own, and there is no shame in it whatsoever. Choosing to face it the honest, patient way — file the claim, certify each week, appeal if she has to, and let the free reemployment help point her at the next job — instead of hiding from it out of pride or handing her information to a stranger who "guarantees" a faster check, is not admitting defeat.
