# Post #338

---

The call came from the doctor, not from Mama.

Denise is 54. She grew up in Auburn Gresham and moved to Harvey for twenty years, then back north when her mother's health started asking questions she couldn't answer from thirty minutes away. She's been sleeping in her childhood bedroom since March, in the house her mother has owned since 1988 — the house her father worked a full career at the post office to pay off before he died in 2019.

Marvella is 73. She has advancing dementia — Alzheimer's type, diagnosed fourteen months ago, progressing faster than the neurologist said it would. She knows who Denise is, mostly. She does not know what month it is. She signed her name to something last October that she cannot recall — a home repair company knocked on the door and Marvella told them yes and handed them a pen, and they have a signed contract for $4,800 worth of gutter work Denise cannot find a single receipt proving they ever completed.

Marvella never signed a healthcare power of attorney. She never signed a durable financial power of attorney. Denise's late father did not think about estate planning in those terms, and neither did Marvella, and neither did Denise — until she walked into the bank and tried to transfer money from her mother's account to pay her mother's mortgage and the teller told her she had no authority to do that.

She went home and cried for a long time. Then she started making calls.

---

**What Denise does not have, and what it costs her:**

Without legal authority, Denise cannot:
- Access or manage her mother's bank accounts
- Pay her mother's mortgage, utilities, or property taxes from her mother's funds
- Sign insurance paperwork or authorize medical procedures
- Apply for Medicaid on her mother's behalf
- Protect her mother's home from exploitation or predatory contracts
- Speak for her mother in any official capacity

No one created this situation maliciously. This is what happens when a family runs out of time before they made a plan — and what happens in that situation is manageable, but only if you know where to start.

---

**What Illinois allows Denise to do, and how:**

**Guardianship of a Disabled Adult — Cook County Probate Division:**
Illinois law allows a family member — or any person with a legitimate interest — to petition the Cook County Circuit Court, Probate Division, to be appointed as guardian of an adult who lacks the capacity to make personal decisions (guardian of the person) or manage their finances (guardian of the estate, sometimes called conservatorship). Denise can petition for both.

This is a court proceeding. It requires a petition describing Marvella's condition and the relief requested, a physician's certificate or clinical assessment of her disability, notice to Marvella and to close family members, and a court hearing at which the judge determines the level of incapacity and the appropriate scope of guardianship. It is not simple, but it is a standard proceeding the Probate Division handles regularly.

The Cook County Circuit Court Probate Division is located at the Richard J. Daley Center, 69 W. Washington Street, Chicago. Forms and instructions are available at the courthouse. The Probate Division Self-Help Center provides guidance for unrepresented petitioners. Filing fees may be waived or reduced for income-eligible petitioners.

**Limited guardianship:**
If Marvella retains some capacity in some areas but not others, the court may appoint a limited guardian — one with authority only over specific domains. This preserves whatever autonomy Marvella still has. A judge determines the scope. A legal aid attorney can advise on what scope to request and why.

**Illinois Adult Guardianship and Advocacy Commission (AGAC):**
The AGAC, at 217-785-1540, provides free information on the guardianship process and supports individuals navigating it. This is a state agency. There is no charge for their guidance.

**Office of the State Guardian (OSG) — Cook County:**
The OSG, at 312-793-3390, provides guardianship services for indigent adults who have no family member or friend able to serve as guardian. Denise can call this office to understand the process and what her role as a family petitioner would involve.

**Legal Aid Chicago (LAF):**
312-341-1070. This is the most important call Denise can make. Legal Aid provides free civil legal representation to income-eligible clients in probate guardianship proceedings. A Legal Aid attorney can assess whether plenary or limited guardianship is the right path, prepare or review the petition, represent Denise at the court hearing, and help her understand her ongoing legal responsibilities once appointed. Free. Denise does not need to spend anything on an attorney if she qualifies.

**Cabrini Green Legal Aid:**
312-738-2452. Provides free civil legal help to clients throughout Cook County.

**CARPLS (Coordinated Advice and Referral Program for Legal Services):**
312-738-9200. Free civil legal advice over the phone, no appointment needed, on a first-come basis. Denise can get an initial legal consultation on the guardianship process before she files anything or spends a dollar.

**AgeOptions (Area Agency on Aging, suburban Cook County):**
708-383-0258. For Chicago families, the Chicago DFSS at 312-744-5000 connects to comparable resources. AgeOptions has care managers and navigators who can help Denise understand what other supports Marvella qualifies for alongside the legal process — including Community Care Program in-home services, Medicaid-funded supports, and the Alzheimer's Association Chicago helpline at 800-272-3900.

**The house:**
Once Denise has guardianship of the estate, she will have legal authority to manage her mother's finances and pay the mortgage. If payments are behind, a HUD-approved housing counselor at 800-569-4287 can advise on options. If there is any question about whether Marvella's signature was obtained on a deed or a lien at a vulnerable moment — the gutter contractor, a solicitor, anyone — Denise should check the Cook County Recorder of Deeds at 312-603-5050 and consult Legal Aid. Deed theft from adults with dementia is a real and documented harm. Catching it requires looking.

**The warning before you pay $3,000 to anyone:**

Adult guardianship of a parent with dementia is one of the highest-urgency legal situations a family faces — and one of the most reliably targeted by people who charge for what is already available at no cost.

**The "elder care intake specialist" who is not an attorney:**
Someone — found through an internet ad, a church flyer, a Facebook group for caregivers — presents themselves as a specialist in Illinois elder law and guardianship who can "guide you through the process" for an upfront consultation fee of $300, $500, or $750. They review your situation, explain the Probate Division process, and refer you to an attorney. Here is the problem. That explanation is available free from the AGAC, the Probate Division Self-Help Center, and CARPLS. A non-attorney is not authorized to provide legal advice in Illinois. You are paying for a referral and a summary of public information. The $500 buys nothing that CARPLS does not provide for free.

**The "expedited guardianship service":**
A company claims to have relationships with the Cook County courts that can accelerate a guardianship petition for a flat fee. There is no such relationship. Guardianship petitions move at the pace of the Probate Division's docket. Emergency or temporary guardianship relief is a real legal mechanism when circumstances require it — but it is authorized by the court through a licensed attorney's motion, not by a service company. If you need emergency relief, call Legal Aid.

**The deed risk:**
While a family is navigating the guardianship process, someone may approach Marvella — or Denise, presenting as a helpful resource — and suggest that the home should be transferred "out of probate" or "protected from Medicaid" by quitclaim deed to a family member, a trust, or an investor for a fee. Do not sign a quitclaim deed without a licensed Illinois attorney reviewing it first. A quitclaim deed signed by a person with dementia may be legally voidable — but reversing it requires a lawsuit. The harm from signing is far easier to create than to undo. If the gutter contractor has a signed contract Marvella cannot recall, the contract is worth examining with Legal Aid before paying $4,800 for work that may not have been done.

**The "Medicaid pre-planning service":**
Someone tells Denise she needs to transfer or "spend down" her mother's assets to protect the home from Medicaid estate recovery — for a fee. Medicaid planning for a person with dementia is a real and legitimate specialty within elder law. But legitimate elder law attorneys do this work under a written fee agreement, are licensed by the ARDC, and disclose costs clearly before they begin. An unlicensed "Medicaid planner" who takes cash upfront, is not an attorney, and does not provide a written agreement is not providing legal advice — they are selling a service they are not authorized to render. Legal Aid can advise on what Medicaid planning considerations are relevant and when a referral to a private elder law attorney is appropriate.

**Beware contact that starts after you shared your situation.**
If Denise mentioned her mother's condition in a Facebook group for South Side caregivers, at church, or to a neighbor — and then someone reached out specifically, with an offer of specialized help, and then quoted a price — that is the sequence that should stop her in place. Legitimate services do not work that way.
