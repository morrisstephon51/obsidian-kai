# Angle: The addiction / recovery ministry. The brother or sister who got clean years ago and now sits with everyone else trying to — takes the 2 a.m. call, drives to the ER, sits in the folding-chair circle in the fellowship hall — and then tries to hold the whole behavioral-health maze in their head for people in the worst hour of their lives: a detox bed that has to be found tonight or the window closes, an insurance authorization that decides whether treatment happens, medication-assisted treatment nobody explained, a sober-living house with a bed and a waitlist, a Medicaid plan that covers the care if you can find the one clinic that takes it. AI as the recovery navigator a funded treatment center already provides. Opens a new resource-navigation lane: behavioral-health / recovery logistics. Distinct from the health/nurse ministry (clinical / population health). Ties to Community Intake.

There is a man at that church who used to be the one everybody prayed for. Now he is the one who picks up the phone at 2 a.m.

He got clean a long time ago. He does not talk about it like a trophy. He talks about it like a debt. So when somebody's son is on the floor of a bathroom, or somebody's daughter calls from a parking lot saying she is done, done for real this time, his is the number that gets dialed. He drives. He sits in the ER for six hours. He runs the folding-chair circle in the fellowship hall on Thursday nights, the one that is not on any bulletin, where people say the true thing out loud for the first time.

And then the hard part starts. Because wanting to get clean and being able to get help are two different countries, and the border between them is made of paperwork and phone numbers that nobody hands you.

Because there is a window. When a person finally says yes — really says yes — that yes has a shelf life measured in hours. And in those hours you are supposed to find a detox bed that actually exists tonight, not next week. You are supposed to know which facility takes their insurance, or that Medicaid covers it but only at the one clinic across two towns that has a three-week wait. You are supposed to know what medication-assisted treatment is and that it is not "trading one drug for another," and to find a prescriber who will start it. You are supposed to know which sober-living house has a bed and which one is a bed in name only. You are supposed to fight an insurance company that wants a prior authorization before a human being who is ready to change their life can walk through a door.

So the man tries to hold all of it. In his head. From his own memory of the last time somebody needed a bed. He calls the number he called two years ago and it has been disconnected. He calls the one that answers and they are full. He is one person, working from a list in his head that goes out of date the day he learns it, in a system that changes faster than any recovering man can keep up with.

So the window closes. The son who said yes at midnight is sober enough by morning to say never mind. The daughter in the parking lot drives home because the only bed anyone could find was ninety minutes away and there was no ride. Somebody relapses on a Friday and nobody knows there is a warm handoff that could have caught him, because the one person who might have known it was asleep after a double shift.

Here is what a family with money gets when their child is ready.

They get a recovery navigator who knows every bed in the region and calls them by name. They get a case manager who fights the insurance authorization so the family never sees the fight. They get an interventionist, a placement service, a private detox that admits the same afternoon. They get someone whose entire job is to keep a person moving from the moment of yes to the door of treatment before the window shuts. That person is not more faithful than he is. They are just resourced.

He can have that help too. For free. On the same phone the calls come in on.

He can describe what is happening right now and get back a real, current read on where the open detox beds are and which ones take this person's coverage. He can hold up an insurance card and get told in plain language what it covers, what a prior authorization is, and what to say to get past it. He can get medication-assisted treatment explained without the shame, and a list of prescribers who actually start people. He can get the sober-living options sorted into which ones have a bed and which ones are worth walking into. He can have the maze — detox, treatment, MAT, housing, the Medicaid plan that unlocks all of it — broken into an order of operations he can follow at 2 a.m. with a shaking person beside him.

Not to replace what he does. The circle is his. The 2 a.m. drive is his. He is the one who can say "I was exactly where you are" and have it be true. No machine has ever sat in an ER at 4 a.m. and told a scared kid he is worth saving. It just takes the maze off his shoulders so he can do the part only he can do, which is not let go.

That is the gap. It was never that the people in our pews fighting to get clean deserve a smaller shot at their own lives than the ones whose families could hire a navigator. It is that those families got handed a team, and ours got handed one tired man with a phone and a debt he is still paying forward.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities and the people navigating resources beside them in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Matteson. Dolton. Not a webinar. A room. Real problems. A real person, a real window, a real 2 a.m.

The people carrying our neighbors back from the edge by hand are not behind. They were just never handed the tools the funded programs take for granted.

We are here to hand them over.

The plug is in.
