There is a woman at that church whose plates sell out before the benediction is finished.

You know her. Everybody knows her. Her mac and cheese has a reputation three towns over. She caters the repasts and the usher board banquet and the pastor's anniversary, and when a family loses somebody, hers is the first pan through the door before anyone thinks to ask. People drive in from other congregations on the Sundays she sells dinners. She takes cash, and she takes an "I'll get you next week," and she keeps her orders on the back of the church bulletin and in her head, and she has been told her whole life, by everyone who has ever eaten her food, that she should open a restaurant. She smiles when they say it. She files it under things that happen to other people. Because a business, in her mind, is a thing with a lawyer and a loan officer and a storefront and a pile of money she has never once had, and she has none of those, so she has decided, quietly and long ago, that what she does is not that. It is just cooking. It is just what she does for her church.

Here is the thing nobody has ever said to her plainly: she is already running a business. She has been for twenty years. It just isn't wearing its own name yet.

And then the hard part starts. Because the distance between what she does now and a real, named, legal business she could actually build on is not money and it is not talent. It is a maze of paperwork and free help nobody ever walked her through, and every step she does not know about is a door she assumes is bolted.

Because the number that makes a business real, the EIN from the IRS, is free — genuinely free, at the government's own site — and there are websites that charge forty, sixty, a hundred dollars for the exact thing the IRS hands out for nothing, and the woman who does not know that either pays a stranger for free paper or, more often, never gets it at all because she assumes it must cost something she does not have. Because the first real step was never a storefront — it was opening a business bank account so her business money and her rent money stop living in the same pocket, and keeping her books somewhere other than a shoebox, so that for the first time she can see what she actually earns. Because the moment she took money for a plate she likely became a business in the eyes of the law already, a sole proprietor, and whether she should form something more, an LLC, to put a wall between the business and her own house, is a real and serious question — but it is a question to ask a free adviser, not one to guess at from a video or a cousin's opinion. Because that free adviser exists and is not a secret kept for the connected: there are retired business owners who will mentor her for nothing, and a state small-business center that will sit with her one on one at no charge, and a women's business center built for exactly her — and she has walked past all of it her whole life because no one ever told her it was hers to walk into. Because she has been pricing her food out of love and habit instead of out of cost, charging what feels neighborly instead of what the ingredients and the hours and a fair wage actually add up to, which is the reason the hardest-working cook in three towns still barely breaks even and calls it a blessing. Because the state has a law — a cottage food law — that may let a home cook sell certain foods legally, and it has real limits on what she can make and where she can sell it, and knowing whether her mac and cheese and her cakes fall inside those lines or outside them is the difference between building in the open and hoping nobody asks. Because when she needs a first small loan to buy the second oven or the warming trays, a bank will look at her shoebox and her cash income and say no, and she will believe that is the end of it — when there are nonprofit lenders, community lenders, built to say yes to exactly the person the bank turned away, at fair terms, with a human being who helps her get ready to ask. Because the "government small business grant" that slides into her messages, or calls her phone, or "guarantees" her money if she just pays a processing fee or sends a gift card, is a scam, every single time, and the woman working hardest to build something is the one the scammer is hunting. And because the fast money that is easy to get, the merchant cash advance with the friendly rep who can fund her tomorrow, is the payday loan of small business, and it can swallow a good little kitchen whole. And under all of it is one plain fact: nobody ever walked her through any of this, not because her gift was small, but because she never had the paid team that the person across town had waiting on day one.

So she keeps going the only way she knows. Cash in the pocket. Orders on the bulletin. Prices set by her heart. The shoebox under the bed. A gift the whole community can taste and not one piece of the scaffolding that would let it hold weight. She is not behind because she is not capable. She is the most capable person in the building. She is behind because a real business is a stack of small, unglamorous, findable things, and every one of them was findable by someone who had a person paid to find it, and she had no such person, so the stack stayed invisible.

Here is the thing about a founder with resources: the scaffolding was there before the first customer. They opened the doors with an accountant already on retainer, an attorney who set up the company right, a banker who knew their name, a marketing person for the logo and the launch, and a relationship with the small-business agency that most people do not know exists. Getting from an idea to a real, protected, priced-correctly, properly-licensed business was not a maze that founder solved alone at a kitchen table, because there was a whole paid team solving it beside them. That founder is not more talented than the woman whose food people drive across the county for. Their food is not better. The scaffolding was just already built for them, and never for her.

She can have that team too. For free. Starting this week, with the free number the IRS gives away that she almost paid a stranger for.

She can get the real EIN at no cost and know never to pay for it. She can set up the business bank account that finally separates her money from her business's money and start simple books she can actually read. She can learn enough about a sole proprietorship and an LLC to walk into the free small-business center and ask the right question instead of guessing at the answer. She can find that center, and the retired mentors, and the women's business center, and see that they were always hers. She can finally price her plates off what they truly cost, so the hardest worker in three towns stops losing money on love. She can learn what the cottage food law does and does not let her do, and take that exact question to the health department instead of hoping. She can find the nonprofit lender who says yes where the bank said no, and get herself ready to ask well. She can spot the "guaranteed grant" and the EIN fee and the too-easy cash advance for the traps they are. She can get the menu written, the flyer made, the little social page and the listing so people who have never tasted her food can find it. She can have the whole maze — the free EIN, the bank account and the books, the structure question, the pricing, the license, the fair loan, the scams to dodge — broken into an order of operations she can start this month, and then be walked straight to the free advisers, the licensing and health offices, and the lenders who carry it the rest of the way.

Not to replace what she does. The gift is hers. The recipe is hers, and the reputation she built one repast and one anniversary at a time, and the trust of every single person who has ever been fed by her hands — no machine will ever have stood over a stove at four in the morning for a grieving family that could not pay. It only takes the scaffolding off her shoulders so she can do the part only she can do, which is the cooking, and the feeding, and the being the woman this whole community already trusts with its most tender days. It just finally lets her get paid like the professional she has been for twenty years.

That is the gap. It was never that the woman in our pews is less talented or works less hard than the founder for whom the scaffolding was already built. It is that that founder had a paid team assembled before the first customer, and ours had one gifted woman with a foil pan and her orders written on the back of the church bulletin.

A word of care, plainly: the EIN from the IRS is free at irs.gov, so any site charging you for one is a site to close, and any "business grant" that asks for a fee, "guarantees" you money, arrives by direct message or robocall, or wants a gift card is a scam every time, because real registration and real grants never charge you to receive them. Be slow with the "business credit" schemes and slower still with a merchant cash advance whose rep can fund you tomorrow — that fast money is the most expensive money there is. And do not guess at your business structure, your license, or whether your food is sold legally from home — those are real decisions with real consequences. This is general navigation, not legal, tax, accounting, business-licensing, or financial advice — helping someone learn that the EIN is free or that a free adviser exists is not the same as forming their business, filing their taxes, pricing their product, or telling them which license they need — so always route the actual formation, licensing, food-safety, tax, and lending decisions to a free small-business adviser at SCORE (score.org) or the Illinois Small Business Development Center (ilsbdc.biz), to the Women's Business Development Center in Chicago (wbdc.org), to an attorney or accountant, to the local city or village licensing office and the county health department for anything with food, to the Illinois Secretary of State and the Illinois Department of Revenue for registration and sales tax, and to the U.S. Small Business Administration (sba.gov) and a reputable community lender the SBDC can point you to.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities and the people building something beside them in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Matteson. Dolton. Not a webinar. A room. Real problems. A real woman who stopped believing a business was for other people, a real EIN she got for free instead of paying a stranger for, a real kitchen that finally got to wear its own name.

The people feeding this whole community by hand, out of a gift and a foil pan, are not behind. They were just never handed the scaffolding the funded never even have to reach for.

We are here to hand it over.

The plug is in.
