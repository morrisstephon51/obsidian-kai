**Key legal and program facts:**

- **Illinois Workers' Compensation Act (820 ILCS 305) — who is covered:** Nearly all Illinois employees are covered, including part-time, temporary, seasonal, and day-labor workers. Coverage does not depend on full-time status, seniority, or union membership. Independent contractors (genuine IC status) are the main exception. A warehouse order picker at a distribution center with six years on the job is covered. An employer's statement that "we don't do workers' comp for a first occurrence" is not a statement of the law. Workers' comp is not a benefit the employer grants — it is insurance the employer is required by law to carry, and a right that attaches the moment the work-related injury occurs.

- **The employer's obligation from the moment of injury:** When a work-related injury occurs, the employer (through its insurer) is immediately obligated to pay for all reasonably necessary medical treatment related to the injury. This includes emergency care, diagnostic imaging (MRI, X-ray), physical therapy, specialist referrals, and any treatment a licensed physician determines is causally connected to the workplace injury. The employer's obligation to pay for medical care does not require the injured worker to file a formal IWCC claim first — it attaches at injury. An employer who tells an injured worker to "use your own health insurance" is improperly shifting costs the employer's workers' comp insurer is legally obligated to pay.

- **Temporary Total Disability (TTD) benefits — the lost wages you may be owed:** When a work-related injury prevents a worker from returning to their regular job or any job, the employer owes Temporary Total Disability (TTD) benefits equal to two-thirds of the worker's average weekly wage, tax-free. For Darnell — averaging forty hours per week at $21.50 — his average weekly wage is $860. Two-thirds of $860 is $573.33 per week in TTD benefits. For two full weeks of lost time, that is $1,146.67 he should have received. Instead he burned his PTO and sick time and received zero workers' comp wage replacement. The IWCC can order back payment of TTD from the date of injury.

- **The right to choose your own treating physician:** Illinois law gives an injured worker the right to choose their own physician — once — in addition to the employer's directed care. In practice, this means: if the employer's insurer initially directs the worker to a company occupational medicine clinic, the worker has the right to also see a physician of their own choosing, and that physician's findings carry full weight in an IWCC proceeding. An MRI from Darnell's own doctor showing a bulging disc at L4-L5 is admissible and material, regardless of what the company clinic documented. Documenting the injury through your own physician is the single most important protective step an injured worker can take.

- **Filing an Application for Adjustment of Claim — what it is and why it matters:** The IWCC Application for Adjustment of Claim (Form 45) is the document that formally initiates a workers' compensation case with the IWCC. It asks for: the injured worker's name and address, the employer's name and address, the date of the injury, and a brief description of what happened and what body part was injured. It is a two-page form. It is filed with the IWCC district office for the county where the injury occurred. There is no filing fee. Once filed, it tolls (pauses) the statute of limitations and puts the employer and its insurer on formal notice. An IWCC arbitrator is assigned, and the case proceeds to either a settlement negotiation or a hearing before the arbitrator. Filing does not require an attorney, though most injured workers benefit from one for contested cases.

- **Statute of limitations — why time matters:** An injured worker has three years from the date of the injury (or two years from the last payment of compensation, whichever is later) to file an Application for Adjustment of Claim. Three years sounds like a long time; it is not, particularly when an employer has discouraged filing and the worker believes the employer's assurance to "take care of it." If Darnell does not file before November 18, 2028, he permanently forfeits his right to any compensation for the November 18, 2025 injury. The $195 "claim service" he paid does not toll the limitations period. Only a filed IWCC Application does that.

- **Retaliation is illegal:** Section 4(h) of the Illinois Workers' Compensation Act prohibits an employer from discharging, harassing, or discriminating against any worker for exercising their rights under the Act, including filing an IWCC claim or retaining an attorney. If an employer terminates, demotes, cuts hours, or otherwise penalizes a worker after they file a workers' comp claim or consult an attorney, that is a Section 4(h) violation. The remedy includes reinstatement and back wages. Arise Chicago assists workers facing workers' comp retaliation in South Suburban Cook County.

- **Legitimate workers' comp attorneys charge no upfront fee:** Under Illinois Supreme Court rules governing attorneys, workers' compensation attorneys are prohibited from collecting an upfront retainer for representing an injured worker on a contingency basis. The standard workers' comp attorney fee is twenty percent of any settlement or award approved by the IWCC arbitrator, collected only if the case results in compensation. If the case results in zero recovery, the attorney receives zero. A website or individual that charges an upfront fee to "help" with a workers' comp claim is not a licensed Illinois workers' comp attorney and is providing a service that a contingency attorney provides at no upfront cost. The Illinois State Bar Association referral line at illinoislawyerfinder.com connects injured workers with licensed counsel at no charge.

**Here is what the Illinois Workers' Compensation Act actually gives you — and what your employer may not have explained:**

**1. Workers' comp is not a benefit your employer gives you. It is insurance they are required to carry.**

Every Illinois employer with one or more employees is required to carry workers' compensation insurance. It is not optional, and an employer's "policy" of not filing for first-occurrence injuries has no legal standing. The moment a work-related injury occurs, the obligation to pay for medical treatment and lost wages attaches by law.

**2. The employer telling you to use your own health insurance is improper.**

Using your personal health insurance for a work-related injury shifts a cost your employer's workers' comp insurer is legally obligated to pay onto you and your insurer. It can also create complications for later workers' comp claims because the treatment record may not document the occupational cause. If you are injured at work, the cost of treatment belongs to workers' comp, not your personal insurance.

**3. You have the right to see your own doctor.**

Even if the employer initially sends you to a company-selected occupational medicine clinic, you have the right to choose one additional treating physician of your own. Your doctor's findings — including an MRI documenting a disc injury the company clinic called minor — are fully admissible in an IWCC proceeding. Get your own documentation. It is often the most important evidence in the case.

**4. You may be owed Temporary Total Disability for your time off work.**

If your injury prevented you from working your regular job, you are owed TTD benefits equal to two-thirds of your average weekly wage, tax-free, for the period of disability. If you burned your PTO and sick time instead of receiving TTD, those losses may be recoverable. An IWCC arbitrator can order back-payment of TTD from the date of injury.

**5. The filing is free. The attorney is free upfront.**

File the Application for Adjustment of Claim at iwcc.il.gov or call 866-352-3033 for help. Workers' comp attorneys in Illinois take contingency — typically twenty percent of any settlement or award, paid only if you recover. No legitimate workers' comp attorney charges an upfront fee. A website that charges $195 to "document your case" is selling you a service a contingency attorney provides at zero upfront cost.

**6. You have a statute of limitations. File before it runs.**

Three years from the date of injury (or two years from the last payment of compensation, whichever is later). If your employer told you they would "handle it" and nothing was filed, the clock is still running. Only a filed IWCC Application tolls the limitations period. Tony's initialed near-miss form does not.
