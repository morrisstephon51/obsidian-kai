She works part-time at a discount store off Ashland, picks up hours when she can, and lives in the upstairs of a two-flat with her boyfriend Deon, who drives for a warehouse temp agency; between the two of them the money is real but thin, and neither one has health insurance. When she turned twenty-something the Medicaid she'd had as a kid quietly lapsed in one of the renewal rounds, and she never sorted it back out because she was never sick. Now she is not sick — she is *pregnant* — and the number stuck in her head is one a coworker said out loud in the break room: *"having a baby in the hospital is like twenty thousand dollars."* So Daysha did the math a frightened twenty-four-year-old does, and the math said *don't go.* Don't go to the doctor, because a visit is money you don't have and a bill you can't face. She told herself she felt fine. She'd get checked "later, when it's closer." She is exactly the kind of healthy young woman who assumes prenatal care is a luxury and not the thing that catches the quiet problems — the blood pressure, the anemia, the sugar — before they become the loud ones. And she assumes she can't get covered: *Medicaid's welfare, that's not me; I work; I probably make too much — or too little — anyway.* So when an ad slid across her phone one night — soft lettering, a photo of a brown-skinned mother cradling a newborn, *"Expecting? You may qualify for a Pregnancy & Newborn Benefit — up to $4,000 in support. Enroll before your due date"* — it did not feel like a scam. It felt like a door. There was a "$2.99 enrollment fee," and then boxes asking her to "verify" her Social Security number, her date of birth, and a debit card "to receive your benefit deposit." A second ad, right under it, promised a **free breast pump and a baby welcome box** — *"just cover $4.95 shipping and confirm your insurance ID."* She didn't have an insurance ID. But she had a debit card with $61 on it, and a thumb hovering over the first box.

Retired now, she trained as a community doula so she could keep doing the part she loved, and she leads Greater New Hope's "Blessed Beginnings," the ministry that quietly watches over the expecting and new mothers in the pews — a car seat in the closet, a casserole after delivery, a ride to a checkup, a hand to hold. She has seen, up close and more than once, what happens when a young woman comes through the doors of Labor & Delivery for the very first time in her whole pregnancy — no prenatal care, nobody having checked her pressure, and a baby in trouble that somebody should have caught at twenty weeks. She keeps a laminated card in her tote, and the Blessed Beginnings mothers know it by heart: *"PREGNANCY CARE IS COVERED. YOU CAN BE COVERED TODAY — THE SAME DAY YOU WALK IN. NOBODY REAL CHARGES YOU TO SIGN UP, AND NOBODY REAL NEEDS YOUR BANK CARD FOR A 'PREGNANCY GRANT.' BABY, IF SOMEBODY CHARGES YOU TO GET YOU CARE, THAT IS THE SCAM."* When Daysha went pale and swayed against a pew during the second hymn, it was Nurse Ophelia who reached her first — and the nurse who never really retired took one look, felt the girl's clammy hand, and knew before Daysha said a word.

- **Presumptive Eligibility for Pregnant Women** — ask any **FQHC community health center, county/city health department, or hospital** to start you on **temporary Medicaid the same day** so prenatal care begins *now*, while your full application is processed — there is **no fee**

- **A local FQHC / community health center or health department** — free or sliding-scale prenatal care and staff who enroll you on the spot; find one at **findahealthcenter.hrsa.gov**
- **Illinois WIC (nutrition — a *separate, companion* door)** — **dph.illinois.gov (WIC)** — free healthy food, formula, and breastfeeding support during pregnancy and after; you can have Medicaid *and* WIC
- **Medicaid doula & home-visiting support** — Illinois Medicaid now covers **doula services and evidence-based home visiting** for eligible pregnant and postpartum members; **ask your HealthChoice Illinois plan** (e.g., CountyCare in Cook County) what maternal supports it offers

- **Family Case Management (FCM)** — through your local health department — a nurse/case manager who helps a pregnant mom and baby stay on track
- **National Maternal Mental Health Hotline** — **1-833-852-6262 (1-833-TLC-MAMA)** — free, 24/7, for pregnancy and postpartum anxiety or depression
- **2-1-1** — free, 24/7 referral to prenatal care, coverage help, and baby supplies near you

**Scam spine:** A frightened young mother searching *"how do I afford having a baby"* is one of the most targeted people on the internet, and the lies are shaped to fit her exact fear. **(1) The fake "pregnancy / newborn grant":** an ad or site promising *"up to $4,000 for expecting mothers — enroll before your due date"* that (a) charges a small "enrollment," "processing," or "activation" fee, (b) harvests her Social Security number, date of birth, and address, and (c) asks for a debit card "to deposit your benefit" — a card it will drain, not fill. There is no universal government "pregnancy grant" that cold-emails you for a fee. **(2) The "free breast pump / baby box — just pay shipping" phish:** breast pumps are genuinely covered free through Medicaid and insurance, which is what makes the fake so effective — the scam site charges "$4.95 shipping" to capture a card, and asks for her "insurance ID" to steal a real Medicaid or plan number it can bill fraud against. **(3) The imposter "Medicaid caseworker":** a call or text — *"to keep your baby's coverage active, verify your information / pay a small reinstatement fee"* — demanding a gift card, a wire, or her Social Security and card numbers. Real Medicaid does not cold-call for gift cards. **(4) The lookalike enrollment site:** a page that mimics the State or "All Kids" to collect an application fee and her whole identity — when the real application (abe.illinois.gov) is always free. **(5) The "sign up faster for a fee" middleman:** someone charging to "get you approved" for coverage that is free to apply for and that an FQHC or health department will help her with at no cost. Every one of them turns the moment a woman finally reaches for help into the moment she gets robbed — of money she doesn't have, of an identity she'll spend years cleaning up, or worst of all, of the *nerve to try again.* **No real maternal program charges a mother a fee to enroll, and none needs her bank card to give her a "grant." The instant someone asks a pregnant twenty-four-year-old to pay to be cared for, that is the robbery introducing itself.**

**Key facts:**
- **Pregnancy is covered — and the income limits are far higher than people assume.** Illinois Medicaid for pregnant women ("Moms & Babies," part of All Kids) covers **prenatal care, ultrasounds, labor and delivery, and the baby.** Many *working* families qualify; a paycheck does not automatically put you over the line.
- **You can be covered the *same day* — this is the part almost nobody knows.** Through **Presumptive Eligibility**, an FQHC, health department, or hospital can start you on **temporary Medicaid immediately**, so you can begin prenatal care *this week*, while your full application is processed. You do not have to wait, and you do not go without care in the meantime.
- **In Illinois, prenatal coverage is available regardless of immigration status.** Fear about status keeps mothers from care that the state makes available to them. Ask; do not assume you're shut out.
- **Prenatal care is not a luxury — it is how the quiet dangers get caught early.** High blood pressure (preeclampsia), gestational diabetes, anemia, and infection often have *no symptoms a young woman would notice* until they are serious. Regular checkups are the difference between catching them at twenty weeks and finding them in a crisis.
- **This is a life-and-death gap, and our community carries the weight of it.** In Illinois, Black mothers die of pregnancy-related causes at *several times* the rate of white mothers — and state reviews find **most of those deaths are preventable.** Early, consistent prenatal care and someone in your corner are among the strongest protections there are.
- **The coverage now includes a doula and a full year afterward.** Illinois Medicaid covers **doula support and home visiting**, and keeps a mother covered for **12 months postpartum** — for her body's recovery, her checkups, and her mental health, not just a few weeks.
- **WIC is a *separate* door, and you can have both.** WIC gives food, formula, and breastfeeding help; Medicaid gives the medical care. Signing up for one does not cost or cancel the other.
- **Breast pumps are really free — through your coverage, not a "shipping fee" site.** Once you have Medicaid or insurance, a pump is a covered benefit. Any site charging "shipping" and asking for your insurance ID to send a "free" pump is fishing for a number to steal.
- **How to start (free, and you don't do it alone):** call **1-866-ALL-KIDS (1-866-255-5437)**, apply at **abe.illinois.gov**, or simply **walk into an FQHC or health department and say "I'm pregnant and I need coverage and a doctor."** They enroll mothers every single day.
- **No real program charges a fee, demands a gift card, or needs your bank card to give you a "grant."** Your actual coverage is applied for free and your care is billed to Medicaid — never to a debit card you type into a pop-up.

**Distinct from:**
- **WIC / maternal-and-child *nutrition* (prior young-mother post)** — that provides **food, formula, and breastfeeding support**; **this** is the **medical coverage** for prenatal care, delivery, the doula, and postpartum — a different door you can hold at the same time
- **Childcare assistance / early-childhood (CCAP, Head Start, Preschool for All)** — that helps **after the baby is here**, so a parent can work; **this** is about **getting mother and baby safely *through the pregnancy and the birth***
- **General adult health insurance / ACA marketplace & Medicaid (prior post)** — that is broad coverage for adults; **this** is the specific, faster **pregnancy pathway** — presumptive eligibility, Moms & Babies, the doula benefit, and 12-month postpartum — that a general enrollment conversation often never mentions
- **Prescription assistance / patient-assistance programs (prior post)** — that lowers **medication** costs; **this** is comprehensive **maternity care**

**The $2.99 That Wanted Her Whole Identity, and the Nurse Who Never Really Retired.**

Daysha Coleman was fourteen weeks along and had told exactly four people.

Deon, of course. Her cousin Bree. A coworker who guessed. And, without meaning to, the whole third pew of Greater New Hope Missionary Baptist Church, the Sunday she went gray during the second hymn and reached for something to hold and found a pew back and then found a hand.

The hand belonged to Sister Ophelia Reese.

---

For thirty-four years, Ophelia Reese had worked Labor and Delivery. She knew the color of a woman who hadn't eaten, the color of a woman whose pressure was climbing, and the particular shaky pale of early pregnancy running on fear and vending-machine crackers. She got Daysha sat down, got her head between her knees, got a cup of juice from the mothers' board in her hand, and asked the questions in the order thirty-four years had taught her.

"How far along, baby?"

"...Fourteen weeks. I think."

"You *think.*" Not unkind. "Who's your doctor?"

And Daysha, who had held it together for months, started to cry. "I don't have one. I haven't— I know I'm supposed to. It's just— do you know how much it *costs?* I don't have insurance. I can't walk in there and get a twenty-thousand-dollar bill, Miss Ophelia. I can't. So I've just been— being careful. Eating right. I was gonna go when it's closer."

Nurse Ophelia Reese closed her eyes for one second, the way she used to at the nurses' station when a girl came in at term who should have been coming in since spring.

Then she opened them and said, "Sweetheart. Come sit with me after service. Because almost everything you just told yourself is wrong, and none of it is your fault, and we are going to fix all of it today."

---

At a folding table in the fellowship hall, over two plates nobody touched, Daysha showed her the phone.

She hadn't sent it yet. That was the mercy of it. But it was *right there* — the soft-lettered ad, the photo of the mother and the newborn, **"Pregnancy & Newborn Benefit — up to $4,000 — enroll before your due date,"** the **$2.99 enrollment fee**, the boxes for her Social Security number and a debit card "to receive your benefit deposit." And under it, the other one: **free breast pump, free baby box, just $4.95 shipping, confirm your insurance ID.**

"I was gonna do it last night," Daysha admitted. "Four thousand dollars. That's more than I— I figured that's how you afford it."

Ophelia looked at the screen for a long moment. Then she set the phone face-down, gently, the way you'd take something dangerous out of a child's hand without startling her.

"Baby, I need you to hear me, and I need you to hear the *order* of it," she said. "That is not a door. That is a trap with a picture of a Black baby on it so it looks like it's *for* you. And the real door — the one they're standing in front of so you can't see it — is free, and it's open, and I can walk you through it before you leave this hall."

---

**Here is what Nurse Ophelia laid on that table — everything a scared, uninsured twenty-four-year-old is never told:**

**1. Your pregnancy is covered. Not "might be." *Is.* And you probably qualify even though you work.**

"There's Medicaid built *specifically* for this — for pregnant women and babies. In Illinois they call it Moms & Babies, it's part of All Kids." Ophelia said it flat and certain. "It covers your prenatal visits, your ultrasounds, your labor, your delivery, and the baby after. And the income line is *way* higher than you think — a whole lot of working women qualify and never find out. You working part-time at that store does not put you out. Stop counting yourself out of a room you haven't walked into."

Daysha shook her head. "That's welfare, though. I don't—"

"It is *health coverage,*" Ophelia said, "the same as the card in my purse from thirty-four years of nursing. There is no version of you being a good mother that starts with you and that baby going without a doctor to protect your pride. None. I've *seen* how that story ends, and I am not going to watch you write it."

**2. You can be covered *today.* The same day. This is the part they bury.**

"You think you have to apply and wait weeks and go without in the meantime. You don't." Ophelia tapped the table. "It's called presumptive eligibility. You walk into a community health center — an FQHC — or the health department, you say you're pregnant, and they can start you on temporary coverage *that same day* so you can see a doctor *this week,* while your full application goes through. There is no waiting room of months. There is no fee. You could have a real prenatal appointment before Friday."

Daysha stared at her. "The same day."

"The same day. That's why I'm not letting you leave here thinking you have to solve twenty thousand dollars tonight. You have to make *one call* and take *one ride,* and I'm the ride."

**3. That $4,000 "grant" is the scam — and the $2.99 is the least of what it wanted.**

Ophelia turned the phone back over. "Look at what it asked you for. Your Social Security number. Your birthday. A debit card *to deposit your benefit.*" She said it slow. "Baby, nobody deposits four thousand dollars into your account by first asking for the card. That box isn't there to *put* money in. It's there to *take* it — and to walk off with your Social Security number so they can open things in your name for years. There is no government office emailing you a 'pregnancy grant' for a two-dollar fee. The FTC's been warning about that exact kind of ad. The real help doesn't charge you a dime to sign up."

"And the breast pump?"

"Real thing, fake site. Once you've got your coverage, a breast pump *is* free — through your plan, not through a 'shipping fee.' That page charging you $4.95 and asking for an 'insurance ID' is fishing for a number to steal or a card to hit. When you've got Medicaid, you order a pump the right way and it costs you *nothing.* Don't you give these people $4.95 or a single digit of anything."

**4. Prenatal care isn't a formality. It's how we catch what would hurt you both — before it does.**

This was the part Ophelia knew in her bones, from thirty-four years of the ones that came too late.

"You feel fine. I believe you. But preeclampsia — that's the blood pressure — and the sugar, and low blood, they don't *announce* themselves in a young healthy girl. No symptom you'd notice till it's an emergency. That's the whole *reason* for the checkups. Somebody puts a cuff on your arm and a hand on your belly every few weeks and catches the quiet thing while it's still small." She held Daysha's eyes. "You almost fainted in church today. That might be nothing. It might be your blood's low, it might be your pressure. I don't want to *guess.* I want a doctor to *know.* This week."

**5. And hear me on this, because it's why I don't play about it: for women who look like us, this is life and death — and most of it is preventable.**

Ophelia's voice dropped, steady and serious. "In this state, mothers who look like you and me die from having babies at *several times* the rate of white mothers. And when they study it after, they find most of those deaths *didn't have to happen.* Care too late. Somebody not listened to. A warning sign nobody caught." She took Daysha's hand. "I am not telling you that to scare you. I'm telling you so you understand why I will personally walk you into that health center Monday morning. Early care, and somebody in the room who's *for* you — that's the protection. And I am going to be that somebody, all the way through."

**6. There's more than one door, and they don't cancel each other out.**

"Once you're covered, there's a *doula* — Illinois Medicaid pays for one now — a trained woman who stands with you through the pregnancy and the birth and after, and I'll tell you, I've *watched* what a doula does for a scared first-time mama. There's WIC for your food and formula, and that's a *whole separate* thing, you can have both. There's a case-management nurse who keeps you on track. And here's the one that'll surprise you: the coverage doesn't stop six weeks after the baby. It's a *full year* — for your checkups, and for your *mind,* because the weeks after a first baby can get dark and there's no shame and there's help for that too."

Daysha wiped her face with the back of her hand and looked at the phone, then at the old nurse, then at the phone again.

"So the free thing is real," she said slowly, "and the four-thousand-dollar thing is the fake."

"Now you've got it in the right order," said Ophelia. "Now let's go get you your baby's first doctor."
