There is a young woman in that congregation who is always the one holding somebody else's baby so a tired mother can sit down, always the first to bring a plate to the sister who's sick — and she herself has not seen a doctor in years. She's thirty-one. She works — cleans houses, does hair on the side, drives when the app is busy, stitches together a living out of jobs that never once came with a health plan. When something aches, she waits it out. When the cough won't quit, she prices the urgent-care visit in her head and decides she can't spare it. She's had a lump she doesn't like, a blood-pressure reading somebody once told her to "keep an eye on," a prescription she rations because the refill costs more than the pills should — and somewhere along the way she quietly decided that health insurance is simply not for people like her, that she'd never qualify for anything, and that going without is just the cost of doing the kind of work she does. Here is what nobody ever told her plainly: a woman who works without benefits is not reckless for being uninsured — the jobs never handed her a plan and no one ever sat her down — and there is a real, free door: she very likely qualifies for coverage that is free or nearly free, and getting it and finally seeing a doctor is not a luxury and not a handout. It is a working woman taking care of the one body that does all of this.

You know this woman. She's the one who says "I'm fine, it's nothing" when you can see her wince, who spends on everybody's needs before her own, who went without coverage in the first place precisely because she was trying to be responsible with money that never stretches far enough. She'll tell you insurance is for people with "real jobs," and she'll say it like it's settled — while the truth is that the jobs she works simply never came with benefits, and that has nothing to do with whether she deserves care or qualifies for it. Nobody ever sat her down and told her she may well qualify for free Medicaid or CountyCare with the income she has, or that a Marketplace plan can cost a small fraction of its sticker price once the subsidies come off the top. Nobody told her a certified Navigator will sit with her for free and do the entire application alongside her, or that a community health center will see her on a sliding scale whether she has coverage or not. Nobody told her that getting covered means free checkups, screenings, and cheaper prescriptions instead of rationing and waiting. And nobody told her the one thing that protects her the second a "verify your details for your new government health card" robocall or a "final hours to enroll, pay your activation fee now" text reaches her: that no one legitimate charges a fee to enroll her, a real Navigator's help is always free, and HealthCare.gov and Medicaid never cold-call demanding a gift card or her bank login — because the person who learned that a woman who's gone without care for years will grab at any voice that says "you're approved" is exactly the person waiting to sell her nothing and take her Social Security number with it.

And that has to be said plainly, and I will say it again before I'm done: a woman who works without benefits and went uninsured because coverage felt out of reach is not reckless, and she is not a failure. The jobs she works never handed her a health plan, and no one ever told her she probably qualifies for coverage that's free or nearly free — that is a gap in the system, not a flaw in her. Skipping the doctor to keep the lights on was survival math, not carelessness. Qualifying for Medicaid or a subsidized Marketplace plan is not a handout; it is coverage her own work and her own taxes already help pay for. Getting covered and finally sitting in a doctor's office is not weakness — it is a working woman taking care of the one body that does all this work, the body everybody else in her life leans on. Not knowing that a Navigator would help her for nothing, or that she'd qualify at all, was never a personal failing — it is what happens to a woman nobody ever walked through the door. Choosing coverage, keeping her own health, and stopping the slow rationing of her own care is not taking anything from anybody. It is a woman claiming the care her labor already helped fund. That is not a deficit. That is a woman who has been going without something she was owed — and was never handed the number of the people who help for free. And it is the exact moment the junk-plan salesman and the fake "health card" caller are waiting for: a woman who's gone too long without care, wants to finally get right, and just needs somebody to say yes.

And there are doors. Real ones. Doors nobody names for a woman who was taught that care is something you earn only with the right kind of job.

Start with the first thing she needs to hear: it costs nothing to find out what she qualifies for, and no honest enroller ever charges her a fee to sign her up. The real first move is not a plan sold to her over the phone by a stranger with a deadline, and it is not her SSN read to a robocall — it is a free sit-down with a certified Navigator, because for a woman who has convinced herself coverage isn't for her, the distance between "I'd never qualify" and free or nearly-free health insurance is often one free appointment nobody ever told her she was allowed to make.

So here are the doors almost no one names for her plainly. The first is free help from a certified Marketplace Navigator or application counselor — through Get Covered Illinois or HealthCare.gov, found at getcovered.illinois.gov, healthcare.gov, or a free 2-1-1 call — a trained person who does the whole application with her at no charge, whether she's in Harvey, Dolton, Markham, Chicago Heights, Riverdale, Calumet City, South Holland, Blue Island, Robbins, or Ford Heights. But hear this warning before she pays a single dollar or reads a single number over the phone, because the scams hunt a woman who's gone without care and wants coverage badly harder than almost anyone — a woman who'll say yes to the first voice that finally says yes to her. Hear it plainly, and I will say it again at the end: no legitimate Navigator, Marketplace, or Medicaid office charges a fee to enroll her, a real Navigator's help is always free, and no real government health program cold-calls to demand a gift card, a wire, or her banking login. Beware the fake or junk "health plan" — the "limited benefit," "medical discount card," or "short-term" plan pushed over the phone as if it were real ACA insurance, that sounds cheap and pays almost nothing the moment she actually gets sick. Beware the enrollment-fee scam — anyone charging an up-front "processing," "activation," or "application" fee to sign her up, when the Navigator and the enrollment itself are free. Beware the "government health card" or "national health plan" robocall and text, invented deadline and all, fishing for her SSN, her Medicaid ID, and her bank details. And beware the phishing site or text that copies HealthCare.gov or "Get Covered" branding to steal her identity and her money outright. The rule is flat: real coverage never costs a fee just to enroll, and the honest path never runs through a gift card, a wired "activation fee," or an SSN read to a cold caller — she never pays to "activate" or "unlock" a plan, never buys a "discount card" believing it's insurance, and never gives her Social Security number or online-banking login to an unverified caller, texter, or website; so anyone charging her to enroll, selling her a "plan" over the phone with a countdown clock, or calling to demand her details for a "health card," is the scam, not the help.
