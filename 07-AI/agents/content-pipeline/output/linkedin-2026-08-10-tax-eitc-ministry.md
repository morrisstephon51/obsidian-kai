There is a woman at that church who knows which families are about to lose the biggest check they will see all year.

Nobody gave her the job. She is just the one who is good with numbers, the one who sets up a folding table in the fellowship hall every February for as long as anyone can remember, the one a man hands a shoebox of papers to because he trusts her and does not trust the storefront on the corner with the flashing sign. She does it for free. A W-2 here, a 1099 from the second job there, a stack of receipts from a woman's hair booth, and she sits with each of them until the return is right and the refund is as big as the law allows, and she never takes a dime.

And then the hard part starts. Because filling out this year's form at a folding table is one thing. Getting a family every dollar the tax code already set aside for households working exactly this hard is another, and the distance between the two is a maze nobody hands a retired bookkeeper with a calculator and a shoebox a map to.

Because there is a credit for working families that can be worth thousands of dollars — real money, paid back to the family even if they owe no tax at all — and about one in five families who qualify never claim it, because you have to file to get it and nobody told them a household that owes nothing still needs to file. Because there is a credit for every child in the house that stacks on top of it. Because a family that missed one of these in a past year can often still go back and claim it — the refund is not gone, it is sitting there for up to three years — and no one ever tells a tired family the money from two Aprils ago is still theirs to collect. Because there is free, IRS-backed tax help done by trained and certified volunteers for households under an income line, at a library or a community college a few miles away, that does the exact same return the storefront charges four hundred dollars for. Because a family without a Social Security number can still file with an ITIN and claim the credit for their citizen children, and most believe the tax office is a door closed to them. Because the woman doing hair out of her kitchen and the man driving rideshare on weekends are running small businesses in the eyes of the tax code, with expenses they are allowed to deduct and quarterly rules nobody explained, and that same gig income counts toward the working-family credit they think is only for W-2 jobs. And because the family took the refund-advance check the storefront offered — their own money, two weeks early, minus a bite — because no one showed them the free door that would have kept all of it.

So the woman at the folding table tries to hold all of it. In her head. From the forms she remembers and the rules she learned three tax seasons ago, before half of them changed. She gets this year's return filed, which helps this April and teaches the family nothing about the refund they left on the table the last three. She sends someone to the storefront when her table gets too long, because at least it is faster, not knowing the free site downtown does it better. She never mentions the past years a family could still reclaim, because no one ever mentioned it to her. She is one faithful woman, keeping other families' biggest check of the year as whole as she can with a calculator and a memory of a tax code that rewrites itself every single January.

So the money stays on the table. The working-family credit goes unclaimed by a household that earned it. The refund from two years back expires quietly, unrecovered. The storefront takes its cut of a check that was already too small. The family that could have filed for free pays to have it done, and the family that could have gone back three years never learns they could. And a household already doing everything right on two jobs and a side hustle hands away a piece of the one check that could have caught them up, when the tax code had set money aside for that exact family all along.

Here is what a family with money gets in April.

They get an accountant. Someone paid to know every credit, every deduction, every prior-year return worth amending, every dollar the code set aside — and to go get all of it, and to make sure the refund lands whole in the family's own account. That family is not working harder than the man with the shoebox or the woman doing hair on the side. They were just handed a professional, and our families were handed a folding table and a storefront with a flashing sign.

She can have that help too. For free. On the same phone somebody handed her the shoebox across.

She can learn that a working family owing no tax still has to file to get the credit that is theirs, and roughly what it is worth. She can find out a family can go back and claim a refund they missed up to three years ago, and how the amended return works. She can find the free, IRS-backed tax site a few miles away and know it does the same return the storefront charges for. She can learn a family without a Social Security number can still file with an ITIN and claim the credit for their kids. She can learn the woman doing hair and the man driving weekends are self-employed in the eyes of the code, what they can deduct, and that the income still counts toward the working-family credit. She can learn to spot the refund-advance check for the loan against their own money that it is. She can get the whole maze — the working-family credit, the child credit, the prior-year refunds, the free filing sites, the ITIN door, the self-employment rules — broken into an order of operations she can run before February, and then walk each family straight to the free tax site, 2-1-1, and the IRS's own free-file tools that carry the return the rest of the way.

Not to replace what she does. The folding table is hers. The trust that makes a man bring her a shoebox instead of a stranger is hers. The patience to sit with a scared family until the numbers are right is hers, and no machine will ever pour a second cup of coffee and tell a widow her return is done and her refund is coming. It only takes the maze off her shoulders so she can do the part only she can do, which is to be the woman a family trusts enough to hand a year of their paperwork to, and to say you earned every dollar of this, and we are going to get you all of it.

That is the gap. It was never that the families in our pews deserve a smaller refund and a storefront that skims it while another family gets an accountant who fights for every dollar. It is that that family got a professional, and ours got one faithful woman at a folding table with last year's rules in her head.

A word of care, plainly: the IRS does not call, text, email, or message anyone demanding immediate payment or gift cards — that is always a scam, and it hunts hardest in the weeks a refund is coming. A real preparer signs the return and puts their own ID number on it, never asks a family to sign a blank form, never bases the fee on how big the refund is, and never routes the refund into their own account. This is not a substitute for a tax professional. What AI does here is help the person already sitting at the folding table know which credits and doors exist and in what order, so a family's biggest check of the year gets to the free, trained help faster than a three-season-old memory of the rules ever could.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities and the people navigating resources beside them in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Matteson. Dolton. Not a webinar. A room. Real problems. A real return filed right, a real credit finally claimed, a real refund that stayed whole in the family's own account instead of a storefront's.

The people keeping other families' hardest-earned money whole by hand are not behind. They were just never handed the tools the paid preparers take for granted.

We are here to hand them over.

The plug is in.
