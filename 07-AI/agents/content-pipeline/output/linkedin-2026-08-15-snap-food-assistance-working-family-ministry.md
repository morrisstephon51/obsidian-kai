There is a man in that church who has not eaten a real meal since Tuesday, and nobody knows, because he has gotten very good at saying "I already ate."

You know him. He clocks in on a forklift at a warehouse before the sun is up, takes the Saturday shift when he can get it, and has provided for his family his whole life without once asking anybody for anything. Then his overtime dried up. In the same season, they cut his wife's hours too. And somewhere in there the arithmetic quietly stopped working — the same paycheck, the same three kids, but a full grocery cart costs what it costs now, and there is more month than there is money. So he did what a proud provider does. He started taking himself off the plate. Coffee for breakfast. Nothing for lunch. "I'm not that hungry, give it to the kids" at dinner. He is going without food so his children don't have to, and he has told no one, because in his mind there is a hard line: food stamps are for people who don't work — and he works — so the help must not be for him.

That line is a lie. That is the part that has to be said plainly, and I will say it again before I am done: SNAP — what people still call food stamps — is not a handout for people who won't work. It was built for exactly his household. Working families qualify for it all the time. He has paid into the system that funds it for thirty years of pay stubs, and it is sitting there, right now, meant for a father in precisely his squeeze — and the only thing keeping food off his own table is a sentence somebody taught him a long time ago that was never true.

And there are doors. Real ones. Doors he has walked past his whole life because nobody ever told him they were his to open.

Start with the first thing he needs to hear: needing food help this year does not erase a lifetime of work. A man can work full-time and still not clear the cost of feeding three growing kids after his hours got cut — that is not a failure of his character, it is a fact of the arithmetic, and the arithmetic is not his fault. Cut overtime is not laziness. A cut second income is not overspending. He did not do anything wrong. The season did.

So here is the door almost no one names for him plainly: SNAP itself. It is applied for free — in this state through the ABE application, through the Department of Human Services — and working households qualify for it every single day. It is not a check somebody hands the undeserving. It is a benefit a working man paid into, meant for a working family in a hard stretch, and there is no shame in reaching for the thing that was built for you. He does not know that. He thinks it is for somebody else. It is for him.

And there is help for this week, while any application is still pending — because a family cannot eat "pending." Food pantries. Real groceries, handed to a working family, from the Greater Chicago Food Depository's network, from mobile pantries that set up close to home, and from the pantry that is very likely two doors down from the sanctuary where he already worships on Sunday. No long form. No interrogation. No shame. For the little ones there is WIC — food and nutrition help for pregnant and new moms and children under five, a separate door from SNAP. And for the school-age kids there are free and reduced-price school meals and summer meal sites, so that at the very least the children get two solid meals a day that don't come out of the grocery money at all. He is standing in front of a whole row of doors — SNAP, a pantry open this week, WIC, meals for the kids, the food ministry in his own church, and one phone call to 211 that maps all of it — and he is starving quietly in front of them because nobody ever told him a single one was his to open.

But hear this warning before he starts searching, because trouble hunts people in his exact spot. The minute a family gets on food assistance, the thieves are already circling. There are skimmers — little devices clamped onto card readers at stores and ATMs — that copy an EBT card and drain a family's whole month of food benefits the moment they load, and once it's gone it is brutally hard to get back. Protect the PIN like it's cash, check the balance often, lock or freeze the card in the state's app when you can, and change the PIN before benefits post each month. And then there is the text, the call, the email that says "your SNAP case will close — recertify now," "verify your EBT card," "confirm your PIN to keep your benefits." Hear it plainly, and I will say it again at the end: the state does not ask for a PIN by text or a phone call. Applying is always free. Nobody honest charges a fee to "get you food stamps." Anybody who calls, texts, or emails asking for the EBT card number, the PIN, the Social Security number, or the date of birth to "unlock" or "protect" the benefits is not the help. That is somebody hunting a hungry family while they are looking down.

So he goes without. Not because he is lazy — lazy is not what puts a man on a forklift before dawn and back for a Saturday shift. Not because they overspent — his overtime and her hours got cut in the same season, which is not a character flaw, it is a Tuesday. He is going hungry because nobody ever walked him to the doors, and the one sentence he did learn about them — "that help is for people who don't work" — is the exact lie built to keep a working man out.

Here is the thing about the family across town. Same story. Cut overtime, cut hours, three kids, a cart that costs more than the paycheck holds. But a friend caught the father before he wore himself down to nothing, and said: "Listen to me. SNAP is not a handout — working people qualify, you paid into it, go apply. And the pantry on Ninety-Fifth is open Thursday, we'll go together." And they did. The application went in. The pantry filled the gap that week. The kids ate, and so did their parents, and the man kept his strength up for the job that keeps the lights on — and nobody at his work, his church, or his kids' school ever knew there had been a lean season, because there was a door and he walked through it. He was not smarter than the man in our pew. He was not better with money. He had one friend who said "this help is for you, not just for other people," and knew the doors were there, and walked him to them. One family stayed fed with their dignity whole. The other slowly starved the provider and ran the grocery bill onto a credit card at interest that will outlast the hard season by years — over a benefit the father already qualified for and had already paid into. The whole difference was a friend who said "food stamps are not for people who don't work," and a silence where nobody ever did.

The man in our pew can be walked to those same doors now. Not a handout he should be ashamed to ask about. The plain, ordinary help a working family paid into and qualifies for, sitting there the whole time, that no one ever told him to reach for.

Because this is where a tool like AI actually meets a man like him — quietly, at the kitchen table, after the kids are asleep and the "I already ate" is done for the day — doing the part that the shame and the strange words and the not-knowing-where-to-start have been blocking. It can be the patient someone that explains what "SNAP" and "EBT" and "the ABE application" and "recertification" and "WIC" actually mean, in plain words, as many times as he needs, and never once makes a grown working father feel like a beggar for asking. It can help him figure out which documents to gather before he applies — the IDs, the pay stubs, the rent, the bills. It can help him find the pantry that's actually open this week, and the SNAP application, and the WIC office, and the summer-meal site near him, instead of leaving him to search, exhausted and embarrassed, at midnight. It can help him understand recertification so his case doesn't quietly lapse. And it can help him recognize the skimmer and the phishing text before they clean out a month of his family's food.

Not to be the one who decides anything. Let that be plain. The machine does not decide whether he qualifies, or for how much — that is the state's determination, on the official application, with a caseworker, not a chatbot's guess. It does not tell him what to report or how to fill it out. It does not replace the SNAP office or the pantry. And it must never, ever be fed his Social Security number, his EBT card number or PIN, his bank details, or his case number — a public AI tool is not the place for a single line of a family's private information. It is only the patient someone that takes a shame-blocked, word-thick, where-do-I-even-start thing and makes it feel walkable, so that a man who works before the sun comes up does not sit at his own table with an empty plate for no reason at all.

That is the gap. It was never that the family across town was more responsible, or better with money, or more deserving of food. Same cut overtime. Same cut hours. Same three kids and the same too-expensive cart. It is that one father had a friend who said "this help is for working people, you qualify, let's go," and the other was left to believe a lie and quietly starve in front of a row of open doors. Not less responsible. Not less faithful. Just never told the doors were his.

A word of care, plainly, and I will say the important parts twice. This is general navigation, not an endorsement of any one program or pantry, and not a decision about who qualifies. Who is eligible, what counts as income, and what has to be reported belong to the state SNAP office and the official application and a caseworker, not to a machine and not to a guess. AI tools can be confidently wrong, so never trust an income limit, a benefit amount, or a rule one gives you without confirming it with the official state program and 211. And never put a Social Security number, an EBT card number or PIN, bank details, or a case number into a public AI tool. Hear the heart of it twice, because it is built for people in exactly this spot: applying for SNAP and WIC is always free, no honest program charges a fee, and no real agency will ever ask for your EBT PIN by text or phone — anyone who does is a thief. Route the real help to real, trusted places: the state SNAP application and DHS first; the Greater Chicago Food Depository's pantries and mobile pantries and your own church's food ministry for groceries this week; WIC for the little ones; free and reduced and summer meals for the kids; and 211 or 211.org to find all of it in one call.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Dolton. Robbins. Chicago Heights. Matteson. South Holland. Not a webinar. A room. Real people. A real hardworking father, taught how to make the same tool the family across town already knew how to use sit with him at the kitchen table, explain the words nobody ever explained, find the pantry that's open this week and the application he qualifies for, and turn "I already ate" into a full plate and a fed family — instead of a proud man wearing himself down to nothing in silence.

The faithful men in our churches are not going hungry because they are lazy. They are going hungry because nobody ever told them the help was theirs, and they were too proud to say the words out loud.

We are here to walk them to it.

The plug is in.
