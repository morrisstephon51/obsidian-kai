Deacon Raymond has led the Men's Fellowship breakfast at First Corinthian Missionary Baptist in Blue Island for eleven years.

Every second Saturday, he pulls the same corner table, orders the same two scrambled eggs, and listens. He does not ask what men in his group are going through. He creates enough quiet that they eventually say it themselves.

Two Saturdays ago, Marcus said it.

Marcus is 38. He grew up in Blue Island, came up in this church — junior choir, youth ushers, VBS every summer until he aged out. He works at a logistics warehouse off I-57 as a forklift operator. He is reliable, licensed, and classified as Tier 2 skilled. His supervisor has been asking him to move to the early shift — 5am to 2pm, more predictable, slightly higher base pay — for four months. He keeps saying he needs to think about it.

What he did not say until that second Saturday was this: his co-parent left last November, moved to Indianapolis for a relationship that became another life. He has their two children — Jerome, 7, and Amara, 4 — full time. He is currently on the 10am-7pm shift because it lines up with his mother's schedule. She comes over at 9:30, stays until he gets home. But his mother is 66, dealing with her own health situation, and the 5am shift would mean leaving two children alone or finding care by 4:45 in the morning.

He cannot take the shift.

He is not asking for help. He is just explaining why he is where he is.

What Deacon Raymond knew to do is exactly what he did: he did not turn it into a prayer request. He said, "Marcus, have you ever looked into the child care assistance program?"

**What CCAP actually is:**

CCAP is the Illinois Child Care Assistance Program, administered by the Illinois Department of Human Services (IDHS). It is a state and federally funded subsidy that pays a portion — in many cases the large majority — of child care costs for eligible low- and moderate-income working families.

CCAP is not a daycare directory. It is a financial subsidy. You choose your own licensed or license-exempt child care provider; CCAP pays that provider directly on your behalf. You pay a co-payment — typically a small weekly amount based on your income and family size — and the state covers the rest of what the provider charges, up to the established state market rate.

CCAP covers:
- Licensed child care centers
- Licensed home-based day care providers
- License-exempt care in certain circumstances, including in some cases relative care — a neighbor, an aunt, a trusted family friend who is formally enrolled as a CCAP-approved provider

It covers full-day and part-day arrangements. It covers before- and after-school care for school-age children. It can cover non-standard hours — early mornings, evenings, weekends — for families whose work schedules don't follow a 9-to-5 pattern. For Marcus, starting a shift at 5am, CCAP can fund a provider who accepts early-morning drop-offs. Those providers exist. IDHS's regional referral agencies can help find them.

**Who qualifies, in plain terms:**

You must be an Illinois resident. Your child must be under age 13 (or under 18 if the child has a qualifying disability or special need). You must be working, in school, or in an approved training or job search program. Your income must fall at or below 185% of the federal poverty level for initial eligibility — for a family of three (one adult, two children), that is currently in the range of approximately $5,800 per month in gross income. Above that, you do not qualify to enter the program. But if you are already enrolled in CCAP and your income rises, you can remain enrolled up to a higher income ceiling — an intentional graduated exit designed to prevent the kind of cliff where a raise costs a family more in lost child care than it pays.

For Marcus at his current forklift rate — even factoring in the shift upgrade he has been declining — he likely qualifies comfortably.

**What CCAP pays:**

The state pays directly to the provider, at state-established market rates for the child's age group and care setting. Your co-pay is set on a sliding scale — the lower your income, the lower your weekly co-payment. For many families in the income range typical of essential-worker households in South Suburban Cook County, the weekly co-pay is in the $5–$30 range. The state covers the remainder.

This is not a small benefit. Licensed full-time infant and toddler care in the Chicago area frequently runs $1,400–$1,800 per month per child. CCAP does not pay at that level for all provider types — it pays at regional market rates the state sets and periodically updates — but it absorbs a very large share of the cost, reducing the family's out-of-pocket expense to a manageable co-pay.

**How to apply:**

**ABE — Application for Benefits Eligibility:** The fastest route is **abe.illinois.gov**. The ABE portal handles CCAP, SNAP, TANF, and Medicaid in a single unified application. You can apply online in a single session — roughly 45 minutes to an hour. You will need: your income documentation (recent pay stubs, employer name, hourly rate, current hours per week); your children's birth dates and Social Security numbers; and information about your work or school status. You can name a specific provider or leave the provider field for later — IDHS will process eligibility first, and you can select a provider once the authorization is issued.

**IDHS Child Care line:** **1-800-843-6154**. IDHS staff can take a CCAP application over the phone, answer eligibility questions, explain how the co-payment scale works for your household, and tell you what documentation to gather before your application interview.

**Cook County FCRC — Markham:** 16241 Kedzie Pkwy, Markham, IL 60428 / **708-210-3000**. In-person CCAP applications and case management for South Suburban Cook County. Walk-ins are accepted; calling ahead to confirm hours saves time.

**Child Care Resource and Referral (CCR&R):** Illinois funds regional Child Care Resource and Referral agencies whose job is to help working parents find licensed, CCAP-approved providers in their area — including providers with early-morning availability for non-standard shift workers. For South Suburban Cook County, staff can search for providers by ZIP code, hours of operation, and age group at no cost to you. Access through 211 or the IDHS website.

**211 Illinois:** Call or text **211**. 211 can connect Marcus to the nearest CCR&R office, emergency backup child care resources while a CCAP application is processing, and other family support programs that coordinate with CCAP.

**Where to go with questions:**

**IDHS — Illinois Department of Human Services:** dhs.illinois.gov / **1-800-843-6154**. For eligibility questions, application status, co-payment amounts, provider enrollment status, and case updates.

**ABE Portal:** abe.illinois.gov. Online CCAP application, case status, income updates, and provider authorization. Available 24 hours.

**IDHS Child Care Provider Search:** Available through the IDHS website and the ABE portal — searchable by city, ZIP code, provider type, age group, and hours. Marcus should use this to verify that any provider he selects is currently licensed, enrolled with CCAP, and in good standing before authorizing payments.

**Child Care Resource and Referral (CCR&R):** Regional network for provider searches, early childhood referrals, and CCAP navigation. Accessible through IDHS, 211, or the Illinois Network of Child Care Resource and Referral Agencies (INCCRRA) at inccrra.org.

**Illinois Legal Aid Online:** illinoislegalaid.org. Free help with CCAP denials, co-payment disputes, provider authorization issues, and appeals of IDHS decisions.

**CARPLS:** carpls.org / **312-738-9200**. Free civil legal hotline for Cook County residents — benefits appeals, consumer issues, tenant rights, family law.

**The scam that runs through this:**

When Marcus searches "child care help for single parents Illinois" or "CCAP assistance Cook County," he enters an information space where legitimate state resources sit alongside operations designed to extract money from people who are already stretched thin.

**The enrollment fee scam:** Websites and social media ads — some running targeted campaigns in zip codes with high IDHS enrollment rates — offer to "apply for CCAP" or "enroll your family in the Illinois child care program" for a fee, sometimes bundled into a "government benefits package" or "family resource enrollment." The CCAP application is free on ABE. IDHS staff are free. No one charging a fee to file a CCAP application is providing a service that cannot be done at no cost, on a phone, in a single session. The fee accomplishes nothing. The state application takes the same amount of time regardless of who submits it.

**The unlicensed provider scam:** Some child care providers operate without a state license and without CCAP enrollment, but advertise that they accept CCAP as a way to attract families. They take a family's CCAP authorization code as informal credentialing and may attempt to bill the state for care that does not meet safety standards, is not properly documented, or is not actually being provided. Families who place children with providers operating this way can face CCAP suspension for improper use of the subsidy — through no fault of their own, but still their problem to resolve. Before Marcus authorizes any CCAP payment to a provider, he should verify that provider's license and CCAP enrollment status in the IDHS Child Care Provider Search. Licensed. Enrolled. Current status. All three.

**The case review credential phishing call:** CCAP recipients receive texts and calls claiming that their "child care case is under review" or that there is "a co-payment record discrepancy that must be corrected to avoid suspension of benefits." The caller asks for the IDHS case number, Social Security number, or Link card PIN to "verify the account." Real IDHS case workers do not request your Social Security number, bank account, or Link card details over an unsolicited call or text. If Marcus receives this contact, the right move is to hang up and call IDHS directly at **1-800-843-6154** to confirm whether there is actually anything flagged on his case.

Deacon Raymond has seen it move in real moments of need — utility shutoffs stopped, a week's worth of groceries covered during a gap month, an emergency car repair that kept someone employed. He has also seen it stretched thin by situations that recurred month after month because the underlying cost — child care — was never resolved. Families paying out of pocket for care they qualified to have subsidized, quietly depleting a fund that was designed for crises, not structural income gaps.

CCAP is not the benevolence fund's job. The benevolence fund's job is the gap that nothing else covers.
