There is a young man in that church who sets up the chairs before service and stacks them after, and never once asks to be thanked. He is eighteen. He is a senior, and he is quick — quicker than he lets on — and he works a shift and still turns his homework in, and he is the first person in his whole family who has ever gotten close enough to college to smell it. And somewhere in the last year, without telling anybody, he made a quiet decision. He decided it was not for him. Not because he can't do the work. Because when he looked at what it costs, the number had too many zeros, and nobody in his house had ever filled out the forms, and nobody at any desk ever pulled him aside and said, son, there is a way, and it starts with something that is free. So he stopped talking about it. He tells people he might just work for a while. And he means it, because he thinks the door is locked, when the truth is nobody ever told him it isn't even shut.

You know him. He is not lazy and he is not dreaming above himself — he is a first-generation student, which means he is trying to walk a road nobody who raised him has ever walked, with no map, in a system that is confusing on purpose. And the thing that stopped him is not a lack of brains. It is a lack of one sentence nobody said out loud. Because there is an application, and its actual name begins with the word Free — the FAFSA, the Free Application for Federal Student Aid — and filing it does not cost him a dime, and it is the single key that can unlock a grant he may never have to pay back, a work-study job, and the lower-cost federal loans, and it is the same key most states and most colleges use to hand out their own money too. He does not know that. Nobody named it for him. So he assumes college is a thing for other people's kids, and that assumption is exactly the empty space the predators move into.

And that is the part that has to be said plainly, and I will say it again before I am done: a first-generation student did not fail because no one before him knew the way, the most important door is free to open, real people will help him open it for free, and no honest helper charges him a fee to file a free application or "find" him scholarships he could find himself. Filing the FAFSA is free on the official federal student aid site. Getting help to fill it out is free — a counselor, a college's financial aid office, programs built for exactly him. Real scholarships never charge him to receive them. He does not know any of that, because nobody sat down and told a kid stacking chairs that the road he thinks is closed was paved for him by law. And that not-knowing is the door the scammers are standing in.

And there are doors. Real ones. Doors nobody names for a first-generation student who has already quietly told himself no.

Claiming aid that federal law and his own state built for exactly a kid like him is not begging and it is not a handout. It is opening a door that already has his name written on it. The certainty that it's not for him is not the truth. It is the lever.

So here are the doors almost no one names for him plainly. The first is the key to all of it: the FAFSA is free to file on the official federal student aid site, and it can open the door to a federal grant — money that, for those who qualify, generally does not have to be paid back — to a work-study job, and to lower-cost federal loans, and most states and most colleges use it to give out their own aid too. The third is the scholarships: free scholarship searches exist, and a real scholarship never charges him a fee to apply for it or to collect it, so any money that costs money to receive is a lie. The fourth is the human being behind the desk: if his family's situation changed, or the number that came back looks impossible, he can go to the college's financial aid office and ask about a special-circumstances review, with the process set by the school and the Department of Education.
