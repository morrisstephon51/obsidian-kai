There is a woman in that church who almost wired gift cards to a stranger who called her, and almost signed away thousands of dollars to a company off a TV commercial, because she opened an envelope from the IRS that said she owed money she does not have — and she was so afraid of being arrested that both of those traps looked like the only way out.

You know her. Forty-seven. She is a home health aide — a CNA — and she spends her days bathing, feeding, and lifting other people's aging parents, the kind of work that is all hands and all heart and never pays what it costs your body. To keep up, she took on some side work, the 1099 kind, the kind where nobody takes taxes out for you and nobody tells you that you were supposed to set money aside. She did not know. And a couple of weeks ago an official-looking envelope came, and the letter inside said she owed back taxes, and there were penalties on top, and the number at the bottom was more money than she has ever had in one place at one time. Then the phone started ringing. A man with a hard, official voice, saying he was a federal agent, saying there was a warrant, saying she would be arrested today unless she paid right now — in gift cards. And in between the calls, a commercial kept playing, a friendly company promising to settle her tax debt for "pennies on the dollar" if she just called and paid their fee. So a good, hardworking woman, terrified of handcuffs she had done nothing to earn, reached for her purse and reached for the phone.

She was about to walk into two traps at once. That is the part that has to be said plainly, and I will say it again before I am done: the real IRS mails you first and will never call demanding gift cards or threatening to have you arrested this afternoon, and a company that swears it can make a tax bill vanish for a big fee upfront is very often a predator, not a rescue. She did not know either of those things. She thought the caller was the government, the gift cards were the only way to stop the arrest, and the "tax-relief" company was her lifeline. None of that was necessarily true — and the only thing standing between her and a much deeper hole was information nobody handed her with the notice.

And there are doors. Real ones. Doors nobody names for a working woman the week a tax bill lands, because who wants to say out loud at coffee hour that the IRS is after her.

Start with the first thing she needs to hear: owing on side work you did not know was taxable is not cheating, and being scared of an IRS letter does not make you a criminal. She did not hide income or run a scheme — she took extra shifts under a 1099 and nobody ever told her that a side gig comes with a tax nobody withholds. She is not a cheat. She is a woman who takes care of other people's parents for a living and picked up more work to keep her own head above water. That is a paperwork gap, not a character flaw. Wanting to pay what she truly owes is honorable — but there is no version of doing the right thing that requires her to wire gift cards to a stranger or sign thousands over to a company off a commercial.

So here are the doors almost no one names for her plainly. The first is the one the panic makes hardest to see: do not pay anyone yet, and check that the notice is even real. The real IRS reaches out by mail first, the letter has a notice number and it tells you your right to respond and appeal, and you call the number printed on the official notice or on IRS.gov — never a number a caller gives you. If the debt is real, the IRS has payment options — an installment agreement, a payment plan, and for someone who genuinely cannot pay, possibly an offer in compromise or a "currently-not-collectible" status. There is free tax help she has probably never heard of: VITA sites that prepare returns for free, the Taxpayer Advocate Service when she is stuck inside the system, and Low-Income Taxpayer Clinics that will help her dispute a notice or even represent her. The notice itself might be wrong — underreporting letters sometimes double-count income or miss deductions she is owed, and she has the right to respond and correct it. She may be owed credits, like the EITC, that lower the whole bill. Legal aid and a Low-Income Taxpayer Clinic exist for a real dispute and for a "tax-relief" ripoff. And her own church's benevolence fund, plus one call to 211, can help with the gap and map the local free help. She is standing in front of a whole row of doors — verify the notice, a payment plan, free tax help, the right to dispute, credits she may be owed, a low-income clinic, and her church and 211 — and she is reaching for gift cards, because nobody ever told her a single one of those doors was hers to open.

But hear this warning before she pays anyone a dime, because the thieves hunt the people who just opened a scary letter. Hear it plainly, and I will say it again at the end: the real IRS contacts you by mail first, never demands gift cards, prepaid cards, wire, or crypto, and never threatens to send police to arrest you over the phone. A caller who does any of that is a thief working through fear. The "settle your tax debt for pennies on the dollar" company is not always a phone scam, but it is often a trap — they charge thousands upfront and frequently deliver nothing, when the real offer-in-compromise she might qualify for can be pursued for free through a low-income clinic. There are "ghost" preparers who promise a giant refund, take a cut, refuse to sign the return, and disappear when it falls apart. There are "instant refund" loans that eat the refund with fees. And there are phishing texts and emails that say "verify your refund" or "your IRS account is locked, click here," built only to steal a Social Security number or a bank login. The rule is flat: the IRS mails you first and never wants gift cards or threatens arrest by phone, real tax help is free — VITA, the Taxpayer Advocate, a Low-Income Taxpayer Clinic — you never pay a stranger and never hand your Social Security number to a caller or a strange website, and you make anyone prove it in writing before you pay a cent.

So she almost paid. Not because she is foolish — foolish is not the word for a woman who lifts grown adults out of bed all day and never calls in sick. Not because she cheated — a 1099 nobody explained is not a scheme, it is a gap nobody closed for her. She almost paid because nobody ever walked her to the doors, and the only voices that spoke up about her tax bill were the one built to rob her with gift cards and the one built to bleed her with a fee.

Here is the thing about the woman across town. Same story. Same envelope, same balance due, same penalties, same "federal agent" threatening arrest, same pennies-on-the-dollar company on the screen. But a friend had already sat with her at the kitchen table and said: "Listen. Before you pay anybody, know this — the real IRS mails you first and never asks for gift cards, so that caller is a thief; hang up. You can ask the IRS for a payment plan, you don't have to have it all today. And there's free help — a clinic, the Taxpayer Advocate — that will sit with you and even check whether that notice is right." So she called the number on the actual letter, set up an installment agreement she could handle, and got a free clinic to look at the notice — which had overstated what she owed. She kept her money and her peace, and she never wired a gift card or signed a "tax-relief" contract. She was not smarter than the woman in our pew. She did not deserve it more. She had one friend who knew the doors were there and named them before the fear took over. One woman kept her savings and her sleep. The other almost lost both to a thief on the phone and a company on TV. The whole difference was somebody who said "the IRS mails you first, ask for a payment plan, get the free help," and a silence where nobody ever did.

The woman in our pew can be met before she pays. Not with a lecture about how she should have kept better records. With a hand on the shoulder and the plain truth that she has doors, and that a scary letter from the IRS does not make her a criminal or a mark for the next voice that calls.

Because this is where a tool like AI actually meets a woman like her — quietly, at the kitchen table late at night, when the notice and the penalty math are spread out in front of her and the phone will not stop — doing the part that the fear and the strange words and the not-knowing-where-to-start have been blocking. It can be the patient someone that explains what "an installment agreement," "an offer in compromise," "currently-not-collectible," "a CP2000 underreporting notice," "penalty abatement," "the Taxpayer Advocate," and "a Low-Income Taxpayer Clinic" actually mean, in plain words, as many times as she needs, and never once makes a working woman feel small for asking. It can help her read what the notice is actually saying and what it is actually asking her to do. It can help her put the words together — a letter responding to the notice, a request for a payment plan — so she is not deciding blind or paying in a panic. And it can help her recognize the gift-card arrest scam, the ghost preparer, and the pennies-on-the-dollar pitch for exactly what they are, before they take one more thing from her.

Not to be the one who decides anything. Let that be plain. The machine does not decide what she owes, and it does not get to promise the debt reduced or forgiven — that belongs to the IRS and to the law, not a chatbot's guess. It does not determine her tax liability or whether she qualifies for any program, and it is not the IRS, not a CPA, not a tax attorney. And it must never, ever be fed her Social Security number, her ITIN, or a bank or card number — a public AI tool is not the place for a single line of a family's private information. It is only the patient someone that takes a terrifying, word-thick, where-do-I-even-start letter and makes it walkable, so that a woman who did the honest thing does not wire away her savings or sign over thousands to a thief or a firm for no reason at all.

That is the gap. It was never that the woman across town deserved it more, or was better with money, or was less afraid. Same envelope. Same penalties. Same caller. Same commercial. It is that one woman had a friend who said "the IRS mails you first, ask for a payment plan, get the free help, don't pay that caller," and the other was left alone at the table with a bill she could not pay and a phone that would not stop ringing. Not less deserving. Not less honest. Just never told the doors were hers.

A word of care, plainly, and I will say the important parts twice. This is general navigation, not tax or legal advice, not an endorsement of any preparer, firm, or program, and not a decision about what you owe or whether you qualify for anything. Tax liability and program eligibility are decided by the IRS and the law — not by a machine and not by a guess. AI tools can be confidently wrong, so never trust an amount, a rule, or an eligibility answer one gives you without confirming it with the official source — IRS.gov, the number printed on the official mailed notice, the Taxpayer Advocate Service, a VITA site, and a Low-Income Taxpayer Clinic — and 211. And never put a Social Security number or an ITIN into a public AI tool or hand it to a caller. Hear the heart of it twice, because it is built for a family in exactly this spot: the real IRS contacts you by mail first and never demands gift cards or threatens to arrest you over the phone — so if a caller does, it is a thief, and real tax help is free. Route the real help to real, trusted places: the number on your actual IRS notice or IRS.gov for a payment plan; a VITA site for free preparation; the Taxpayer Advocate Service when you are stuck; a Low-Income Taxpayer Clinic for a dispute or an offer in compromise; your own church's benevolence fund for the gap; and 211 or 211.org to find local free tax help in one call.

A real working woman, taught how to make the same tool the woman across town already knew how to use sit with her at the kitchen table, explain the words nobody explained, help her read the notice and draft a payment-plan letter, and name the "you'll be arrested, pay in gift cards" caller and the "pennies on the dollar" firm for what they are — so that she can keep her money and her peace instead of wiring it away in a panic while a thief waits on the line.

The faithful women in our churches are not falling into these traps because they cheated or were careless. They are falling because a letter came they did not understand, and nobody ever told them the IRS has a payment-plan door and that a caller is not the same as the government — and fear is the worst possible time to pay anyone.
