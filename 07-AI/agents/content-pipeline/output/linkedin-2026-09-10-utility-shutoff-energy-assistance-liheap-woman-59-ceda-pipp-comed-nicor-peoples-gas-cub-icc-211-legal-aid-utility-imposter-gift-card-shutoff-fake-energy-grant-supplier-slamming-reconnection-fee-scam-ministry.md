There is a woman in that congregation who is almost always the one helping someone else. All day she is a home health aide — she lifts a man twice her size out of a bed he can no longer leave, she bathes a grandmother whose own children live too far to come, she measures out pills and warms up soup and holds a stranger's hand while he tells her about a wife who died in 1974. She is good at it because she is kind, and she is tired in a way that sleep doesn't fix. And when her shift is over she does not get to rest, because at home there is a grandbaby she is raising and a grown son with a disability she has cared for his whole life, and the money that has to cover all of it is fixed, and thin, and it does not stretch. Every year when the cold comes down on Illinois she starts doing a math problem that has no answer: the gas bill, the electric bill, the medicine, the food, the little one's coat that no longer fits. This year she has fallen a little behind on ComEd and on Nicor, and she lies awake with it. And into exactly that kind of fear — a house that has to stay warm, a bill that outran the paycheck, a woman too proud and too tired to tell anyone — the phone rings. A calm, official voice. Her utility's name, even its real number, right there on the caller ID. "Ma'am, your account is scheduled for disconnection. Your power will be shut off within the hour unless you make a payment right now." And her heart drops through the floor, because there is a baby in that house and a son who needs the machines that plug into the wall, and the voice already has her by the throat before she can think. Here is what nobody has sat her down and told her plainly, and what somebody has to: that call is a lie built to rob her, the real utility does not work that way, and Illinois already built her a road — a road that starts free, and starts with her hanging up.

You know this woman. She would be mortified that you know she's behind on a bill. She has spent her whole life being the strong one, the one who helps, and somewhere along the way she decided that needing help herself is a kind of failing. And the truth is so much kinder and more solid than that shame will let her believe: a woman who cares for other people's parents all day and her own grandchild and disabled son all night, and who falls behind on the heat when a fixed income meets an Illinois January and a rate hike, has not failed at anything. That is not carelessness. That is arithmetic — the arithmetic that catches careful, hardworking people every winter. Being behind on ComEd or Nicor is a bill to be worked, not a verdict on her character. Needing energy assistance is not a handout and it is not begging; LIHEAP is money set aside on purpose, by law, for a household exactly like hers, and using it is precisely what it was built for. Asking CEDA or the Citizens Utility Board or the utility itself for help is not weakness — it is the wise, responsible thing a caregiver does to protect the people under her roof. And being frightened by a shutoff call does not make her a fool; those calls are engineered by professionals to jump straight past a good person's judgment and land on her fear. Nobody told her that the real first move, when a voice on the phone starts a one-hour clock, is simply to hang up and call the number on her own bill. Nobody told her that LIHEAP will help pay the heat, that a plan called PIPP can cap what she owes at a share of what she actually makes, that ComEd and Nicor and Peoples Gas run their own hardship programs, and that a free watchdog called CUB will take her side. And nobody warned her that the single most dangerous thing she can do this winter is buy a gift card because a stranger told her to.

And that has to be said plainly, and I will say it again before I'm done: falling behind on a utility bill while you work all day caring for others and all night caring for your family is not a moral failing, and it is not something to hide until it becomes an emergency. It is one of the most ordinary things that happens to decent people on a fixed income when the temperature drops. Choosing to face it the wise, patient, honest way — hang up on the threats, call the real number, apply for the real help, ask for the real plan — instead of scrambling for gift cards because a spoofed phone number frightened her, is not being difficult and it is not being slow. It is being a good steward of a household that depends on her completely. That is not a deficit. That is exactly the steadiness that keeps a caregiver from being robbed — because the predators in this business hunt fear, and a woman scared enough about a cold house to stop asking questions is the easiest mark they know.

And there are doors. Real ones. Doors nobody names for a tired woman who has been handling everything alone.

Start with the first thing she needs to hear, because it is the move that changes everything: when a call, a text, or a knock at the door says "pay right now or your power goes off tonight," she hangs up. She does not argue, she does not pay, she does not stay on the line — she hangs up and calls only the number printed on her actual ComEd, Nicor, or Peoples Gas bill. The real utility does not run one-hour clocks, does not demand gift cards, and cannot survive her hanging up and dialing the real number herself. That one habit — hang up, call the number on the bill — defeats the most dangerous scam in this whole story.

So here are the doors almost no one names for her plainly. The first, and the one that guards everything, is that flat rule: hang up on the threat and call only the number on her real bill. The second is LIHEAP — the Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program — applied for through Illinois' Help Illinois Families at helpillinoisfamilies.com or 1-833-711-0374, and through her local agency in Cook County, CEDA; it is real money toward the heating and energy bill for a household exactly like hers, and it is free to apply for. The third is PIPP, the Percentage of Income Payment Plan, which can cap what she owes each month at a share of her income and chip away the past-due amount as she keeps up. The fourth is the utility's own hardship help — ComEd CARE, the Nicor Gas Sharing Program, Peoples Gas Share the Warmth — plus a deferred-payment arrangement to spread out what's behind, all asked for through the number on her bill. The fifth is the Citizens Utility Board — CUB, free at 1-800-669-5556 and at citizensutilityboard.org — a nonprofit watchdog that will help her lower her bill, get out of a bad third-party supplier, and understand her rights. The sixth is the Illinois Commerce Commission at icc.illinois.gov, which enforces winter disconnection protections and takes free complaints if a utility threatens an illegal shutoff. The seventh is free legal aid — CARPLS at 312-738-9200 and Legal Aid Chicago — if she's facing an unlawful cutoff or tangled in a supplier contract she never knowingly signed. The eighth is the wider circle of honest help: the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov and the Illinois Attorney General to report the imposter, the fake grant, and the slamming supplier, and a free 2-1-1 call to find local warming centers and emergency assistance, whether she's in Harvey, Dolton, Markham, Chicago Heights, Riverdale, Calumet City, South Holland, Blue Island, Robbins, or Ford Heights. Add to that what she already carries — hands that show up for the hardest work there is, a family worth every sleepless night, and a church that would rather help her keep the lights on than ever let her sit in the dark out of pride — and she is not the first woman in that congregation to think she had to face the winter alone, and then find there was help with her name already on it.

But hear this warning before she buys a single gift card, hands anyone her Social Security number or her bank login, pays a "reconnection fee" to a caller, or signs whatever a door-to-door "utility rep" puts in front of her — because the predators hunt a frightened caregiver in winter harder than almost anyone, and the fear of a cold house with a baby in it is the lever they pull.
