There is a woman at that church who becomes the first phone call every new family makes before they even unpack.

Nobody appointed her. She just speaks both languages, and she remembered what it was to arrive somewhere with a suitcase and no idea how any of it worked. So when a family lands, she is the one at the airport. She is the one who rides along to the DMV, who sits in the plastic chair at the school office and turns the enrollment packet into words a mother can answer, who explains that the light bill and the water bill are two different envelopes.

And then the hard part starts. Because getting here is not the finish line. It is the first day of a maze that is timed.

A letter comes from USCIS, in English, and it does not care that no one in the house can read it. It sets a date. There is a work permit that cannot be applied for until a certain day and stops being valid on another, and the weeks in between decide whether a father can legally take the job that is already waiting for him. There is a one-year asylum window that closes quietly and does not reopen. There is a court notice with an address an hour away and a consequence for missing it that most people do not learn until it is too late. And there is a man down the block who calls himself a notario, who will take the rent money and promise to handle all of it, and file nothing.

So she tries to hold all of it. For four families at once. She translates the letters at her kitchen table at night. She keeps the dates in her head and on scraps of paper. She drives to the appointments. And she is one woman, with her own job and her own children and a whole congregation of new arrivals who all found her number the same week.

So something slips. A work-permit renewal gets started too late and a father loses the job he was doing everything right to keep. A letter sits on a counter for three weeks because no one knew it was the one that mattered. A filing window closes. A family hands their savings to the notario because he spoke their language and she was busy that day, and by the time anyone reads the paperwork, the damage is done.

Here is what a family with money gets when they arrive.

They get an immigration attorney who reads every notice the day it comes. They get an accredited representative who knows exactly which form unlocks the next one and files it inside the window. They get a resettlement caseworker whose whole job is the benefits, the school, the housing, the clock. They get a team paid to walk a family through the maze so a paperwork mistake never costs them the life they crossed the world to build. That team is not more welcoming than she is. It is just resourced.

She can have that help too. For free. On the same phone the families call her on.

She can hold up a USCIS notice and get it explained in plain language, in the language the family reads, with the date and the next action made unmistakable. She can describe where a family is in the process and get back a clear order of operations — which form comes first, which document unlocks the next, which window is about to close. She can have every deadline held for every family at once, the renewal dates and the court dates and the one-year windows, so none of them slips past while she is at the airport meeting the next arrival. She can ask what a case might even qualify for and get a real map instead of a rumor from the man down the block.

Not to replace what she does. The welcome is hers. She is the one at the airport, the one in the plastic chair at the school, the one who remembered her own suitcase and decided no one else would arrive alone. No machine has ever met a family at the gate and told them they were home now. It just takes the maze off her shoulders so she can do the part only she can do, which is welcome.

That is the gap. It was never that the families who come to our churches deserve less of a fair start than the ones who can afford a lawyer. It is that the ones who can afford it got handed a whole team on day one, and ours got handed one woman with two languages and a heart that could not stand to watch somebody arrive alone.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities and the people navigating resources beside them in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Matteson. Dolton. Not a webinar. A room. Real problems. A real family's stack of letters on the table.

The people walking new arrivals into their whole new lives by hand are not behind. They were just never handed the tools the funded agencies take for granted.

We are here to hand them over.

The plug is in.
