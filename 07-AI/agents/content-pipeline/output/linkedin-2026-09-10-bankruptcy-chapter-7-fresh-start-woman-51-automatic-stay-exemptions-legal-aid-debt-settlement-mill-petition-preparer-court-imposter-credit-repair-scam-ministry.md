There is a woman in that congregation who fed your children. Maybe not yours by name, but somebody's — for twenty years she stood in a school lunchroom in a hairnet and an apron, scooping and wiping and calling the shy ones "baby," and she was proud of that work the way you can only be proud of work that serves. She paid her bills. She was raised that you pay every dollar you owe, on time, and if you can't, you cut back until you can, because an honest woman keeps her word. And then life did what it does to people who did everything right: a marriage came apart, and one income that had never quite been enough turned into two rents; and somewhere in there she spent a stretch in a hospital bed and came home to bills that still land in the mailbox with her name on them; and her hours got cut in a budget she had no vote in. So she did what a proud woman does — she juggled. She paid the minimums, then paid one card with another, then started letting the phone ring because she knew the voice on the other end. And now something has happened that she is too ashamed to say out loud in the fellowship hall: a chunk is gone from every single paycheck before she ever sees it, garnished straight off the top, or she opened her banking app one morning and the balance said frozen. And she has decided, in the quiet place where a proud woman keeps her worst fears, that this is simply her cross now, because filing bankruptcy would mean she failed, that it would be cheating the people she owes, that she would lose the little she has left. Here is what nobody has sat her down and told her plainly, and what somebody has to: this is not failure and it is not cheating, there is a law built for exactly this, and the help to use it is free or close to it.

You know this woman. She would skip her own lunch to make sure a child got fed and never mention it. She has spent her whole life being the one who gives, the one who makes a little stretch, the one who never asks — and somewhere along the way she absorbed the lie that a person who can't pay her debts is a person who did something wrong. And the truth is so much straighter than that shame will let her believe: bankruptcy is not a dishonest loophole and it is not a scam on her creditors. It is a fresh start that Congress wrote into federal law on purpose — the power to make uniform bankruptcy laws is set right into the Constitution, older than she is — precisely so that an honest, hardworking person who gets knocked flat by things she did not choose can get back up and not spend the rest of her life buried. Farmers use it. Factories and famous companies use it. Neighbors she'd never guess have used it and rebuilt. The debt she is drowning under has already been bought and sold to collection agencies for pennies on the dollar, and it has already been fed for years by the interest she poured into it. A garnishment eating her check is not a verdict that she is a bad woman — it is proof she needs the one legal tool built to stop it. And keeping her home, her car, her retirement, and her household things through what the law calls exemptions is not gaming anything; it is exactly what Illinois protects on purpose, so that a fresh start is a start and not a ruin.

And that has to be said plainly, and I will say it again before I'm done: needing a fresh start after a divorce, an illness, and cut hours is not a moral failing, and using the law built for that moment is not stealing from anyone. It is one of the most common things that happens to decent, hardworking people, and the fresh start is how the system was designed to work — not a back door, the front door. Choosing to face it the honest, careful way — sit down with a real lawyer, find out whether Chapter 7 or Chapter 13 fits, let the automatic stay stop the bleeding, and keep what the law says she gets to keep — instead of drowning in silence or handing money to a stranger who promises to "cut your debt in half," is not being irresponsible and it is not giving up. It is being a good steward of what's left, and of the household and the years still ahead of her. That is not a deficit. That is exactly the kind of steadiness that keeps a woman from being robbed twice — first by the debt, and then by the predators who feed on the shame around it — because the people who work this business hunt embarrassment and fear, and a proud woman who is mortified that it came to this is the easiest person in the room to talk into the wrong hands.

And there are doors. Real ones. Doors nobody names for a woman who has already decided, quietly, that this is just her burden to carry alone.

Start with the first thing she needs to hear, because it is the move that changes everything: she does not let shame, or an old belief that filing means she "failed," be the last word. She gets a free consultation with a real bankruptcy attorney — most give the first sit-down at no charge — and if money is tight, she goes straight to legal aid, which helps low-income people file for free or next to nothing. In that one conversation she finds out whether Chapter 7 (which can wipe out most credit-card debt, medical bills, and personal loans in a matter of months) or Chapter 13 (a court-protected repayment plan that can save a home or a car she's fallen behind on) is the road for her. And the moment a case is filed, the law does something almost nobody tells her about: the automatic stay stops the garnishment, unfreezes the road ahead, halts the repossession and the shutoff and the foreclosure clock, and makes the collection calls stop — by law, that day. That one decision — to talk to a real lawyer instead of suffering in silence — is what turns a check that's being eaten alive into a check she gets to keep, and it is the thing almost no one tells a proud, tired woman to do.

So here are the doors almost no one names for her plainly. The first, and the one that guards everything, is that flat truth: a free consult with a real bankruptcy attorney costs nothing, and legal aid will file for the low-income for free — so she never needs money in hand to find out what she's entitled to. In the South Suburbs and across Cook County that means Legal Aid Chicago, CARPLS at 312-738-9200, and Chicago Volunteer Legal Services; in the collar counties it means Prairie State Legal Services — real lawyers and trained advocates who do this every day. The second is the two roads themselves: Chapter 7, the true fresh start that discharges most unsecured debt in months, and Chapter 13, the plan that lets her catch up on a house or car over time under the court's protection — and only a real attorney can tell her which one fits her income and her goals. The third is exemptions: under Illinois law, most people who file Chapter 7 keep everything they own — the home, the car within limits, the retirement account, the everyday household goods — because the law was built to give her a floor, not to strip her bare. The fourth is the required credit-counseling course, a low-cost session she must take from a nonprofit agency on the U.S. Trustee's approved list before she files (and a short debtor-education course before her debts are wiped) — low-cost, and waivable if she truly can't afford it, but only real from an approved agency. The fifth is the money side of filing itself: the court filing fee can be waived entirely if her income is low enough, or paid in installments, so the fee is never the wall that stops her. The sixth is the circle of honest reporting for the predators who will circle her: the CFPB at consumerfinance.gov for debt-relief and credit-repair companies, the Illinois Attorney General at 1-800-386-5438 for debt-settlement scams, and the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov. And the seventh is a free 2-1-1 call for local legal, food, and financial help, whether she's in Harvey, Dolton, Markham, Chicago Heights, Riverdale, Calumet City, South Holland, Blue Island, Robbins, or Ford Heights. Add to that what she already carries — twenty years of honest work, a record of paying until she couldn't, and a church that would sooner sit beside her through a filing than let her believe she has to carry this alone — and she is not the first woman in that congregation to think bankruptcy was a scarlet letter, and then find it was a door marked fresh start.

But hear this warning before she stops paying her creditors because a stranger told her to, funnels her money into a "debt relief" company's escrow account, pays an upfront fee or buys a gift card to "stop your garnishment today," hands her Social Security number or her bank login to anyone who called her, lets a non-lawyer "preparer" fill out her forms, or pays somebody to "erase the bankruptcy" from her credit — because the predators hunt a proud, embarrassed, cornered woman harder than almost anyone, and the shame she feels is the exact lever they pull. So anyone doing those things is the scam, not the fresh start. Beware the debt-settlement mill — the most common trap of all — the slick company that tells her to stop paying her creditors and send the money to them instead, charging fat monthly fees to "cut your debt in half," wrecking her credit for years, and often leaving her sued anyway and deeper in the hole than a bankruptcy would have left her. Beware the non-attorney document mill or notario-style "preparer" who charges to type her forms while pretending to advise her, and who, getting her chapter or her exemptions wrong, can get her whole case thrown out and her property lost. Beware the upfront-fee caller promising to "stop your garnishment" or "erase your debt" for a fee, a gift card, or your banking — because no honest service makes a garnishment vanish for money paid to a stranger. Beware the imposter who calls claiming to be the court, a judge, or "the trustee on your case," inventing a "problem" or a "fee" and demanding a gift card or your Social Security number — the most devastating of all, because it wears the authority of the very system that's supposed to protect her. And beware the credit-repair shark who, after it's over, promises to "erase the bankruptcy" or "fix your score fast" for a fee, when the real rebuild is free, gradual, and hers to do. The rule is flat: no fee before a debt is settled, the court never demands a gift card, a preparer is not a lawyer, and no one can erase a true record for money.
