There is a man at that church who knows whose heat is about to go off.

Nobody gave him the job. He is just the one who fixes the furnace that quit on a Saturday, the one who notices a space heater glowing in a front room in January and does the math on it, the one a woman pulls aside after service and hands a folded red notice to because she trusts him and does not know who else to hand it to. He keeps a little cash from the benevolence fund and a little of his own, and when somebody's lights are two days from going out, he quietly makes a payment and does not talk about it.

And then the hard part starts. Because paying somebody's overdue bill this month is one thing. Getting that family what the state and the utility already set aside for households in exactly this situation is another, and the distance between the two is a maze nobody hands a tired man with a furnace filter in his truck a map to.

Because there is a program that pays a real part of the heating and cooling bill, and can cover the cost of getting service turned back on, and most families believe it is only for people with nothing at all, or that they already missed the window — when it runs on a calendar with priority times for seniors and disabled folks and families with little kids, and the money runs out, so the whole thing turns on knowing to apply and when. Because there is a plan that caps the energy bill at a share of what a household actually makes, and knocks a piece off the past-due balance every single month the family pays on time, so an impossible number becomes a set of stairs out — and nobody at the shutoff line ever says its name. Because there is a cold-weather protection that keeps the heat from being cut in the worst of winter, and a certificate a doctor can sign that stops a disconnection for a person whose life runs on that power — the oxygen machine, the insulin in the refrigerator, the CPAP — and it buys real time, and no one tells the family the note exists. Because there is a program that will come out and weatherize the house for free — seal it, insulate it, fix or replace the furnace — so the bill stops being crushing at the source instead of getting paid down one crisis at a time, and a renter can qualify too. Because the utility is required to offer a payment arrangement that spreads the debt out, and one phone call restructures it, and the family did not know they were allowed to ask. Because there are hardship grants from the gas and electric companies themselves that stack right on top of the first program. Because there is a free, independent watchdog that will read the bill line by line and catch the overcharge nobody noticed. And because the family kept the oven open and the burners on for heat instead — which is the one thing that can turn a cold month into a funeral.

So the man with the furnace filter tries to hold all of it. In his head. From the last family he helped, two winters ago, when the phone numbers were different. He pays the bill directly, which helps this month and teaches the family nothing about the door that would have helped every month after. He tells someone the assistance is probably closed, because that is what he heard, not knowing the window had just opened. He never says the payment-plan program's name, because no one ever said it to him. He is one faithful man, keeping other people's houses warm with a memory of a system that rewrites its own rules and deadlines faster than any one person can keep them.

So the money stays on the table. The family pays the reconnection deposit out of pocket because no one said the deposit could be waived. The assistance window closes unused. The house stays a sieve, so the same bill comes due, colder, next month. The payment plan that would have turned the arrears into stairs never gets set up. And a family already doing everything right on a check that runs out before the first heats one room with a space heater and the kitchen with an open oven, when the state and the utility had set money aside for that exact house all along.

Here is what a family with money gets on the coldest week of the year.

They get a bill they simply pay, and if it ever got complicated, an assistance caseworker or a utility advocate who knows every door — the program calendar, the payment-plan program, the medical hold, the free weatherization, the hardship grants — and walks them through the ones that fit. They get someone paid to know that the reconnection deposit can be waived, that a doctor's note stops the shutoff, that the watchdog will find the overcharge. That family is not more responsible than the man in the third pew with the furnace filter. They were just handed a navigator, and he was handed a folded red notice and told to make it disappear before Tuesday.

He can have that help too. For free. On the same phone somebody handed him the notice from.

He can find out when the assistance window is actually open and how to get a family in it before the money runs out, priority spots first. He can learn the payment-plan program caps the bill at a share of income and forgives the arrears a piece at a time, and how a family enrolls this week. He can learn that a doctor's certificate stops a disconnection for someone whose health depends on the power, and exactly who signs it. He can find out the house can be weatherized for free, renters included, so the bill stops being the emergency. He can learn the utility must offer a payment arrangement and that a reconnection deposit can be waived. He can find the hardship grants that stack on top. He can learn the free watchdog will audit the bill and catch what the family is being overcharged. He can get the whole maze — the assistance calendar, the payment-plan program, the medical hold, the weatherization, the payment arrangement, the stacked grants, the bill audit — broken into an order of operations he can run this week, and then walk it straight to the free local intake agency, 2-1-1, and the utility's own hardship line that will carry the family the rest of the way.

Not to replace what he does. The Saturday furnace call is his. The quiet payment nobody talks about is his. The woman who trusted him enough to hand him the notice instead of hiding it is his, and no machine will ever climb into a cold basement at nine at night and get the heat running again. It only takes the maze off his shoulders so he can do the part only he can do, which is to be the man a family trusts enough to show the red notice to in the first place, and to say you are not behind on being a good provider, this house is ours too, and nobody here is going to freeze.

That is the gap. It was never that the families in our pews deserve a colder house and a harder road to keeping the heat on than the family that can just pay the bill without reading it. It is that that family got a navigator, and ours got one faithful man with a furnace filter and a memory of last winter's phone numbers.

A word of care, plainly: no one should ever heat a home with a kitchen oven, a stove, or a grill — that is how carbon monoxide kills a family in their sleep, and it is the danger hiding under every unpaid heating bill. If there is ever the smell of gas, that is the utility's emergency line and everyone outside, right now. This is not a substitute for a caseworker. What AI does here is help the person already keeping the lights on know which doors exist and in what order, so a cold house gets to the people paid to keep it warm faster than a two-winter-old memory ever could.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities and the people navigating resources beside them in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Matteson. Dolton. Not a webinar. A room. Real problems. A real furnace fixed, a real assistance grant finally claimed, a real family that kept the heat on through January instead of a space heater and an open oven door.

The people keeping other families' lights on by hand are not behind. They were just never handed the tools the funded programs take for granted.

We are here to hand them over.

The plug is in.
