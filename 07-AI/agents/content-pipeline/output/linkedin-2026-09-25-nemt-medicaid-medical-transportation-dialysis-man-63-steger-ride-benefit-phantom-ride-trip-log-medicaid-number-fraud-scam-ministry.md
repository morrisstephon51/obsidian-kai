**Scam spine:** People on Medicaid who depend on rides to survive — dialysis patients, cancer patients, people with disabilities, frail elders getting to the specialist — are a **goldmine for fraud,** because the rides are frequent, the patients are desperate not to miss, and the money flows from Medicaid *behind the scenes* in a way the patient never sees. The crook's business isn't really *driving* you; it's **billing Medicaid,** and you are the number they bill under. **(1) The "pay to enroll in your free rides" advance-fee scam:** a man in the waiting room, a flyer, a call — *"Sign up with us to get your medical rides handled! One-time enrollment/membership fee of $[X]."* **NEMT is free — there is no fee to sign up for a benefit you already have.** The "fee" is the whole con. **(2) The blank-trip-log / "just sign here to save time" fraud — the dangerous one:** a driver hands you a **stack of blank or pre-filled trip tickets** and asks you to **sign them all at once,** or asks you to **sign for a ride you didn't take,** or to sign "extra" times. Every signature becomes a **phantom ride billed to Medicaid in your name** — trips that never happened, sometimes on days you were home or in the hospital. **Never sign a blank log, and never sign for a ride you did not actually take.** Sign only for the trip you actually rode, on the day you rode it, after the ride. **(3) The "give me your Medicaid number — here's $50" kickback:** a recruiter offers **you or a neighbor cash, gift cards, or "help"** in exchange for your **Medicaid ID number,** or to take **unnecessary rides,** or to let them bill in your name. Taking that money makes **you** part of the fraud — and even if you never see a dime, your number in a crook's hands means **phantom rides, drained benefits, and your name on an investigation.** Your Medicaid number is like a credit-card number: **you do not hand it to a stranger with a van.** **(4) The cash "gas money" / "copay" / tip skim:** a driver demands **cash** each way — *"gas contribution," "a little copay," "just a tip"* — for a ride that is **supposed to be free.** A covered NEMT ride costs the patient **nothing;** a driver squeezing you for cash is **stealing from a sick man.** **(5) The "this is your Medicaid transportation" imposter phishing:** a caller who "just needs to verify" your **Medicaid ID, date of birth, Social Security number, and bank account** to "set up your rides." The real arrangement runs through the **number on your card, your plan, or your clinic** — not a cold caller wanting your identity. That call is **identity theft.** **(6) The no-show / stranded patient:** the crookedest outfits **bill for rides they never provide** — they signed you up, billed Medicaid, and then **don't show,** leaving you stranded and missing a treatment while they collect. If a ride company is unreliable **and** you suspect it's still billing, that is fraud — report it and switch to the real process. **The real NEMT benefit is free, it's arranged through your card / plan / clinic social worker and not a man with cards in the waiting room, it never charges a fee or a cash copay, and you never sign a blank paper or give your Medicaid number to a stranger. The "enrollment fee," the book of blank tickets, the "give me your number for fifty bucks," and the cash-only driver are the tells.**

**Key facts:**
- **Rides to your medical appointments are a REAL Medicaid benefit — and they're free.** It's called **non-emergency medical transportation (NEMT).** If you're on Medicaid and have **no other way** to get to a **covered** appointment, Medicaid can arrange a ride at **no cost.** For standing treatments like **dialysis,** it can often be set up as a **recurring** ride so you never have to scramble.
- **You arrange it through your card, your plan, or your clinic — not a stranger.** Call the **number on your Medicaid card** or your **managed-care plan's Member Services,** or ask your **clinic/dialysis social worker** to help. **Book in advance** (often a couple of business days). That is the whole real process — no fee, no sign-up company required.
- **NEMT is FREE — any "enrollment fee" or "membership fee" is a scam.** You already have the benefit; nobody legitimately charges you to "sign you up" for it. And there is **no cash copay, tip, or "gas money"** for a covered ride — a driver demanding cash is stealing.
- **NEVER sign a blank trip log, and never sign for a ride you didn't take.** That is exactly how crooked companies **bill Medicaid for phantom rides in your name** — trips that never happened. Sign **only** for the actual ride, on the actual day, **after** it happens. If a driver pushes a stack of blank tickets at you, that is a red flag to report.
- **Guard your Medicaid number like a credit card.** Don't give it to a ride recruiter, a waiting-room salesman, or a cold caller. Anyone offering **cash for your Medicaid number** or for taking extra rides is pulling you into **fraud** — which can jeopardize **your** benefits and put **your** name on an investigation.
- **Phantom billing can come back on YOU — but help exists.** If you get a **letter questioning transportation claims,** or learn your Medicaid number was misused, **don't panic and don't ignore it** — call **free legal aid** to respond and clear your name, and report the company to **HFS OIG** and **HHS OIG (1-800-HHS-TIPS).** The fraud is the company's, not the patient's.
- **Never miss a treatment over a ride — tell your social worker first.** Missing dialysis (or chemo, or other essential care) is **dangerous.** If you can't get there, **do not just skip** — call your **clinic's social worker** and your **plan,** and get the real ride set up. That's what they're there for.

**The Man Who Drove Everybody: How a Church Nurse Who Buried a Husband Terrified by a Medicaid Fraud Letter Stopped a Dialysis Patient From Signing a Book of Blank Tickets for a Stranger — and Got Him the Free Rides That Had Been His All Along.**

Melvin Yancey had the man's card in his shirt pocket and a pen in his hand when Nurse Bernice caught him at the church door.

The card was cream-colored and said something like **MEDICAL TRANSPORT — WE HANDLE EVERYTHING** with a phone number and a little cartoon of a smiling van. Melvin had been carrying it for a week, turning it over in his pocket at red lights he no longer drove through, because the man who'd handed it to him in the dialysis waiting room had said the thing Melvin most needed to hear in all the world: *"You don't ever have to worry about a ride again. We do it all."*

And Melvin Yancey needed to not worry about a ride, because worrying about the ride was **killing him.**

Three days a week. Monday, Wednesday, Friday. Four hours in the chair while the machine cleaned the blood his kidneys couldn't, and then the long climb back up out of it — the dizziness, the cramps in his calves like somebody wringing a towel, the blood pressure so low the room went gray when he stood. You do not drive home from that. Melvin, who had driven a township plow through a whiteout at four in the morning for thirty-four years, could not safely drive the eleven miles home from his own dialysis chair, and the shame of that sat on him heavier than the fluid did.

His wife was three years gone. His girl was downstate with babies and a job. So Melvin had been buying rides off his neighbor, Terrell, twenty dollars there and twenty back on a good week — forty dollars, three times a week, out of a check that came once a month and did not stretch to the medicine as it was. And when Terrell picked up overtime at the warehouse, Melvin had no ride, and once — twice, if he was honest — he had **just not gone.** Told himself he'd catch up next time. Sat home Friday feeling the water rise in him, ankles like bread dough, and by Sunday couldn't get a full breath, and Monday morning his daughter's voice on the phone made him call the paramedics, and he'd spent a night in the emergency room while they pulled the fluid off him and a young doctor said the words *"Mr. Yancey, you understand you can die from missing these"* in a way that made it sound like Melvin was being careless, when the truth was Melvin was being **poor and proud and out of rides.**

So when a friendly man in the waiting room, sharp jacket, easy smile, leaned over and said *"You having trouble getting here, sir? We can fix that — we handle all your rides, Medicaid pays for it, you don't do a thing but sign up,"* Melvin Yancey had felt something in his chest unclench for the first time in months.

There was, the man said, just a small **enrollment fee** — "administrative, one time, keeps you in the system." And there was a little **book of tickets** to sign — "just formalities, save us both a headache down the road, sign 'em all now and you're set." And he'd need Melvin's **Medicaid number,** of course, "to set the billing up."

And Melvin had almost done all three, right there in the vinyl chair with the needles still in his arm. The only reason he hadn't was that his hands were shaking too bad from the treatment to write, and the man had said, "No worries, catch you Friday, bring the fee" — and Friday, Terrell had a shift, and Melvin missed, and then it was Sunday and he'd dragged himself to Galilee because his mother had raised him that you go, water or no water, and Nurse Bernice Threadgill had taken one look at him across the sanctuary and known.

"Melvin Yancey," she said now, both his hands in hers at the door. "Don't you tell me you're fine."

"I'm all right, Nurse Bernice."

"You are *swole up with fluid,* is what you are. I nursed that machine thirty years, baby. I can see it in your face. When did you last make a full treatment?"

Melvin looked at his shoes. "Wednesday."

"It's Sunday."

"I know what day it is."

"And what's this in your pocket." She'd seen the card's edge. She was a nurse; she saw everything. "Somebody at the clinic hand you a card? Telling you they'll handle your rides? Wanting you to pay a little fee and sign a little book?"

Melvin's head came up. "How'd you—"

Bernice Threadgill closed her eyes for a second, and when she opened them they were wet, and she said, "Because they did it to my Eugene. Come sit down with me, Melvin. Right now. Before you sign one thing. I have got to tell you about my husband, and a letter that came to him when he was dying — and then I am going to get you your rides the *real* way, and I promise you before God it will not cost you a dollar."

---

She sat him in the front pew and told it.

How Eugene, her husband, thirty-one years a mail carrier, had gone on dialysis at the very end, when her own shifts meant she couldn't drive him to every session. How a **medical-ride company** — a man with a card, just like Melvin's — had signed Eugene up. How the man had Eugene **sign a whole book of blank trip tickets** at once, "to save time." How the rides came sometimes and sometimes didn't, and Eugene, sick and tired and not wanting to trouble anybody, hadn't complained.

"And then, baby," Bernice said, "a letter came. From the state. Said there were transportation claims on Eugene's Medicaid they were looking into. *Investigating.* And my Eugene — the most honest man God ever made, a man who'd carry a nickel back to the post office if he found it in his bag — my Eugene read that letter and he *shook.* He thought he'd done something. He thought he was going to be arrested. In his *last months,* Melvin. He spent good weeks he didn't have to spare scared to death and ashamed, and he'd say to me, 'Bernie, what did I do, what did I sign' — and all he had ever done was **sign where that man told him to sign.**"

She had to stop a minute.

"That company had billed Medicaid for *dozens* of rides Eugene never took. Rides on days he was in the hospital. Rides on days I *know* he was home in the bed because I was sitting with him. They used my husband's number and my husband's signature on those blank tickets to steal, and when the state came looking, it came looking at *Eugene's* name first, because it was Eugene's number on the claims. I got a legal-aid lawyer — after he passed, Melvin, I'm ashamed to say I didn't catch it in time to spare him the fear — and she got it straightened out. His name was cleared. The fraud was *theirs.* But my husband died a little afraid he was a criminal, and he was the finest man I ever knew, and I will *never* — " her voice cracked — "I will never let that happen to another soul in this church. Not one. That's why I keep that table. That's why I pester everybody about their appointments. That's why I'm holding your hands right now instead of letting you go home and sign that book."

Melvin Yancey sat very still.

"I almost signed it," he said quietly. "The whole book. I couldn't write, my hands were shaking. That's the only reason."

"Then your shaking hands were the Lord," Bernice said. "Now listen to me, because here's the part that'll make you want to holler. Melvin — how long you been on dialysis?"

"Coming up three years."

"Three years. And in three years, has *anybody* — anybody at all — ever told you that **Medicaid will pay for your rides to that chair, for free, and always would have?**"

Melvin stared at her. "...What?"

"It's called *non-emergency medical transportation.* NEMT. It is a **real Medicaid benefit.** If you're on Medicaid and you can't get to a covered appointment, they arrange the ride. For dialysis they can set it up **standing** — same days every week, so you never scramble. And it does **not cost you a dime.** No enrollment fee. No membership. No twenty dollars to Terrell. No cash to a driver. *Free.* It's been yours this whole time, baby. Three years you been skipping treatments and paying money you don't have, and it was **yours the whole time,** and nobody put it in your hand."

Melvin Yancey — the man who drove everybody, the man who never asked for anything — put his face in his hands and his shoulders shook, and Nurse Bernice Threadgill put her arm around him in the front pew of Galilee Missionary Baptist and let him.

"I know," she said. "I know. Three years. Well. We're gonna fix it starting tomorrow morning. And that man with the card? We're gonna fix *him* too."

---

They did it over the next two weeks, and Melvin kept waiting for the catch, because a lifetime had taught him that free had teeth.

First, the real ride. Monday morning Bernice sat at Melvin's kitchen table with his **Medicaid card** and put it on speaker, and they called **the number right on the back of it.** She had him say the words plain: *"I'm on dialysis three days a week and I have no way to get there — I need help arranging my medical transportation."* The woman on the line did not ask for a fee. Did not ask him to sign a book. She took his treatment schedule — Monday, Wednesday, Friday, the clinic's address, his pickup time — and she set up a **standing ride,** recurring, the same every week, so Melvin would never again sit home watching his ankles swell wondering if a ride was coming. Then Bernice, who knew the building, called the **dialysis unit's social worker** — a real person, in the clinic, whose whole *job* included this — and looped her in, so the ride and the treatment were talking to each other. "You should've been told this your first week, Mr. Yancey," the social worker said, and she sounded like she meant it. "I'm sorry you weren't. You're set now."

No fee. No book. No cash. A ride, three days a week, free, arranged in an afternoon at a kitchen table.

Then, the man with the cards. Bernice would not let it go — not after Eugene. They wrote down everything: the company name on the card, the phone number, the "enrollment fee" the man had asked for, the book of blank tickets he'd wanted Melvin to sign, the fact that he'd asked for Melvin's Medicaid number in a *waiting room.* And they reported it — to the **Illinois HFS Office of Inspector General's Medicaid fraud line,** to the **federal HHS OIG at 1-800-HHS-TIPS,** to the **Illinois Attorney General's consumer fraud line,** and to the **FTC.** "Your one report," Bernice told him, echoing something she'd read once, "is the next dialysis patient's warning. If I'd had somebody make Eugene's report *before,* maybe that man never gets to the next fella. So we make it. Loud."

And then — because the crooks are thorough — the letter. Sure enough, a few weeks on, an envelope came to Melvin's house from the state, questioning some **transportation claims** billed under his Medicaid number. Melvin's stomach dropped; he saw Eugene in it. But this time there was a nurse in the pew who'd walked this exact road, and she did not let him sit alone with it for one hour. She drove him straight to **Prairie State Legal Services,** and a lawyer looked at the dates and saw immediately what Bernice had known she'd see: the company had **billed for rides Melvin never took** — including two on a day Melvin had been in the *emergency room,* which the hospital records proved. The lawyer helped Melvin respond, documented that Melvin was a **victim** of the billing, not a party to it, and got his name cleared and the claims flagged as fraudulent. "You did exactly right, Mr. Yancey," she said. "You never signed their book, you reported them, and you came in with this letter instead of hiding it. That's the whole ballgame."

"My friend told me to," Melvin said. "The nurse. She lost her husband to not knowing."

The lawyer looked at Bernice. Bernice looked at the window.
