There is a man in that church who has never once been late.

You know him. Everybody knows him. He is there before the doors open to set out the chairs, and he is there after to stack them, and in between he stands in the back with his arms folded because his knees do not love the pews anymore. He works in one of those enormous warehouses out by the interstate, the ones with the trucks lined up all night, and he has worked in them for going on twenty years. He is fast. He is precise. He can read a pick list and a pallet and a forklift and a shipping label the way some people read music, and he has trained half the young men who came in after him and then watched a few of them get made supervisor over him because they had a piece of paper he did not. His body is starting to send him letters now. The shoulder. The lower back. The thing in his hip he does not tell his wife about. He is forty-five years old, and for the first time it has occurred to him, quietly, in the dark, that this job only pays him while he can still lift, and that the day his body quits is the day the money quits, and that day is closer than it used to be.

Understand him clearly. He is not unskilled. He is one of the most skilled, most disciplined, most dependable human beings in that entire building. He solves problems all day long that would break most people by lunch. He is not lazy and he is not slow and he did not waste his life. He was simply told, once, a long time ago, by a tired counselor in a crowded school, that college was for the other kids, the ones with the grades and the money and the parents who knew how it worked, and that a boy like him should just be grateful to find steady work. And he believed it. Why would he not believe it. So at seventeen he clocked in, and he has been clocking in ever since, and somewhere along the way the door to a classroom closed in his mind so softly he never even heard it click.

And his family has decided, quietly and a long time ago, that this is just how it is. That "going back to school" means debt they cannot carry and years they do not have and sitting in a room full of teenagers feeling like a fool. That training and credentials and career changes are for people with a cushion, people who can afford to stop working for two years, people with somebody in the family who already did it and can show them the way. They are not those people. So they never ask. They just watch the shoulder get worse and pray the body holds.

Here is the thing nobody has ever said to him plainly: the door did not close. Nobody ever showed him it was still open, and painted just enough to look like a wall.

Because the government already set money aside, years ago, to train a working adult exactly like him for a whole new trade, and it has a name almost no one in that congregation has ever heard — a federal law, a real program, real dollars, sitting in a building he drives past every week — and no one ever handed him the address. Because there are free centers in his own county whose entire job is to sit down with a grown man who wants a better future and help him retrain for one, and they cost nothing, and he has never walked through the door because no one ever told him they were his. Because a registered apprenticeship does not ask him to stop earning while he learns — it PAYS him to learn, a real check while a real journeyman teaches him a real trade, so that "I can't afford to go back" turns out to be answered by the very thing he was afraid to reach for. Because the community college fifteen minutes from his house does not only sell four-year degrees to teenagers — it runs short programs, measured in weeks and months, not years, that turn a hard worker into a licensed electrician, an HVAC tech, a commercial driver, a welder, a phlebotomist, an IT support tech, work that pays double what the warehouse pays and does not depend on his back holding out. Because federal aid can help pay for training, and the rules on shorter workforce programs are changing right now, so the thing that was not possible two years ago may be possible this year — but only if somebody checks what is actually current instead of assuming the answer is still no. Because Illinois runs a whole website, a whole front door, built for a person standing exactly where he is standing, and he has never seen it. Because 211, three digits on the phone already in his pocket, will connect him to local help he never knew existed. And because — this is the part underneath all of it — every one of these doors is free, or better than free, and the "training program" that calls him and texts him and tacks a flyer to the break-room wall promising a guaranteed government job or a real certification in a single weekend if he just pays a fee up front is a scam, every time, and the man working the hardest to build a better life for his family is precisely the man the scammer has circled on his list.

So he keeps clocking in. Fast. Precise. Early. Carrying a body that is quietly filing its complaints, in a job that only pays while he can still lift, training the young men who will be promoted over him, certain that the classroom closed on him at seventeen. He is not stuck because he is less. He is one of the ablest men in that building. He is stuck because getting skilled turned out to have a map, and a set of free doors, and a person who knew the way — and his family was never handed a single one of them.

Here is the thing about the family across town, the one with resources. When their son wanted to change careers at forty-five, nobody in that house treated it like a crisis. Somebody already knew which credential paid and which was a waste. Somebody had done an apprenticeship, or knew a guy who ran one, or could just make a few calls. There was a counselor, a cousin, a father-in-law, a whole quiet network of people who knew how retraining worked and walked him straight to it. Getting skilled was never a locked door for that family. It was a hallway they already knew the length of. Their son is not smarter than the man stacking chairs after service. He is not harder-working. Somebody simply handed him the map, and nobody ever handed one to ours.

He can have that too. Starting this month. With a free center in his own county and a phone call he did not know he was allowed to make.

He can walk into the American Job Center that was always his and sit down with a person whose whole job is to help him retrain. He can find out whether the federal training money fits him, instead of assuming it never could. He can look at an apprenticeship that pays him while he learns a trade, so he never has to choose between feeding his family this month and building a future for the next twenty years. He can find the short program at the community college down the road, the one that turns him into a licensed tradesman in months, not decades. He can check — really check, with real current information — whether federal aid will help pay for it. He can have the whole tangle broken into a plain order of operations he can start this week, and then be walked straight to the real programs that carry him the rest of the way.

And once he is moving, he has the thing the family across town never even had to name: a patient, tireless someone who knows how all of this works. The one that helps him see which free programs he might qualify for. The one that finds the nearest center and the shortest honest path. The one that helps him make sense of an apprenticeship next to a certificate, reads the intake paperwork beside him so he does not give up at the first confusing form, and never once makes a grown man feel slow or foolish for walking back into a classroom at forty-five. A resourced family just always seemed to have that person. Ours never did. Now it can.

Not to take the class for him. Let that be plain. Not to do the trade, not to pass the license test, not to weld the seam or wire the panel or drive the rig. The skill is his. The hands are his. The twenty years of discipline that had him early every single morning, the work ethic that trained half the floor, the mind that reads a warehouse like sheet music — none of that gets replaced by a machine. It only finds him the door. It only clears the paperwork and the confusion and the quiet shame out of the way, and gets him to the exact same starting line the man across town started from.

The Plug AI says three things, in this order. Get Connected. Get Skilled. Get into AI. Last time we talked about the first word. This is the second. Because a man who never gets skilled stays exactly where a hard body and a good heart can carry him and no further, and for far too many of our people the road to a real skill has been painted, carefully and cruelly, to look like a wall that closed on them at seventeen.

That is the gap. It was never that the man in our pews is less capable than the man across town. It is that one of them was handed a counselor, a map, and a family who knew which door led where, and the other one was handed a strong back, a time clock, and a soft lie he has believed for twenty-eight years.

A word of care, plainly. Every one of these programs sets its own rules, and I do not set them and neither does anyone in that church — so this is general navigation, not a promise that anyone qualifies, not an endorsement of one school or one trade or one program over another, and not legal or financial advice. Real workforce training through these public programs does not charge you a fee, and a registered apprenticeship pays YOU — so anyone demanding money up front for "free government training," or a guaranteed government job, or a real certification in a weekend, is running a scam, and the harder a family is trying to climb, the more of these will come hunting them. The rules on federal aid for short workforce programs are changing, so do not assume the answer is still no and do not assume it is automatically yes — check what is actually current. Helping someone learn that WIOA exists, or that apprenticeships pay, or that the community college runs a short program, is not the same as enrolling them or promising they qualify. So route the real eligibility and the real sign-up to the real programs: your local American Job Center, found through CareerOneStop at careeronestop.org or by calling 1-877-US-2JOBS; registered apprenticeships at apprenticeship.gov; your local community college for its short workforce credentials — Prairie State College in Chicago Heights and South Suburban College in South Holland are two that serve our area; Illinois workNet at illinoisworknet.com; FAFSA and the school's own financial-aid office for Pell and other aid; and 211 or 211.org to find local help.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities and to the working people getting skilled right beside them in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Matteson. Dolton. Chicago Heights. South Holland. Not a webinar. A room. Real problems. A real man who stacked chairs after service with a shoulder that was quitting on him, a real free center he did not know was his, a real apprenticeship that paid him to learn, a real license with his name on it that pays whether or not his back holds out.

The hardest workers in our churches are not unskilled. They were just never handed the map the skilled never even have to think about.

We are here to hand it over.

The plug is in.
