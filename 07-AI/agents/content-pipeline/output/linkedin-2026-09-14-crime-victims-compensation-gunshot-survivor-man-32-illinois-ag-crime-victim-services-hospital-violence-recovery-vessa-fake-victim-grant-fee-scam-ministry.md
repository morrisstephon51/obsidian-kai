There is a man in that church you would never look at twice and think *survivor*. He is thirty-two. He is back in the pew most Sundays now, back on his feet, shaking hands in the vestibule — and three months ago, on a Tuesday near closing, he was lying on the floor of the food mart where he works nights, holding his own side, listening to two men run out the door with a hundred and forty dollars from the drawer and the sound of the shot still in his ears.

You know him. He did everything right. He didn't reach for anything, didn't argue, put the drawer up on the counter with both hands the way you're supposed to. And one of them shot him anyway, on the way out, for nothing — and then they were gone into the dark on Halsted and he was on the tile praying he'd see his kids again. He lived. That's the miracle nobody talks about, because the news moved on by the next morning and the police never caught anybody. He came home with a scar that pulls when he reaches, a hand that doesn't grip like it used to, a stack of hospital and ambulance bills taller than any paycheck he's ever earned, and something else he can't say out loud — the way he jumps now when the door chimes, the nights he doesn't sleep, the way his wife watches him go quiet and doesn't know what to do with it.

And here is the part that would let him breathe if somebody said it plainly: the state of Illinois has a fund built for exactly this. It has his name on it, in a manner of speaking — his and every other person hurt by a violent crime — and he has never heard of it. He figures because they never caught the shooter, nothing can be done and nobody owes him a thing. He's wrong about that, and being wrong about it is costing him money he doesn't have and help he's already earned. Nobody handed him that door. The people who found him first were a letter and a phone call that smelled like help and were built to rob a wounded man.

Because a hurting man with real bills and real fear, sitting in silence because he's "supposed to be able to take it," is exactly who these hustles are built to find. So when a letter comes on official-looking paper — *You may be entitled to a Crime Victim Compensation payout of up to $9,400. To release your funds, remit the $250 processing fee* — it does not read like a scam. It reads like the first real answer anybody's given him, and two hundred and fifty dollars to unlock nine thousand feels like the smartest thing he could do all month.

So there it is — the squeeze. A young father who took a bullet doing an honest night's job, out of work, in pain, up at 3 a.m. with the ceiling and the memory, thousands in the hole, and a voice or a letter saying *pay a little and we'll hand you a lot.* That is the exact moment they get him: the "processing fee" that unlocks nothing, the "recovery lawyer" who wants money upfront to chase a settlement, the fundraiser page wearing his own story to skim the donations his church took up, the shame that keeps his mouth shut so no honest person can catch the con before he does.

Say it plainly, and I'll say it again before I'm done: the danger is not that he's foolish, or weak, or milking anything. The danger is that he has a real, honest need — medical bills a robbery left him holding, weeks of wages the recovery ate, a mind that needs care after what it saw — and a set of real, honest doors nobody ever showed him. So he's left to the first person who writes, and that person's whole job is to get a fee or a Social Security number before he can ask one question. He doesn't know there's a state program that pays crime victims back. He doesn't know it doesn't need the shooter to be caught. He doesn't know it's free and needs no lawyer. None of that is a failing in him. It's a stack of pages nobody put in his hands.

And there are doors. Real ones — that reach a survivor and his family, and that almost nobody names for a thirty-two-year-old who lived through a gunshot and is too proud and too tired to ask.

Start with the first thing he needs to hear, before a single door: being shot doing an honest night's work is not weakness, and it is not his fault. Surviving it — and still dragging himself up for his kids the next month — is more strength than most people will ever be asked to find, not less. The silence he's keeping isn't toughness; it's what trauma tells a proud man to do, and it's the very thing that leaves him alone in the one spot the hustlers are waiting. And asking for the compensation the state set aside for crime victims is not begging and it is not milking it. It's a right, written into Illinois law for exactly this, funded in large part by the fines and fees that offenders themselves pay — the system working the way it was built to. The shame in this story belongs to the man who pulled the trigger, and to every predator who circles a wounded family — never, ever to him.

Before he pays a cent or gives anyone a number: there is the **Illinois Crime Victims Compensation Program**, run through the **Illinois Attorney General's** Crime Victim Services and decided by the **Illinois Court of Claims** — it can reimburse his medical and hospital bills, his counseling, and his lost wages, up to a state cap; it is free to apply for; he does not need a lawyer; and — hear this — it does *not* require the shooter to be caught or convicted.
