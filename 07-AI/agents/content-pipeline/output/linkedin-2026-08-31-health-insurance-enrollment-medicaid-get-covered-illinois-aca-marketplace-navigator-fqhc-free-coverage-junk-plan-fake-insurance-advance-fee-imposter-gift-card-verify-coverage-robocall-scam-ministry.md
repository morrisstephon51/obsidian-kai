There is a woman in that congregation who has not seen a doctor in three years, not because she is careless but because she is certain she cannot afford to. She is fifty-two. She stitches together part-time work — a few days here, a shift there — and none of it comes with insurance, so she has learned to live around her own body: to skip the checkup, to stretch a prescription that was meant for thirty days into forty-five, to press her hand to a pain and tell herself it is nothing because to find out would cost money she does not have. And what nobody ever sat her down and told her is the thing that would change everything: that free and low-cost health coverage is real and built for exactly this, that a working woman whose jobs do not offer insurance can get covered, and that finding out and enrolling costs nothing at all.

You know her. She is not asking anyone to carry her and she would be embarrassed you even noticed the rationed pills — she is a proud woman who has worked her whole life and never once thought of coverage as something meant for her. Nobody told her that Medicaid exists for exactly her income and can cover her at little or no cost. Nobody told her that Get Covered Illinois — the state's own official Marketplace — offers plans where premium tax credits can bring the monthly cost down to a few dollars, or nothing, for someone earning what she earns. Nobody told her that a certified Navigator will sit with her and do the whole thing for free, or that the community health center up the road will see her on a sliding scale today, before any coverage even starts. And nobody told her the one thing that would protect her the moment she finally goes looking: that enrolling is free, that the state and the Marketplace never text or call to demand a fee or gift cards to "keep" her coverage, and that no one who is real ever charges her to sign up — so the first voice that asks her to pay to get covered is not a helper, it is a thief who found a woman tired and proud enough not to ask twice.
