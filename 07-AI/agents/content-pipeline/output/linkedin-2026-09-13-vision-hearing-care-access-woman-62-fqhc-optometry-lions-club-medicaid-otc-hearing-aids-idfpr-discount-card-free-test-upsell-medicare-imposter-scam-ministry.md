There is a woman in that church who has started holding the bulletin at arm's length to read it, and last week she almost gave her Medicare number to a man on the phone who promised her "free hearing aids, approved, just confirm a few things." She is sixty-two. She is not on Medicare yet. And the only reason she didn't hand it over is that the call dropped before she found her card.

You know her. She cleans offices downtown, or she works the school cafeteria line, or she does home care for somebody else's mother, or she still takes in sewing at the kitchen table. She is sixty-two — too young for Medicare, too "much" income for some help and too little for a $600 pair of glasses — in those gap years where a body starts asking for maintenance right when the insurance disappeared. And two of her senses have been quietly slipping for a while now. The words on her prescription bottle went soft, so she holds them out at arm's length and guesses. Street signs come late. She failed the eye chart at the license branch and had to go back. And her hearing — she stopped catching what the grandkids say from the back seat, so she just smiles and nods, and she's started sitting out of the loud end of the fellowship hall because it's easier than asking "what?" for the fourth time. She isn't complaining. She thinks this is just what getting older costs, and that the costing is her problem to carry alone.

And here is the part that knocks the wind out of her when somebody finally says it plainly: the reason her eyes and ears feel like a luxury she can't buy is that the whole system is built to leave them out. Dental, vision, hearing — those three. Even Medicare, the thing she's counting the years down to, does not cover routine eyeglasses or hearing aids at all. So when she prices a real exam and glasses at four hundred dollars she doesn't have, and a real pair of hearing aids at the cost of a used car — three, five, six thousand dollars — she does the only math a careful woman can do. She rations. She squints. She nods. She pulls back. Nobody ever handed her the page that says there are free and low-cost doors for exactly this.

So when the phone rings and a smooth voice says "Medicare has approved you for free hearing aids, ma'am, we just need to verify your number," it doesn't sound like a trap. It sounds like the first person in years who noticed she can't hear.

So there it is — the squeeze. And that is the exact moment they find her: the "free hearing test" that turns into a five-thousand-dollar sale, the "vision and dental discount card" for nineteen dollars a month, the caller who wants her Medicare number to "release" hearing aids that Medicare does not even cover.

Because a woman who can't afford to see or hear well, who thinks it's her own private failing, who is embarrassed to keep saying "what?" — that is a woman under a squeeze, and a squeeze is what these people sell to. That is the part that has to be said plainly, and I will say it again before I'm done: the danger is not that she is vain, or "going downhill," or too proud to admit she needs help. The danger is that she has been made to feel that seeing and hearing are extras she hasn't earned — so she will hand her Medicare number, or her last card, to the first voice that treats her eyes and ears like they matter. She doesn't know Medicaid might cover the whole exam and the glasses and the hearing aids for free. She doesn't know the optometry school does exams for a fraction of the price. She doesn't know the Lions Club has been putting glasses on people like her for a hundred years, or that hearing aids you can actually afford now sit on a pharmacy shelf. None of that is a failing in her. It is a stack of pages nobody ever put in her hands.

And there are doors. Real ones — free and low-cost ones — that give a hardworking woman her eyes and her ears back, and that nobody names for a sixty-two-year-old holding the bulletin out at arm's length.

Start with the first thing she needs to hear, before a single door: needing glasses or hearing aids is not vanity, and it is not "giving up." It is a body doing exactly what bodies do at sixty-two, and there is no more shame in it than in needing a coat in January. Not having four hundred dollars for an exam and glasses, or five thousand for hearing aids, on what a cleaner or a cafeteria worker or a home aide earns is arithmetic, not failure — the money simply isn't there, and pretending it is would be the foolish thing. Holding the pill bottle at arm's length, squinting at the road, nodding along because the fellowship hall is too loud — that is not her decline. That is a careful woman rationing her own senses because no one told her the free doors were hers to open. And asking a stranger, or a machine, "what does OTC even mean, is this discount card real, will Medicaid cover this" is not her being slow. It is her protecting her own eyes and her own safety. She is not a woman letting herself go. She is a woman quietly doing without, being told in a dozen small ways that seeing and hearing well are for other people.

So here are the doors almost no one names for her plainly, none ranked, all worth a look. First and biggest, because it surprises people: Illinois Medicaid, for adults, can cover a routine eye exam and eyeglasses — and hearing aids — and now that she is low-income in the gap years, she may finally qualify, so that is the very first thing to check. A federally qualified health center — an FQHC, a community health center — does eye exams on a sliding scale, some have an optical shop right there, and they can order the diabetic retinal exam that protects the sight of anyone with diabetes, which in our community is so many of us. The Illinois Eye Institute and the college of optometry, and audiology school clinics, do low-cost, carefully supervised exams, glasses, and hearing tests. The Lions Clubs — the folks who collect old glasses in the box at the library — provide free or low-cost eyeglasses and help with vision and hearing. EyeCare America, run by the eye surgeons' own academy, arranges free eye exams for eligible seniors and free glaucoma screening. New Eyes gives vouchers for a new pair of prescription glasses. For her ears, there are now over-the-counter hearing aids — a real, FDA-authorized category since 2022, for mild-to-moderate loss, sold at the pharmacy for hundreds instead of thousands — a legitimate way in for a woman who was quoted five grand. Illinois has a program, ITAC, that will send her a free amplified telephone. Charitable hearing programs — Sertoma and Hearing Charities of America, and the manufacturers' own foundations — help income-qualified adults with real aids. AgeOptions, our suburban Area Agency on Aging, and 2-1-1 can point her to all of it. And before she pays a dollar or lets anyone fit her, she can check — free, in two minutes, at IDFPR's website — whether the "eye doctor" or the "hearing specialist" is even licensed. That is a whole row of doors — Medicaid, an FQHC, the school clinic, the Lions Club, EyeCare America, New Eyes, over-the-counter aids — and she is at the kitchen table with her hand near her Medicare card for a caller's "free" offer, because nobody ever told her a single one of the real ones was hers.

But hear the warning before she buys anything or gives anyone a number, because the thieves hunt a woman who can't see or hear well and is ashamed of it. Hear it plainly, and I will say it again at the end. There is the "vision and hearing and dental discount card" — nineteen, thirty dollars a month for a "plan" that is not insurance at all, sold as if it were. There is the "free hearing test," advertised at the strip mall or the senior lunch, that turns into a two-hour high-pressure sale of five- and six-thousand-dollar hearing aids on financing she can't repay and can't return. There is the call — the one that almost got her — that says "Medicare has approved you for free hearing aids, just confirm your number," when Medicare does not cover routine hearing aids, so that call exists only to sell her name or to steal her Medicare number for medical identity theft and equipment fraud, the very same racket as the "free back brace" and "free genetic test" calls. There is the too-good online eyeglass site that takes her card and ships nothing, or ships the wrong prescription. There is the "vision grant" or "hearing grant" that says pay a small fee to unlock your free glasses or aids — when no real charity ever charges you to receive its help. There is the lease-to-own hearing-aid trap. There are the cheap "amplifiers" sold as if they were medical hearing aids. And there is the phishing text: "confirm your Medicare or insurance to claim your free glasses." The rule is flat: Medicaid may cover her exam, her glasses, and her hearing aids for free, so she checks that first; FQHCs, school clinics, Lions Clubs, EyeCare America, and over-the-counter aids are real and cheap or free; a "discount card" is not insurance and she should not pay a monthly fee for one; Medicare does not cover routine glasses or hearing aids, so "Medicare-approved free hearing aids, just give me your number" is always a scam; no real charity charges an upfront fee to release free glasses or aids; she never gives a Medicare number, a Social Security number, or a card to a caller, a free screening, or a pop-up; and she verifies any eye doctor or hearing dispenser free at IDFPR before she pays or lets anyone fit her.

So she almost gave him the number. Not because she is gullible — gullible is not the word for a woman who has counted every dollar for forty years of honest work. Not because she doesn't care about being safe — she taught her whole family to be careful. She almost gave it because she has spent two years quietly unable to hear her own grandchildren, and he was the first voice on the line who acted like that was a problem worth solving.

She was not smarter than the woman in our pew. She did not deserve to see and hear more. She had one person who knew the doors were there and named them before the squeeze could do its work. One woman is reading her own prescription bottles again and hearing her grandbabies from the back seat. The woman in our pew can be met before she gives out that number. Not with a lecture about how she should have gone to the doctor sooner, or seen the scam coming.
