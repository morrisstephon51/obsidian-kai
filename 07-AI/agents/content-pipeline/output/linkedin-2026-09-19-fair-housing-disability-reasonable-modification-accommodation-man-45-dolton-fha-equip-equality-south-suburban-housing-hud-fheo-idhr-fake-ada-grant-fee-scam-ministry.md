**Scam spine:** Facebook-advertised and Google-targeted "ADA Housing Assistance Programs," "Disability Home Modification Grant Services," and "Fair Housing Filing Services" charge renters $300–$800 upfront for two fictional services: (1) applying for "federal disability housing modification grants from HUD" — no such individual-renter grant program exists through HUD for private residential units; (2) "professionally filing your fair housing complaint on your behalf" for a fee, when HUD and IDHR complaints are free, self-filing takes under 20 minutes online, and intake staff assist callers at no cost. A third variant: "ADA compliance audits" for $200–$500 that produce an inspection report certifying whether the apartment is "ADA-compliant" — renters do not need an inspection report to request a reasonable modification; the right is statutory, self-executing, and belongs to the tenant from the moment they have a disability and a need. These operations systematically exploit the real confusion between the Americans with Disabilities Act (which applies to commercial buildings and public accommodations, not private residential housing) and the Fair Housing Act (which applies to virtually all private housing and prohibits disability discrimination including refusal of reasonable modifications and reasonable accommodations).

**Key legal facts:**
- **Fair Housing Act (42 U.S.C. § 3604(f)):** It is unlawful discrimination for a landlord to refuse to allow a person with a disability to make, at the tenant's own expense, "reasonable modifications" to the dwelling or common areas that are necessary for the person's full enjoyment of the premises — as long as the tenant agrees (if reasonably requested) to restore the unit to its original condition upon move-out
- **Reasonable modifications (structural changes):** grab bars, shower seats, ramps, widened doorways, lever door hardware, lowered counters — the tenant pays; the landlord must allow
- **Reasonable accommodations (non-structural policy/rule changes):** designated accessible parking spaces, service or emotional support animal permission notwithstanding a no-pets policy, first-floor unit transfer if available, adjusted rent-payment schedule — the tenant requests; the landlord must grant unless it creates an undue financial or administrative hardship (a high bar, rarely met)
- **Illinois Human Rights Act (775 ILCS 5/3-102):** parallel and sometimes broader protections; covers buildings with fewer than 5 units that federal law may not reach; administered by IDHR
- **Steering:** a landlord's statement that a disabled tenant should "find somewhere more suitable" or "find a handicap unit" is textbook discriminatory steering under the FHA; it does not need to accompany a refusal to rent — the statement itself is a violation
- **Complaints must be filed within 1 year of the discriminatory act;** file promptly; each new refusal restarts the clock
- **Remedies:** actual damages (including emotional distress), injunctive relief (the landlord must allow the modification), civil penalties of up to $21,663 for a first violation, and attorneys' fees — complainants represented by fair housing organizations pay nothing
- **Fair housing testing:** organizations like SSHC deploy "testers" — people without disabilities asking the same requests — to document that the landlord approved modifications for non-disabled tenants; this is standard evidence-gathering and has been upheld by courts
- Building must have 4+ units for full federal FHA applicability to disability; Illinois law often covers smaller buildings

Darnell had been in that apartment for four years before the accident.

He knows the neighbors. He knows the landlord's name. He knows the building's rhythms — which pipes knock in winter, which parking spots the overnight workers take, which corner of the lot floods. Three years in, he asked for three things.

The grab bar conversation was first.

He found one on clearance at Home Depot — stainless, rated for 250 pounds, $47. A friend from his old driving route who does handyman work would install it for a bag of groceries and an afternoon. Total cost: maybe $180. He'd restore the anchor holes with silicone patch when he moved out someday. He'd even put that in writing.

His landlord said no.

"The lease says no modifications to the unit without written permission."

Darnell asked for the written permission.

"We don't grant that."

The ramp request went the same way. Darnell wasn't asking the landlord to build anything. He'd arranged for three men from New Calvary to come on a Saturday morning with donated lumber and rubber edging. A single step, about four inches. The ramp would cover it and could be removed entirely when he left. The landlord wouldn't have to pay for a thing.

"No modifications to common areas. That's policy."

The parking conversation ended the way Darnell still thinks about, alone, late at night.

He'd explained it plainly: the 90-foot walk across a cracked lot was fine in good weather, but in rain, in ice, in any conditions that made the surface uncertain, he was at real risk of falling. A closer space — any space — would reduce that risk. He wasn't asking for a new space to be created. He was asking to be assigned one of the existing spaces near the entrance that nobody seemed to use regularly.

His landlord looked at him for a moment.

"Look, this building's not really set up for that kind of situation. If the unit doesn't work for you anymore, maybe you should find somewhere more suitable."

Darnell didn't say anything. He walked back to his apartment the long way.

Here is what Darnell didn't know, and what too many renters with disabilities in south suburban Cook County don't know.

**1. The Fair Housing Act requires your landlord to let you make reasonable modifications to your unit. You pay. They cannot say no.**

Under the federal Fair Housing Act, a person with a disability has the right to make "reasonable modifications" to their dwelling or to common areas — at their own expense — when those modifications are necessary for them to have the same enjoyment of the housing as a person without a disability.

A grab bar in a shower stall is the textbook example the Department of Justice uses when explaining the law. It is not a gray area.

The landlord can ask you, in writing, to agree to restore the unit to its original condition when you move out. That's it. They cannot charge you upfront. They cannot deny permission outright. "Our policy prohibits modifications" is not a valid reason under federal law — in fact, applying such a policy to a disabled tenant is the discriminatory act.

If you're a renter with a disability in Illinois and your landlord is telling you no, call the South Suburban Housing Center at **(708) 957-4674**. The call is free. The legal help is free.

**2. The Fair Housing Act requires your landlord to provide reasonable accommodations — changes to rules, policies, or practices. A parking space is the textbook example.**

A "reasonable accommodation" under the Fair Housing Act is different from a modification. It's not a physical change to the unit — it's a change to how the landlord operates.

Assigning an accessible parking space to a tenant with a mobility impairment is the most common reasonable accommodation request in residential housing. Courts have consistently upheld it. The landlord's obligation is to make the accommodation unless doing so creates "undue hardship" — a very high bar that essentially requires proving the accommodation is financially or administratively impossible. "We don't do it that way" does not qualify.

If Darnell's landlord owns 8 parking spaces and Darnell needs one near the entrance, the landlord must assign it. Full stop.

**3. What your landlord said to Darnell in that parking lot was an illegal act called steering.**

The Fair Housing Act prohibits discrimination not only through explicit refusals but through statements that discourage a disabled person from remaining in housing or suggest they should find housing elsewhere. "This building isn't set up for your situation" and "maybe you should find somewhere more suitable" are the kind of statements that fair housing testers are specifically trained to document.

You don't have to be denied a lease for your civil rights to have been violated. The statement itself is actionable.

Write down what was said. Write down the date, the time, who was present. Keep that note.

**4. Fair housing complaints are free. HUD and IDHR investigate. You do not need a paid service.**

You have two free options:

**HUD FHEO** — hud.gov/fairhousing / **1-800-669-9777** — file online or by phone. HUD will assign an investigator. If they find probable cause, the case goes to an administrative hearing at HUD's expense. You pay nothing. A successful complainant can receive actual damages, compensation for emotional distress, injunctive relief (the landlord is ordered to allow the modification), and civil penalties against the landlord.

**Illinois IDHR** — **(312) 814-6200** — file a parallel complaint under the Illinois Human Rights Act. State law sometimes covers smaller buildings and provides additional remedies. The two complaints can run simultaneously.

**Equip for Equality** — **(312) 341-0022** — Illinois's disability rights legal organization will represent you in fair housing cases at no cost if your case has merit.

You have one year from the discriminatory act to file. Start now.

**5. There is no HUD grant program for individual renters' disability modifications. Anything charging you to apply for one is a scam.**

HUD operates various housing programs. None of them involve sending grant money to individual renters in private apartments to pay for grab bars.

The Fair Housing Act gives you the right to make the modification yourself, at your own expense, with the landlord's required permission. That right is worth more than any grant — because it's legally enforceable and it belongs to you permanently, not just until a grant runs out.

A "HUD-affiliated disability housing modification program" that charges $300–$800 and promises to obtain federal grants is not HUD. It is a scam. HUD does not affiliate with fee-charging intermediaries to process grant applications on individuals' behalf.

If you've already paid one of these services, file a complaint with the Illinois Attorney General's Consumer Fraud Hotline: **1-800-243-0618**. And file the actual Fair Housing complaint — the one that's free — with HUD or IDHR.

**6. "ADA compliance" has almost nothing to do with your apartment. Don't pay for an inspection.**

The Americans with Disabilities Act applies to commercial buildings and public accommodations — offices, stores, restaurants, government buildings. It does not apply to private residential housing.

Your housing rights as a disabled renter come from the **Fair Housing Act** and the **Illinois Human Rights Act**. Neither of those laws requires you to obtain an inspection, generate a report, or prove ADA compliance. The right to a reasonable modification and reasonable accommodation is triggered by your disability and your need — not by any document.

A service that charges you for an "ADA compliance inspection of your rental unit" is charging you for something that (a) uses the wrong legal framework and (b) produces a document you don't need and cannot legally use to compel your landlord to do anything.
