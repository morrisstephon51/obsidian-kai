There is a woman at that church other women tell.

She does not have a title. There is no line for her in the bulletin. She is just the one a woman decides to sit next to on purpose, in the back, near the door, and then leans over during the last song and says something very quiet that is not about the song.

She keeps a phone that rings at hours phones should not ring. She keeps a guest room made up in case somebody needs it at midnight with two kids and no bag. She has driven a sister across the county line to a place she does not name out loud. She has sat in a parking lot and just listened while a woman decided, out loud, for the first time, that the thing happening to her at home is not something she has to keep surviving.

And then the hard part starts. Because being in danger and getting to safety are two different countries, and the border between them is made of court forms and deadlines that nobody hands a frightened woman a map to, on the exact days she has the least room in her mind to read one.

Because there is an order she can get the same day, for free, without a lawyer and without ever filing a police report, and most women believe they need all three and so they never start. An emergency order of protection can be granted the day she walks into that courthouse. It can order him to stay away, to leave the home, to have no contact. She does not have to have proof stacked in a folder. She does not have to have already called the police. She just has to know the door is there. Because there is a difference between that emergency order, which holds for about three weeks, and a plenary order that can hold for two years, and no one tells her the first one is a bridge to the second, so she thinks the protection ran out when it was only phase one. Because this state has an Address Confidentiality Program that gives her a substitute address, so he cannot find her through the school registration or the lease or the DMV, and she has never heard those words. Because there is crime-victim compensation that will reimburse the cost of moving, the medical bills, the counseling, the wages she lost sitting in court — real money, set aside for exactly this — and almost no one applies because no one ever says it exists. Because there is a law that lets her take job-protected time off to go to court and still have work to come back to, and another that lets her collect unemployment if she had to leave a job to get away from him, and she quit in silence thinking she had thrown her income away. Because there is a Safe Homes Act that lets her break the lease early and change the locks without the penalty a landlord will threaten her with. Because there is a 24-hour hotline and a trained advocate who will meet her at the courthouse and sit in that row so she is not alone in front of a judge. And because the most dangerous moment in all of this is the moment she leaves, which is the one thing the movies get right and the paperwork never mentions, and a safety plan is a real, specific thing that a person who does this for a living can build with her before she moves.

So the woman other women tell tries to hold all of it. In her head. From the last sister she helped, two years ago, when the forms had different names. She tells a woman to bring proof she does not actually need. She gives her a hotline number that changed. She does not mention the compensation fund because she never knew it was there. She is one faithful woman, holding the safety of other women together with a made-up guest room and a memory of a system that rewrites its own rules between one woman at her table and the next.

So the protection stays unfiled. The woman goes back, because back was the only place mapped, and the emergency order that would have bought her three safe weeks was three counties and one form away the whole time. The compensation that would have paid for the apartment goes unclaimed. She quits the job instead of taking the leave the law gave her. And a woman who found the courage to say it out loud in a parking lot does the most dangerous part of it — the leaving — with no plan, because the one person she trusted enough to tell was carrying twelve other women on the same tired memory.

Here is what a woman with money gets on the worst week of her life.

She gets a family-law attorney who files the order of protection before the courthouse closes. She gets a victim advocate whose entire job is to sit beside her, hold the timeline, and make sure the emergency order becomes a plenary one. She gets someone who applies for the compensation, who knows the address program exists, who builds the safety plan for the day she leaves. That woman is not braver than the sister in the back pew. She was just handed a team, and the sister in the back pew was handed a phone number and a prayer.

She can have that help too. For free. On the same phone she called her from.

She can find out that an emergency order of protection is same-day, free, and needs no lawyer and no police report, and walk in knowing exactly what to ask for. She can learn the difference between the emergency order and the plenary one so the protection does not lapse when she thought it was over. She can find out the Address Confidentiality Program exists and how to enroll so he cannot trace her through the school. She can learn that crime-victim compensation will cover the move and the counseling and how to file for it. She can learn she has a right to job-protected leave and to unemployment, before she quits in silence. She can learn the Safe Homes Act lets her out of the lease. She can get the whole maze — the order, the address program, the compensation, the leave, the lease, the safety plan — broken into an order of operations, and then walk it straight to the free 24-hour helpline and the trained advocate who will sit in that courtroom row the rest of the way.

None of it to replace what she does. The back pew is hers. The made-up guest room is hers. The parking lot where a woman said it out loud for the first time is hers, and no machine will ever sit in that car at midnight and just stay. It only takes the maze off her shoulders so she can do the part only she can do, which is to be the person another woman trusts enough to tell in the first place, and to say you are not crazy, this is not yours to carry alone, and there is a door.

That is the gap. It was never that the women in our pews living in fear deserve a harder road to safety than the ones who can put a lawyer on retainer. It is that those women got an advocate and a plan, and ours got one faithful sister with a guest room and a number that went out of date.

A word of care, plainly: if someone is in immediate danger, that is 911, right now. This is not legal advice, and no post replaces a trained advocate. The free, confidential Illinois Domestic Violence Helpline is 1-877-863-6338, 24 hours a day. What AI does here is help the woman who is already standing in the gap know which doors exist and in what order, so she can get a frightened sister to the people paid to keep her safe faster than a two-year-old memory ever could.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities and the people navigating resources beside them in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Matteson. Dolton. Not a webinar. A room. Real problems. A real order filed the same day, a real safe address, a real woman who got out with a plan instead of just a prayer.

The women holding other women's safety together by hand are not behind. They were just never handed the tools the funded programs take for granted.

We are here to hand them over.

The plug is in.
