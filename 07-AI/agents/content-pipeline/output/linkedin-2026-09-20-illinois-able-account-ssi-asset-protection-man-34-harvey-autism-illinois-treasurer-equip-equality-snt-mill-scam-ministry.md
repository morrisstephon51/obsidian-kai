**Here is what ABLE accounts do — and what the seminar didn't say:**

**1. The $2,000 SSI resource limit is real. ABLE accounts live outside it.**

An SSI recipient is required to keep countable resources below $2,000. An Illinois ABLE account balance up to $100,000 is specifically excluded from that calculation under federal law. A person with a disability can have $2,000 in their checking account and $30,000 in their ABLE account and Social Security counts only the $2,000.

This is the law. It has been the law in Illinois since 2016. It does not require a trust. It does not require an attorney. It requires an internet connection and twenty-five minutes.

**2. Working does not cause SSI to vanish — it reduces it gradually.**

The SSI earned income formula is not a cliff. Every dollar of wages does not cancel a dollar of SSI. The first $85/month of wages is excluded entirely (general exclusion plus wage exclusion). After that, only half of remaining wages count against the SSI payment. A person earning $600/month loses approximately $257 of SSI — not all of it. They keep the remainder of their SSI check plus their wages plus their Medicaid.

Marcus has been earning money. His SSI has been reduced by the formula. He has not lost his Medicaid. His mother did not know this because no one had explained it slowly.

**3. ABLE to Work: employed SSI recipients can save even more.**

If a person with a disability is employed, they can contribute an additional amount from their wages on top of the standard $18,000 annual limit. In 2025, this additional amount equals the federal poverty level — approximately $15,060. A working SSI recipient who earns enough can potentially save more than $33,000 in a single year in an ABLE account without affecting benefits.

Marcus bags groceries fifteen hours a week. He is eligible for this provision. He has never been told it exists.

**4. ABLE money can pay for almost anything a disabled person needs.**

Housing. Rent. Utilities. Transportation. Medical equipment. Medications. Job training. Assistive technology. Personal support. Education. Even funeral expenses. The IRS's definition of "qualified disability expenses" is broad enough to cover nearly every significant expenditure in a disabled adult's life.

This is not a restricted account that can only be used for one purpose. It is a savings account with low-cost index fund investments and federal protection from the SSI resource limit.

**5. A Special Needs Trust is the right tool in specific circumstances — but not this one.**

A Special Needs Trust is appropriate when someone with a disability receives a large sum — an inheritance, a settlement, a gift — that would push their assets over $100,000 and beyond the ABLE account protection. It is also used in estate planning when a parent wants to leave assets to a disabled child in a way that protects benefits across generations.

For those situations, a Special Needs Trust drafted by a licensed disability attorney is the correct instrument.

For a 34-year-old who earns $620 a month bagging groceries and is trying to save $6,000 without losing Medicaid — it is not the right instrument. The ABLE account is. And the ABLE account costs nothing to open.
