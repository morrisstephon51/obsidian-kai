There is a young woman in that congregation who is the pride of her whole family. She was the first of them to go to college — the first to walk a stage in a cap and gown while her mother cried in the third row and her grandmother said the word "graduate" like it was scripture. She is thirty-four now. She works a public-service job in the south suburbs — the kind of work where you help people all day and the check is modest on purpose — and every month a loan statement comes for the degree that made her family proud, and the number on it is bigger than it was the year she graduated, not smaller. She does the thing you do when a number scares you and you do not understand how it grew: she stops opening the envelopes. She lets the calls go to voicemail. She has slipped quietly toward default without ever deciding to. And last week a smooth voice on the phone told her that for a few hundred dollars up front and a monthly fee, their company would "handle her forgiveness" and make all of it go away — and for the first time in months, she felt like someone finally had a door.

You know her. She is not irresponsible and she did not waste the degree — she is the first person in her family to have ever stood where she is standing, which means there was no one at her kitchen table who had ever walked the maze of servicers and repayment plans before her, no one who could explain why a balance grows on interest or what the letters even meant. That is not a character flaw. That is the price of being first. The repayment system is genuinely confusing, and it is confusing in ways that hit the people with the least backup the hardest, and it caught up with her the way it catches up with millions of first-generation graduates who did everything they were told to do and were never handed the map for what came after the stage. So she assumes the only options are drown quietly or pay the smooth voice to save her — and that quiet gap between them is exactly where the thieves are waiting.

And that is the part that has to be said plainly, and I will say it again before I am done: a first-generation graduate who borrowed to become the first in her family with a degree and now cannot make the payments on a public-service check did not fail, the help to lower or even erase those federal loans is real and free to ask for, her own servicer and studentaid.gov are where that help actually lives, and no honest helper charges her a fee up front or asks her to hand over her federal aid login. She does not know any of that, because nobody sat the first person in her family to go to college down and told her that the programs were built for exactly a person doing exactly her kind of work. And that not-knowing is the door the scammers are standing in.

And there are doors. Real ones. Doors nobody names for a young woman who has already quietly decided the only way out is to pay a stranger or disappear.

Start with the first thing she needs to hear: a woman who did the hard, right thing — who borrowed to become the first graduate her family ever had and now works helping other people for a modest check — did not come up short because the payments outran the paycheck. Owing on a federal student loan is not a character verdict, and it is not proof she borrowed foolishly. Balances grow on interest in ways nobody explains to an eighteen-year-old signing papers, servicers are confusing by design, and the two biggest programs that could help her — Income-Driven Repayment and Public Service Loan Forgiveness — were built for precisely a person in precisely her job. Not one of them ever being named to her plainly is not proof she managed badly. It is only proof that no one ever handed the first person in her family to go to college the map for what came after the diploma.

So here are the doors almost no one names for her plainly. The first, and the most important, costs nothing and comes before she pays anyone a single dollar: she logs into her real account at studentaid.gov and calls her real loan servicer, so she knows exactly what she owes and to whom — because you cannot fix a number you are too afraid to look at, and everything else starts there. The second is Income-Driven Repayment, a plan that sets her federal payment based on what she actually earns and the size of her family, which for some borrowers can be very low or even nothing at all, and applying for it is free through her servicer or studentaid.gov. The third — and this one could change her life — is Public Service Loan Forgiveness: if she works for a government agency, a public school, or a qualifying nonprofit, the rest of her federal loans can be forgiven after the required qualifying payments, precisely because of the kind of work she already does, and certifying her employment costs nothing. The fourth is a real way out of default — loan rehabilitation or consolidation, and the Department of Education's Fresh Start help — which can bring a defaulted federal loan back into good standing without a fee. The fifth is a legitimate counselor: a nonprofit or accredited financial counselor who charges little or nothing, or her own employer's HR or a college financial-aid office, to help her tell the real options from the noise. And underneath all of it, for the shame of being the one whose success came with a debt she cannot carry alone, there is her family, her church, and another first-generation graduate who once stood exactly here, opening the same envelopes with the same fear, and found IDR or PSLF and will tell her exactly who to call — so she is not sitting alone with a statement certain she is the only one this ever happened to. She is standing in front of a whole row of doors, and she is sure they are all traps or charity, when most of them are simply the programs the country already built for the exact work she is already doing.

But hear this warning before she pays one dollar up front or reads her federal aid login to anyone, because the scams hunt a stressed borrower harder than almost anyone — a person drowning in a balance and dodging calls will move fast when someone finally offers rescue. Hear it plainly, and I will say it again at the end: real student loan help does not work this way. Beware the "student loan relief" or "debt forgiveness" company that charges her a fee up front or a monthly subscription to enroll her in programs that are free — charging up front for federal student loan help is against the law, and they are selling her something the government hands out for nothing. Beware the call, text, or email saying she has been "pre-approved" for forgiveness and must "act before the deadline," dressed up with an official-sounding name and a ticking clock — real forgiveness has no salesperson calling to rush her. And beware anyone, on a site or on the phone, who asks for her FSA ID username and password "to process it for her," because that login is the key to her entire federal aid account, and the real Department of Education and the real servicer never need her to hand it to a stranger. The rule is flat: real federal student loan help — signing up for Income-Driven Repayment, certifying Public Service Loan Forgiveness, getting out of default — is free through her servicer and studentaid.gov; no legitimate helper charges her up front, promises a specific forgiveness, or needs her FSA ID password — so anyone doing those things is the scam, not the help.

And hear this, too, because it matters as much as the warning: her real loan servicer, the real studentaid.gov, the real Income-Driven Repayment and Public Service Loan Forgiveness programs, and a real nonprofit counselor are not scams. The help is real and it was built for a graduate exactly like her. The programs are real and the work she already does may be the very reason she qualifies. The counseling is free or low-cost and there is nothing to buy. The caution is about the fee-charging "relief" companies, the "you've been pre-approved, act now" fake-forgiveness messages, and the FSA-ID phishers — not about the real Department of Education, the real servicer, or the real people whose actual job is to help her for free. Part of the help is learning to tell the free servicer who lowers her payment apart from the smooth voice who wants her money and her login.

Here is where the AI fits, and where it does not — and this is the second time I am saying the part that keeps her safe, on purpose. That is real help, and it is a great deal. But the AI does not tell her which repayment plan to choose as if it were certain, and it does not promise her a forgiveness or a payment figure, because it cannot, and because a machine can be confidently wrong. This is general help finding your footing, not legal or financial advice, and not a determination of what she qualifies for or what she will pay. Eligibility, plans, payment amounts, and forgiveness rules are set by the Department of Education and her servicer — not by an app and not by a guess. She verifies everything the tool helps her understand at the official studentaid.gov, with her servicer, or with a legitimate counselor. And she never puts her FSA ID, her Social Security number, or her bank login into a public AI tool, and she never reads those to a caller or types them into a link — because the honest help does not work that way.
