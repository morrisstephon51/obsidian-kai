There is a man in that congregation who has stopped opening a certain kind of envelope. He is forty-five. He works with his hands — he cuts hair, he fixes what breaks, he takes the 1099 job when it comes — and he is good at it, and he is proud of it. But nobody withholds taxes from a self-employed man's pay, and one rough year ate the cushion he meant to set aside, and now he owes the IRS a number that follows him into every quiet moment. A letter came. He did not open it all the way. Then a call came that was so much worse — a hard voice saying it was the IRS, that a warrant was being issued, that officers were coming today unless he paid right now — and he stood in his own kitchen with his heart going like a hammer. And this week an ad slid across his phone promising to "wipe your IRS debt for pennies on the dollar," and for a second it sounded like the hand reaching down into the hole he is in.

You know him. He is not a tax cheat and he is not a deadbeat — he is a working man caught by a bill with no paycheck deduction standing behind it, which is a trap that catches a lot of honest self-employed people who never had an employer quietly taking the taxes out for them. Nobody handed him a sheet the day he went out on his own explaining that the IRS itself has payment plans, that a person who truly cannot pay can sometimes settle for less or have collection paused, or that there are free clinics and a free advocate inside the IRS whose whole job is to help a regular person deal with exactly this. That is not a character flaw. That is what it looks like when a hard-working man hears the letters "IRS," feels only fear, and decides the safest thing is to not look — because no one ever told him that looking is survivable, and that the door out is real.

And that is the part that has to be said plainly, and I will say it again before I am done: a self-employed man who fell behind on his taxes is not a criminal and is not a failure. Owing the IRS is a common, solvable problem — common enough that the IRS built payment programs for exactly this — and asking for help to deal with it is not weakness. It is the responsible thing a grown man does with a hard thing. The door out of that fear is real, some of the best help for it is free, and no honest helper guarantees the IRS will settle, charges him thousands up front, threatens him with arrest over the phone, or wants a gift card. He does not know any of that, because nobody ever told a scared working man that the IRS is something you can face and still be standing afterward. And that not-knowing is the exact gap the scammers are standing in.

And there are doors. Real ones. Doors nobody names for a proud man who has already decided the only move is to stop answering.

Start with the first thing he needs to hear: the debt is not the end of him, and hiding from it only lets the fear — and the fake callers — do their worst. The honest first step is not paying a stranger and not running — it is finding out what his real options are, because for a lot of people who owe, the real answer is far more livable than the arrest-threat voice on the phone wants him to believe.

So here are the doors almost no one names for him plainly. The first is that the IRS has payment options — installment agreements, plans that let a person who owes pay it down over time instead of all at once — and what he qualifies for depends on his situation and the IRS's rules, so the honest move is to look at it, not to hide from it. The second is that an Offer in Compromise is a real thing: the IRS does sometimes accept less than the full amount owed, but it is decided by the IRS based on his ability to pay, it is not guaranteed, and he applies for it directly with the IRS himself — the application has a fee that can be waived for low income — so "pennies on the dollar, guaranteed" is a sales pitch, not a promise anyone alive can keep. The third is that if he genuinely cannot pay anything right now, there is a status the IRS calls Currently Not Collectible that can pause collection — so "I have nothing" is a real thing to say to them, not a reason to disappear. The fourth is that free or low-cost help exists: a Low Income Taxpayer Clinic helps people who owe or who are fighting with the IRS, often for free or nearly free, and the Taxpayer Advocate Service is a free, independent office inside the IRS itself that steps in when a person is stuck. The fifth is that he has rights — there is a Taxpayer Bill of Rights, the real IRS reaches him by mail first and gives him notice, and there is an appeals process — so he does not lose everything overnight, and he is not without recourse. And underneath all of it, for the private shame of a man who feels like he is the only one who let this happen, there is his family, his church, and another self-employed man who was just as sure the IRS was going to take his truck and his freedom — who instead set up a payment plan, or walked into a clinic and let someone help, and who is still working and still standing today. So he is not the only one who ever fell behind and found a way through.

But hear this warning before he pays one stranger a dollar or types his information into one link, because the scams hunt a frightened man who owes the IRS harder than almost anyone — a person this scared, this ashamed, will move fast the moment a confident voice promises to make the fear stop. Hear it plainly, and I will say it again at the end: real tax help does not work this way. Beware the phone call or text claiming to be the IRS — or the police, or a sheriff — threatening immediate arrest, deportation, a suspended license, or a frozen account unless he pays this minute with a gift card, a wire, a cash app, or crypto, because the real IRS does not call demanding instant payment by gift card and does not threaten to send officers to arrest him over the phone; it writes to him by mail first. Beware the company advertising that it can "settle your tax debt for pennies on the dollar" or "wipe your IRS debt," charging thousands in up-front fees and promising an Offer in Compromise that nobody can guarantee, because the honest truth is the IRS decides that, not a middleman, and the Federal Trade Commission has warned about exactly these mills. And beware the email, text, or site with a link telling him to type his Social Security number, his bank information, or a payment to "release" a refund or "settle" his debt, because the real IRS does not email or text links demanding his personal information or his money. The rule is flat: real tax help — a payment plan or an Offer in Compromise — is done directly with the IRS, or with a free Low Income Taxpayer Clinic or the free Taxpayer Advocate Service; the Offer in Compromise fee can be waived for low income and is not thousands paid to a stranger; no one can guarantee the IRS settles; and the real IRS mails him first and never wants a gift card — so anyone who guarantees a settlement, charges thousands up front, threatens him with arrest on the phone, asks for a gift card, or needs his number typed into a "tax relief" link is the scam, not the help.

And hear this, too, because it matters as much as the warning: the real IRS, the real IRS payment plans and the real Offer in Compromise program, the real Low Income Taxpayer Clinics, the real Taxpayer Advocate Service, and honest licensed tax professionals — a CPA, an enrolled agent, a tax attorney who charges a fair, disclosed fee — are not scams. The programs are real, the payment options are real, and the honest path runs right through the IRS and the free clinics and the advocate. A trained clinic that helps a man deal with what he owes, or an enrolled agent who is upfront about the work and the cost, is not a trick — it is how the honest help is supposed to work. The caution is about the imposter caller, the pennies-on-the-dollar mill, and the phishing link — not about the real IRS program or the real professional whose actual job is to help a man handle his taxes. Part of the help is learning to tell the free, trained helper and the honest professional apart from the confident voice that wants his money and his number.

Here is where the AI fits, and where it does not — and this is the second time I am saying the part that keeps him safe, on purpose. That is real help, and it is a great deal of it. But the AI does not tell him he qualifies for a settlement or how much the IRS will take, and it does not promise him approval or a timeline, because it cannot, and because a machine can be confidently wrong. This is general help finding your footing, not tax or legal advice and not a determination of what he owes or what the IRS will accept. His options and any settlement are decided by the IRS under its rules and his finances — not by an app and not by a guess. He verifies everything the tool helps him understand with the IRS, with a Low Income Taxpayer Clinic, with the Taxpayer Advocate Service, or with a licensed tax professional. And he never puts his Social Security number or his bank information into a public AI tool, and he never types it into a "tax relief" link or pays a stranger by gift card — because the honest help does not work that way.

So if you are reading this and you are him — if you have stopped opening the envelopes, if a voice on the phone told you a warrant was coming, and now an ad is promising to wipe your debt for pennies — hear the whole of it, one more time, because you can face this and still be standing, and you do not have to pay a stranger to deal with the IRS. The first step is not a payment and it is not disappearing — it is a question: what are my real options. Open the letter. It is exactly the kind of man you already are.
