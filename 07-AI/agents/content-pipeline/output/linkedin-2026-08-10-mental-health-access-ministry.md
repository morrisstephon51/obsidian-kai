There is a sister at that church people call at 2am. Nobody assigned her. Her number is just the one that gets passed hand to hand when somebody is falling apart. The phone rings after service and a voice she half-recognizes says the thing everybody says: I just need to talk to somebody. And she stays on the line.

She has sat in an emergency room at three in the morning after an attempt, holding a mother who could not stop shaking. She has talked more than one person off the edge over a phone she never hangs up. She is not a therapist. She has said so a hundred times. She prays, she listens, she shows up, she does not flinch when the ugly thing finally gets said out loud. And because she does not flinch, they come to her and to no one else.

And then the hard part starts. Because needing help in this community and getting to real help are two different fights, and the second one is a maze built out of waitlists, providers who do not take the insurance, a shortage of anybody who looks like the person hurting, and a whole culture that has quietly taught people to pray about it and say nothing.

Because there is a number that answers, day or night, and it is free, and most people carrying this do not know it exists or are terrified of what happens when they call. Because community mental health centers and the clinics built for exactly this are required to serve people whether or not they can pay, on a sliding scale, and they take Medicaid, and nobody ever told the family that the door was that close. Because Illinois Medicaid covers behavioral health, real therapy, and the woman rationing her own breakdown because she assumes she cannot afford one may already be covered. Because there is a mobile crisis response you can reach through that same number, so the answer to a son in psychosis does not have to be a squad car and everyone's worst fear. Because there is a warm line for the night that is heavy but not a 911 emergency, staffed by people who have been there, so 2am does not have to be her phone every single time. Because there are free support groups for the family, so the mother of a son with schizophrenia is not the only person in the world who understands her Tuesday. Because a therapist who takes Medicaid and speaks Spanish and grew up in a place like Harvey exists, and the only reason the family never found one is that no one built them the list. And because stigma is not a diagnosis, and needing help was never weakness, no matter what the room the person grew up in taught them.

So the sister tries to hold all of it. In her chest, at 2am, with no training, no license, and no list of who takes what insurance or who has an opening this month. She sends someone to a clinic that has a nine-week waitlist. She does not know the warm line exists, so every hard night is her night. She tells a family they probably cannot afford therapy when Medicaid would have covered it. She carries the weight of people who trusted her with the thing they told no one else, and she carries it alone, and it is slowly costing her the sleep and the peace that made her the person they call in the first place.

So the call does not get made to the number that would have answered. The family waits nine weeks and the crisis does not wait nine weeks. The son in psychosis meets a police car instead of a clinician. The mother sits in her own house believing no one on earth understands, three miles from a support group full of people who do. The therapy that Medicaid would have paid for never gets sought. And a good woman burns herself down to the wick holding what a whole system was supposed to help her hold.

Here is what a family with resources gets when the mind is the thing that breaks.

They get a psychiatrist. A therapist on the calendar every week. A care coordinator whose entire job is to find the in-network provider, book the intake, and manage the crisis so the family does not have to become experts on the worst day of their life. They get someone who knows which door, in what order, and how to get through it this week and not in nine. That family is not stronger than the one calling our sister at 2am. They were just handed a team, and ours was handed one exhausted woman with a good heart and a phone that never stops.

She can have that help too. For free. On the same phone the 2am calls come in on.

She can find a therapist who takes Medicaid and speaks the family's language and would not feel like a stranger, instead of guessing. She can find the nearest community mental health center or clinic that serves regardless of ability to pay, and know its sliding scale before she sends anyone. She can learn exactly what happens when someone calls or texts 988, so she can hand the number over with the fear taken out of it. She can find the mobile crisis response that comes instead of the police. She can find the warm line for the heavy night that is not an emergency, so she is not the only one awake. She can find the free NAMI family group so the mother of the son with schizophrenia walks into a room of people who already understand. She can get a plain read on the intake paperwork nobody can face when they are already drowning. She can have the whole maze — the crisis line, the clinic, the therapist, the Medicaid coverage, the family group — broken into an order of operations, and then walk each person straight to 988, to a licensed clinician, and to the community mental health center that carry it the rest of the way.

Not to replace what she does. The 2am phone is hers. The ER chair is hers. The reason a person tells her the thing they have told no one else is hers, and no machine will ever have stayed on that line or earned that trust. It only takes the maze off her chest so she can do the part only she can do, which is to sit in the dark with somebody and say you are not crazy and you are not weak and you are not alone, there is real help, and I am going to help you find the door before you spend one more night believing there isn't one.

That is the gap. It was never that the people in our pews deserve less care for their minds than the family that can put a psychiatrist and a therapist on retainer. It is that that family got a team, and ours got one faithful sister running on no sleep and no list.

A word of care, plainly: legitimate crisis help is free. 988 is free. A community mental health center serves you regardless of whether you can pay. So anyone who is not a licensed clinician charging you cash to "coach" or "counsel" you through something a real therapist on a sliding scale or on Medicaid would treat, and any app selling itself as therapy that is not therapy, is not the door — and needing help is not a character flaw a stronger prayer would have fixed. Pray, and get the person to real care, both. This is general navigation, not mental-health treatment, diagnosis, therapy, or crisis counseling — helping someone find a clinician is not the same as being one — so if someone is in immediate danger, call or text 988 or call 911 now, and otherwise always route the actual care to 988 (call or text), a licensed clinician, a community mental health center or FQHC on a sliding scale, Illinois Medicaid behavioral-health services, or NAMI. For a heavy night that is not a 911 emergency, the Illinois Warm Line is 1-866-359-7953. NAMI runs free family and peer support groups and a helpline at 1-800-950-6264. The SAMHSA National Helpline is 1-800-662-4357, and 2-1-1 can point to local help.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities and the people navigating resources beside them in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Matteson. Dolton. Not a webinar. A room. Real problems. A real call made to a number that answered, a real therapist found who took Medicaid and did not feel like a stranger, a real mother who walked into a room and found out she was never the only one.

The people carrying our neighbors' minds through the night by hand are not behind. They were just never handed the tools the resourced take for granted.

We are here to hand them over.

The plug is in.
