There is a woman in that church who cuts her pills in half to make them last, and skips a dose or two the week before her check comes, and would sooner sit in silence and get sick than tell a single soul she cannot afford her own medicine.

You know her. She is at the eight o'clock service and the eleven. She still works — helps an old man bathe and dress in the morning, serves the children their trays in the school cafeteria at noon, and she is on her feet the whole day with diabetes in her blood and pressure in her chest. She is the one who brings the plate to the sick and shut-in. And when she gets home, she opens the cabinet where the orange bottles are, and she does the math nobody sees. How many days until payday. How many pills are left. And then she cuts.

She splits the blood-pressure tablet with a knife to make thirty days stretch to forty-five. She skips the second dose on the days she feels "well enough." And when the insulin is low and the money is lower, she stretches the vial past where it should be gone, using less than the doctor told her, telling herself it is fine, it is just this once, just until the first.

It is not fine. That is the part that has to be said plainly, and I will say it again before I am done: rationing insulin, or heart medicine, or blood-pressure medicine, is not a small economy. It is the kind of thing that puts a working woman in an emergency room, or worse, and she is doing it quietly, month after month, because she cannot see another way and she is too proud to say the words out loud.

And here is what almost no one has ever told her: there are other ways. Real ones. Doors she has walked past her whole life because nobody ever pointed at them.

Start with the simplest one, the one that costs nothing but a question. Her own doctor. There is a good chance the very medicine she is rationing has a generic that costs a fraction of the brand. There is a good chance there is another medication, just as good for her, that costs far less. There is a good chance a ninety-day supply is cheaper than buying it thirty days at a time. She does not know that, because she has never asked — she thinks the price on the counter is simply the price, fixed, final, the way the weather is. It is not. But the doctor cannot lower what the doctor does not know is crushing her, and she has never said it, because saying it feels like begging. So she suffers a fixable thing in silence.

Then the pharmacist — the person standing right there at the counter she picks up from every month, who knows more about the price of medicine than almost anyone she will ever meet. The cash price and the insurance price are sometimes not the same, and sometimes the cash price is lower, and a pharmacist can tell her that. There are pharmacy discount programs. There is the plain fact that the same exact drug can cost one price at this counter and a very different price three miles away. She has never once asked, "Is there a cheaper way to get this?" — because it never occurred to her that the person in the white coat was allowed to help her find one. He is. Most of them want to.

And there is a whole kind of help she has never heard named: patient-assistance programs. Many of the companies that make these medications run programs — free to apply for — for people who cannot afford what they make. Not a scam, not a gimmick, an actual door, sitting there on the official maker's own pages, for people in exactly her spot. There are community health centers, the kind that serve everybody, with pharmacies that price medicine on a sliding scale by what a person actually earns. There are discount tools that compare the price of the same drug across pharmacies. If she is on Medicare, there is help specifically for drug costs, called Extra Help, or the Low-Income Subsidy, for people with limited income. And for insulin in particular, there are now programs and caps that have brought the cost down for many people — something that was not true not long ago, and that she has no way of knowing unless someone tells her to go ask.

She does not know any of that. She hears none of it in the places she trusts. So she cuts the pill, and skips the dose, and stretches the vial, sitting on top of a half-dozen doors that could let her take her medicine exactly the way it was prescribed — without ever choosing between the insulin and the light bill again.

But here is the warning she needs before she goes looking, because trouble hunts people in her exact spot. There are fake "prescription discount card" mailers and websites that exist for one reason: to get her to hand over her Medicare number, her insurance ID, or her Social Security number. There are outfits that promise to get her medications "free" and then charge her a fee up front to do it. There are online pharmacies, slick and cheap-looking, selling counterfeit and unsafe pills to desperate people. Hear this plainly, and I will say it again at the end: the legitimate programs are free to apply for. A real discount card never needs her Social Security number or her bank account. And she should never, ever buy prescription medicine from an online seller she cannot verify. Anybody demanding money up front, or her Medicare number to "unlock" a price, or her bank details, is not the help. That is somebody hunting a sick woman while she is worried.

So she goes without the full dose. Not because she is careless with money — she is one of the most careful people you will ever meet; carelessness is not what makes a woman split a pill with a paring knife. Not because she is being dramatic — she is doing the opposite, hiding it, downplaying it, carrying it alone. She is going without because nobody ever walked her to the doors, and the choice she is quietly making between her medicine and her rent is not a choice a person should ever have to make in silence.

Here is the thing about the woman across town. Same diagnosis. Same numbers on the chart. Same tight, fixed, stretched-to-nothing income. But one evening her daughter came by and caught her at the counter with the knife and the little pile of pill halves, and instead of scolding her, sat down and said: "Momma. Do not ever do that again. There is a program for this. Let me help you." And they called the community health center together. They found the maker's assistance program and filled it out. They asked the doctor about a generic and a ninety-day supply. And now that woman takes her medicine the way the doctor actually wrote it — every dose, every day — and her numbers came down, and her daughter stopped lying awake wondering if the phone was going to ring. That woman was not smarter than the woman in our pew. She was not better with money. She was not more faithful. She just had one person catch her, and know the doors were there, and walk her to them. One woman got her medicine. The other kept cutting — until the morning she did not feel "well enough," and the ambulance came. And the whole difference was a daughter who said "there is a program for this," and a silence where nobody ever did.

The woman in our pew can be walked to those same doors now. Not charity she should feel ashamed to ask about. The plain, ordinary help that was built for exactly her situation, that has been sitting there the whole time, that no one ever told her to reach for.

Because this is where a tool like AI actually meets a woman like her — quietly, at the kitchen table, the orange bottles in a row and the calendar on the wall, doing the part that the shame and the strange words and the not-knowing-where-to-start have been blocking. It can be the patient someone that explains what "generic" and "therapeutic alternative" and "ninety-day supply" and "patient-assistance program" and "sliding-scale pharmacy" and "Extra Help" actually mean, in plain words, as many times as she needs, and never once makes a grown woman feel small for not knowing. It can help her write down the exact questions to carry to her doctor and her pharmacist — the ones she would never think to ask, the ones that change the price. It can help her find the community health center and the free clinic near her instead of leaving her to search, tired and worried, alone. It can help her understand where the real, official patient-assistance program lives so she does not fall into a fake one.

Not to be the one who decides anything. Let that be plain. The machine does not tell her to split a pill, or skip a dose, or stop a medicine, or start one — that belongs to her doctor and her pharmacist, and with insulin and heart medicine getting it wrong can cost a life. It does not diagnose. It does not give medical advice. It does not replace her doctor, her pharmacist, or her health center. And it must never, ever be fed her Social Security number, her Medicare or insurance number, her medical records, or an account number — a public AI tool is not the place for a single line of a woman's private medical and financial life. It is only the patient someone that takes a heavy, word-thick, shame-blocked thing and makes it feel possible, so that a woman who spends her days keeping other people alive does not have to keep herself alive on half a dose.

That is the gap. It was never that the woman across town took better care of herself, or budgeted better, or deserved her medicine more. It is that one of them was caught at the counter and told "there is a program for this, let me help you," and the other was left to do the math alone with a paring knife. Not more careful. Not more faithful. Just never told the doors were there to open.

A word of care, plainly, and I will say the important parts twice. This is general navigation, not medical advice, and not an endorsement of any one program, pharmacy, discount card, or product over another. Never split, skip, stop, or change how you take a prescribed medicine to save money without first talking to your doctor or your pharmacist — that decision is theirs, not a machine's and not your own guess, and with insulin, heart, and blood-pressure medicine it can be dangerous. AI tools can be confidently wrong, so never trust a price, a program, or a rule one gives you without confirming it with your doctor, your pharmacist, and the official Medicare.gov, the Social Security Administration, and the medication maker's own site. And never put a Social Security number, a Medicare or insurance number, medical records, or account numbers into a public AI tool. Hear the heart of it twice, because it is built for people in exactly this spot: the legitimate patient-assistance programs are free to apply for, a real discount card never needs your Social Security number or your bank account, and you should never buy prescription medicine from an online seller you cannot verify. Route the real help to real, trusted places: your own doctor and pharmacist first; the community health center or free clinic for sliding-scale medications; the medication maker's official patient-assistance program; Medicare.gov and the Social Security Administration for Extra Help if you are on Medicare; and 211 or 211.org to find it all.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Dolton. Robbins. Chicago Heights. Matteson. South Holland. Not a webinar. A room. Real people. A real hardworking woman, taught how to make the same tool the family across town already knew how to use sit with her at the kitchen table, explain the words nobody ever explained, write the questions that change the price, find the health-center pharmacy and the real assistance program, and take her medicine the way the doctor wrote it — every dose, every day.

The faithful women in our churches are not going without their medicine because they were careless with money. They are going without because nobody ever told them the doors were there, and they were too proud to say the words out loud.

We are here to walk them to it.
