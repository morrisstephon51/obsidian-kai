There is a member at that church nobody put in a role, but everybody has his number. He is the one you call the Friday you get walked out. Not the pastor, not yet. Him. Because he keeps the little jobs board by the fellowship-hall door, and because when the plant laid off a shift two years back, he was the one who found half those men something before the severance ran out.

He knows who lost work before they announce it. He can read it in a man's shoulders in the third pew. So he pulls him aside, not with pity, just quiet, and says bring me the resume, let me look at it. He sits at the kitchen table and fixes the resume the way he knows how. He drives a sister to the interview across town and waits in the car with the engine running so she has a ride home either way. He writes a number down on the back of a church bulletin, the temp agency that hired his nephew.

And then the hard part starts. Because being out of work and actually getting back to work are two different countries, and the border between them is made of systems nobody explains and doors nobody points you to.

Because the unemployment claim gets filed and then gets flagged, held for a reason the letter does not name, and the appeal has a deadline buried in a paragraph. Because there is free money for training, real money, a program that will pay for the CDL or the CNA or the IT certificate that changes a wage for good, but it runs on enrollment windows and eligibility documents, and by the time a person hears about it the cohort is full. Because an old record that has nothing to do with today kills the background check three offers deep, and there is a way to seal it, but nobody said so. Because the American Job Center down the road can assign a real career navigator for free, but only to the people who walk in and know the words to ask for one. Because the resume gets uploaded four hundred times and never reaches a human, because a screen throws it out before anyone reads it, and no one told him the screen exists.

So he tries to hold all of it. In his head. From the last man he helped when the plant closed. He hands out a temp-agency number that was gold in 2023 and disconnected now. He fixes a resume to look right to him, not knowing a machine reads it first and reads it differently. He is one man, working from memory of a job market that rewrote its own rules between the shift that got cut last time and the family at his table tonight.

So the door stays shut. The unemployment appeal deadline passes unraised and the benefits stop. The free training that would have moved a wage from twelve dollars to twenty-six fills its seats while a good man waits for someone to mention it exists. The offer that would have carried a family gets pulled at the background check nobody knew could be cleared. The resume that could have gotten a callback dies in a screen. And a person who wanted nothing but to work slides from between-jobs to behind-on-everything, quietly, in the space of a season.

Here is what a laid-off professional gets on the way out the door.

They get an outplacement package. A career coach who rewrites the resume to clear the very screens that throw ours out. A recruiter who calls them a peer and walks their name into rooms. Someone whose whole job is to compress the time between the last check and the next one, who knows the deadlines, the programs, the doors. That coach is not more deserving than he is. They are just resourced.

He can have that help too. For free. On the same phone the family called him from.

He can put the actual resume in and get back what a hiring screen sees and what to change so a person reaches a human. He can describe an unemployment claim that got held and get a plain read on why, what an appeal is, and the deadline he cannot miss. He can find out which free training program pays for this exact certificate, whether this family qualifies, what documents it needs, and when the window closes. He can learn that an old record can be sealed and what the first step is. He can get the whole maze — claim, appeal, training money, the job center, the background check underneath it — broken into an order of operations he can run this week, while the door is still open.

Not to replace what he does. The kitchen table is his. The engine running in the interview parking lot is his. He is the one who reads it in a man's shoulders before a word gets said, and no machine will ever hand somebody a temp number and mean I have got you the way he does. It just takes the maze off his shoulders so he can do the part only he can do, which is believe in a person out loud until they get back on their feet.

That is the gap. It was never that the members in our pews one layoff from losing everything deserve a slower road back to work than the ones who get handed a coach on the way out. It is that those workers got an outplacement firm, and ours got one faithful man with a jobs board and a number that goes out of date.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities and the people navigating resources beside them in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Matteson. Dolton. Not a webinar. A room. Real problems. A real resume, a real deadline, a real door somebody gets to walk through.

The people carrying our neighbors back to work by hand are not behind. They were just never handed the tools the funded programs take for granted.

We are here to hand them over.

The plug is in.
