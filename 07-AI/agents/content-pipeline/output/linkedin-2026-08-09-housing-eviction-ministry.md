There is a man at that church whose title is "trustee," which most people think means he counts the offering and checks the boiler. What it actually means is that he is the one who knows, before anyone announces it, which family is about to lose their home.

He knows because the mother pulled him aside after service, not crying, just quiet, and said the rent got behind when the hours got cut and now there is a paper taped to the door. He knows the fund can cover a piece of it — not all of it, it never covers all of it — and he knows that a piece is sometimes enough if it lands at the right hour. So he writes the check from the mutual-aid line nobody sees. He sits at her kitchen table. He drives her to the courthouse on the morning of the hearing so she does not have to stand in front of a judge by herself in a hallway full of strangers.

And then the hard part starts. Because being behind on rent and actually keeping your home are two different countries, and the border between them is made of deadlines nobody explains and forms nobody hands you.

Because there is a clock. A five-day notice is not the end — it is the start of something a person can still win, if they know it is a start. An eviction hearing has a date that does not care how hard the month was. Rental assistance exists, real money, but the application stalls on one missing pay stub or one landlord who won't return a call, and the window closes while a family waits for a document. Legal aid exists — a lawyer who can get a case continued or thrown out on a technical defect the tenant never knew was a defect — but the clinic has three slots and a Tuesday deadline. The housing-authority waitlist reopens for six hours, once, on a day you had to already know about. And the utility shutoff arrives on top of all of it, because trouble does not come one bill at a time.

So the trustee tries to hold all of it. In his head. From the last family he helped two years ago. He tells the mother to bring "the papers" without knowing which papers this judge wants. He gives her a legal-aid number that was right in 2023 and rings to a full voicemail now. He is one man, working from memory of a system that rewrites its own rules between the family he helped last time and the one at his table tonight.

So the roof goes. The hearing happens and nobody raised the defense that would have stopped it. The rental-assistance money that would have covered the gap gets approved four days after the sheriff's date. The family that could have stayed put with one phone call to a landlord who would have said yes ends up in a cousin's living room, then a car, then the part of the story nobody wants to tell in a testimony.

Here is what a family with money gets when the notice comes.

They get a tenant's attorney who knows the judge, knows the defect in the filing, and gets the case continued before lunch. They get a housing counselor who assembles the rental-assistance packet so it does not stall. They get someone who calls the landlord as a peer and negotiates a payment plan in one afternoon. They get a person whose whole job is to keep a family in place from the moment the notice lands to the moment the crisis breaks. That person is not more faithful than he is. They are just resourced.

He can have that help too. For free. On the same phone the family called him from.

He can describe what is happening right now and get back a plain-language read on what a five-day notice actually is, what the hearing date means, and what has to happen before it. He can find out which rental-assistance program this family qualifies for, exactly which documents it needs, and how to keep it from stalling. He can get the legal-aid clinics sorted by who has an open slot and a deadline this family can still make. He can learn what a landlord payment plan looks like and what to say to open one. He can have the whole maze — notice, hearing, assistance, legal aid, the utility shutoff underneath it — broken into an order of operations he can follow this week, before the date that does not move.

Not to replace what he does. The kitchen table is his. The ride to the courthouse is his. He is the one who knew which family to ask about before they said a word, and no machine will ever write a check from a fund and mean it the way he does. It just takes the maze off his shoulders so he can do the part only he can do, which is stand beside them.

That is the gap. It was never that the families in our pews one missed paycheck from the street deserve a smaller chance to keep their homes than the ones who can put an attorney on retainer. It is that those families got handed a housing counselor and a lawyer, and ours got handed one faithful trustee with a checkbook and a memory that goes out of date.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities and the people navigating resources beside them in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Matteson. Dolton. Not a webinar. A room. Real problems. A real notice, a real deadline, a real door somebody gets to keep.

The people holding our neighbors' roofs up by hand are not behind. They were just never handed the tools the funded programs take for granted.

We are here to hand them over.

The plug is in.
