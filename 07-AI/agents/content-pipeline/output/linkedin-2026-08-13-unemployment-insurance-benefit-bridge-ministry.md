There is a woman in that church who has never once asked anybody for anything.

She works. That is who she is. Fourteen years she cleaned rooms at the same hotel, or set and stripped the same banquet hall, in on the early shift, staying for the extra one when they were short, the kind of steady that a place quietly builds itself on and never thanks. She ushers on Sundays, or she is back in the kitchen after service with the foil pans, feeding everybody else. If you asked her about herself she would tell you she is blessed and change the subject to you. She has never been on unemployment in her life, and she would tell you that with a little pride, because in her mind unemployment is for a certain kind of person, and she is not that kind. She is the kind who works.

And three weeks ago they laid her off.

Not fired. Not for anything she did. The hotel "restructured," or changed hands, or cut a floor, and when the list came down it had the senior people on it, the ones who had been there long enough to cost a little more, and her fourteen years turned out to be the reason her name was on it instead of the reason it was safe. She cleaned out her locker, said she was fine, told the girls she would see them around, and drove home to a house where the bills do not restructure. The car note comes. The light bill comes. The grocery run comes whether there is a check behind it or not. And the small savings she had, the little cushion she was proud of, started going down like water out of a cracked cup.

And she has not told anybody.

She tells the church she is "fine, just between things." She puts the groceries on a card and moves money she does not have from one thing to cover another, robbing Peter to pay Paul the way her mother used to say it. She is looking for work, hard, but the good jobs do not answer and the fast ones pay half of what she made, and every week the cushion gets thinner and the shame gets thicker. Because in her mind, needing help is the failure. She has carried other people her whole life. She does not have a slot in her head for being the one who needs carrying.

Here is what almost nobody sat this woman down and told her: unemployment is not welfare. It is insurance. It is a thing she has been paying into out of every single paycheck for fourteen years — money that came out of her wages, quietly, the whole time she was cleaning those rooms, to buy exactly this, a bridge for exactly this day. She did not stop paying premiums on her car insurance and then feel like a beggar for filing a claim after somebody hit her. This is the same thing. She got hit — laid off, through no fault of her own — and there is a claim she already bought the right to make. Nobody ever told her that. So she is sitting in the wreck, ashamed to call it in.

Because she may well qualify and has decided, all by herself, that she does not. This is the quiet trap that costs people the most. Unemployment is generally for people who lost work through no fault of their own and are able and available and looking for the next job — which is her, exactly, to the letter — but she "heard" it is only for certain situations, or a cousin got denied once, or she just assumes a woman who was laid off from housekeeping is not "the type," and so she never files at all. And the honest truth is not "you definitely qualify" and it is not "you definitely don't." The honest truth is that the state decides that, not her fear and not a rumor at the bus stop, and the only way the state can decide yes is if she applies. An enormous number of people who would have gotten it never find out, because they disqualified themselves before anyone official ever looked.

Because she tried once, and it beat her. She went to the website one night, tired, and it asked for things she did not have in front of her, or it timed out, or it threw an error and dumped her back to the start, and she closed the laptop and took that as a sign — see, it is not for me, it does not want me. She did not know that a confusing website is not a "no." She did not know she could get free help to fill it out, sitting next to a real person who does this all day, at the state's own office or a career center that will also help her hunt for the next job while she is there. The wall she hit was the website. She read it as the whole system saying no.

Because even a denial is not the end, and nobody told her that either. If a claim comes back denied — and sometimes the first answer is a no that a second look turns into a yes — there is an appeal, it is FREE, and it has a deadline printed right on the notice, a real date, and if you miss it you lose a thing you might have won. People walk away from money they were owed every day, not because they truly did not qualify, but because a denial letter looks final and official and no one ever told them that "denied" often means "denied, for now, unless you appeal by this date." A free legal-aid lawyer can help her with that appeal at no cost. She does not have to fight the letter alone.

Because — and hear this part, because it hunts people in exactly her spot — the minute someone is laid off and desperate and searching "how do I file for unemployment," the wolves are already waiting in the search results. The fake websites dressed up to look like the state's. The "we'll file your claim for you" services that charge a fee to do the free thing she could do herself. The texts and emails and calls that say her benefits are "on hold" and she just needs to "verify" her Social Security number or her bank login or click this link to "release" the money — phishing, every one of them, fishing for the exact information a thief needs to steal her identity or her benefits. The people who will tell her to buy gift cards to "unlock" a payment. Filing is free. Only the real state agency runs it. No honest office on earth asks you to pay a fee, buy a gift card, or hand over your bank password to give you money you are owed.

So she sits in a pew she has anchored for years, groceries on a card, savings running out, looking for work with one hand and hiding the whole thing with the other, convinced that the bridge is not for her — a bridge she built with fourteen years of her own withheld wages. She is not going without because she failed. She provided for a decade and a half. She is going without because nobody ever told her the insurance was hers, and pride and a broken website and a stack of scams stood between her and the one thing that was designed to catch her.

Here is the thing about the woman across town. She got laid off the same week, from the same kind of job, with the same fourteen years and the same pride. But she had a coworker on the way out the door who said, "File today. Don't wait. It's insurance you already paid for — this is what it's for. Here's the real site, not the fake one, and I'll sit with you while you do it." And when the first notice came back looking wrong, that same coworker said, "Appeal it, there's a deadline, don't let it sit." So the woman across town has a benefit bridge quietly carrying her household while she takes her time to find the right next job instead of the first panicked one. She is not more hardworking than the woman in the pew. She is not more deserving. She just got told, by somebody who knew, that the thing was hers and how to reach it. One of them has a bridge. The other has a cracked cup and a growing pile of shame. And the difference between them was never the work ethic. It was who had somebody in their ear saying the insurance you bought is yours — go claim it.

The woman in the pew can be walked to that same bridge now. Not a handout she is ashamed to take. The insurance she already paid for, out of her own wages, for exactly this week.

Because this is where a tool like AI actually meets a woman like her, quietly, at the kitchen table, doing the part her pride and a bad website have been blocking. It can be the patient someone at nine o'clock at night who explains what "unemployment insurance" actually is — that it is insurance, that she paid into it, that this is not begging — and what "eligibility" and "no fault of your own" and "waiting week" and "work-search" and "appeal" mean, in plain words, as many times as she needs, and never once makes her feel like the kind of person she was raised to look down on. It can help her find the real, official state site and steer her clear of the copycats and the fee-charging fakes. It can help her get her own basic facts lined up before she files — the name of the employer, the dates she worked — so the website stops beating her. It can draft the exact questions to ask the state office or a free legal-aid lawyer. It can help her actually read a denial notice and find that deadline before it passes. And it can help her line up 211 and food and rent bridges for the weeks between filing and the first payment, so the cushion stops draining while she waits.

Not to be her caseworker, and not to be her lawyer. Let that be plain. The machine does not decide whether she qualifies — only the official state unemployment office can look at her actual work history and say yes or no and how much and how long. It does not give legal advice, and for a denied or disputed claim only a real legal-aid attorney should guide the appeal. It does not file the claim for her. It does not replace the state agency or the lawyer. And it must never, ever be handed her Social Security number, her bank or account login, or her state-account password — that is exactly what the fake sites and the phishing texts are fishing for, and a public AI tool is not the place for any of it. It is only the patient someone who makes a frightening, confusing process feel possible, so that a woman who has quietly carried everyone else can finally reach for the bridge she already built.

That is the gap. It was never that the woman who ushered and cooked and cleaned rooms for fourteen years is less deserving of a bridge than the woman across town. It is that one of them had somebody say "file today, it's yours, here's how," and the other got a website that kicked her out, a head full of shame that told her it was welfare, and a phone full of scammers pretending to be the help. Not less faithful. Not less hardworking. Just never told the insurance she bought with her own wages was hers to claim.

A word of care, plainly, and I will say the important parts twice. This is general navigation, not an endorsement of any one agency, company, or attorney over another, and it is not legal advice. AI tools can be confidently wrong, so never trust an eligibility rule, a deadline, or a dollar amount one gives you without confirming it with the official state unemployment office or a real legal-aid attorney — unemployment rules and dates are specific and time-bound, and only the state and a qualified lawyer can tell you what applies to your claim. Never put private information into a public AI tool: not your Social Security number, not your bank or account login, not your state-account password. And hear this one twice, because it is built for people in exactly this spot: filing for unemployment is FREE and only the official state agency handles it, so no one legitimate ever charges you a fee to file, asks you to buy gift cards, or texts you to "verify" your Social Security number or "release" your benefits — anyone who does is a scam, and a copycat website or a fee-charging "filing service" is not the same thing as the real, free help that exists. Route the real help to real, trusted places: the official Illinois state unemployment office (IDES) to file the claim and to appeal a denial; an Illinois workNet or American Job Center career center for free help filing and for the job search itself; a free legal-aid provider for a denied or disputed claim; and 211 or 211.org to bridge food, rent, and utilities during the wait.

The Plug AI brings real AI literacy to faith communities in south suburban Cook County. Harvey. Dolton. Robbins. Chicago Heights. Matteson. South Holland. Not a webinar. A room. Real people. A real woman who cleaned rooms for fourteen years, taught how to make the same tool the woman across town never even needed sit with her at the kitchen table and help her find the real state site, file the claim, read the notice, and hit the appeal deadline — so the woman everybody in that building has quietly leaned on can finally claim the bridge she already paid for.

The most faithful people in our churches are not going without because they failed to provide. They are going without because nobody ever told them the insurance they already bought was theirs to claim.

We are here to walk them to it.

The plug is in.
