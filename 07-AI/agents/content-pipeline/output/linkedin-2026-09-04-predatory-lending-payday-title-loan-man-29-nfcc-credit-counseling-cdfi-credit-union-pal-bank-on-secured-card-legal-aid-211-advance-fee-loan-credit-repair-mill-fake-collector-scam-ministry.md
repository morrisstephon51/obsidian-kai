There is a young man in that congregation who is always the first one there to help move chairs and the last one to ask anybody for anything. He's twenty-nine. So he did what the sign in the window said he could do: he walked into a storefront, signed a page he didn't fully read, and walked out with five hundred dollars against his next check, or against the title to that same car. And it fixed the car. But the loan didn't end when the car got fixed. It rolled. And it rolled again. And now, months later, he's paid back the five hundred more than once over in fees alone and he still owes it, and every payday a piece of his check disappears before he ever touches it, and somewhere in there he's quietly decided the problem is him — that he's just "bad with money." Here is what nobody ever told him plainly: that loan was built to do exactly this to a working man, getting caught in it is the trap working, not him failing — and there is free, real help to refinance out of it and build his credit the honest way, and taking that help is not weakness, it's a working man taking back the wheel.

You know this young man. He's the one who says "I'm good, I'll figure it out" when you can see he's tired, who never wants to be a burden, who took the loan in the first place precisely because he was trying to be responsible and get himself to work without asking anybody for a dime. He'll tell you he messed up and he'll own it twice over — while the truth is that a payday loan and an auto-title loan are products engineered to trap a working person in fees, and the triple-digit interest and the endless rollover were the plan, not an accident. But hear this warning before he sends a single dollar or reads a single number over the phone, because the scams hunt a young man who's behind and ashamed harder than almost anyone — a man who wants a yes so badly he'll stop reading the fine print. Beware the payday and auto-title trap itself: the triple-digit APR, the endless rollover, and the title loan that can repossess the very car he needs to get to work. Beware the credit-repair mill that charges a big up-front fee and promises to "erase" accurate debt or "boost" his score with a "new credit identity" or CPN number — that is illegal, it can make him the one committing fraud, and it can leave his credit worse than it started. And beware the fake debt collector — "phantom debt" — that calls to threaten arrest, wage seizure, or a lawsuit over a debt he doesn't owe or that expired years ago, demanding he pay right now by gift card or wire; and any "you're pre-approved, verify your bank login and Social Security number" text or site is phishing, straight out.
