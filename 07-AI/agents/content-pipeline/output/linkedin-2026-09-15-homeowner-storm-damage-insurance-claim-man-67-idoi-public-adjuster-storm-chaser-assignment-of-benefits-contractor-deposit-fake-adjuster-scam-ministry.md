There is a man in that church whose roof came apart on a Tuesday in June, and by Wednesday morning he had already been handed the wrong help.

You know him. Sixty-seven years old, retired eighteen months now after a lifetime of getting up before the sun — the mill, then the city, then a stretch driving for the district until his knees told him to stop. He owns his home. That brick ranch off the main road, the one with the awning he put up himself and the garden his wife started before she passed — he has the deed in a drawer and almost nothing owed on it, and that house is the single largest thing a lifetime of honest work ever bought him. So when the storm came through — that green-sky, sideways-rain derecho that stripped shingles off half the block and dropped a limb through the neighbor's fence — and he woke to a brown stain spreading across the living-room ceiling and a steady drip into a soup pot on the carpet, he did what he has always done. He stood in the yard in the gray morning light and tried to figure out how a man fixes this himself.

And here is the part that would let him breathe if somebody said it plainly: he doesn't have to fix it himself, and he doesn't have to figure out the money. He has been paying for exactly this, every single month, for thirty years. There is a homeowner's insurance policy with his name on it, built for the morning a storm opens his roof — and he has never once had to use it, so he has no idea how it works, what it covers, or how to make it do what he paid it to do. Nobody handed him that door. The people who found him first pulled into his driveway with a ladder on the truck and a clipboard in hand, and their whole business is a wrecked roof and a worried man who's never done this before.

Because a proud man standing in his yard looking up at torn shingles, on a fixed income, who has never filed a claim in his life, is exactly who these crews are built to find. So when a clean truck rolls up within the hour — sometimes before he's even called anyone — and a friendly man in a company polo says, *"We were working your neighbor's place, saw your roof, real bad up there. Don't you worry about a thing — we handle the whole insurance claim for you, insurance pays for all of it, no cost to you, and we'll even waive your deductible. Just sign right here so we can get started before the next storm and before your deadline runs out,"* it does not sound like a scam. It sounds like the first real answer anybody's given him. And "no cost to you" and "sign here and I'll deal with the insurance company" feels like the smartest, easiest thing he could do on the worst morning he's had since the funeral.

So there it is — the squeeze. A newly retired man, alone in the house now, a soup pot filling on the carpet, a ceiling he's afraid will come down, no idea what his policy says or how a claim even starts, and a warm, confident voice in his driveway saying *sign this today and it's all handled.* That is the exact moment they get him: the paper that turns out to be an "assignment of benefits" signing his whole claim and his settlement check over to a stranger; the "we'll waive your deductible" that is really insurance fraud he doesn't know he's agreeing to; the big deposit up front for a crew that half-finishes or never comes back; the "adjuster" who calls that night wanting his policy number and his Social Security number to "get you approved faster." And the urgency — *before the next storm, before the deadline* — that keeps him from calling his own insurance company, or his daughter, or anybody who's done this before and could catch it.

Say it plainly, and I'll say it again before I'm done: the danger is not that he's foolish, or careless, or "should've known how insurance works." The danger is that he has a real, honest need — a roof torn open, a ceiling coming down, a home he has to protect — and a real, honest door he already paid thirty years for that nobody ever taught him how to open. So he's left to the first crew that pulls in, and that crew's whole job is to get a signature or a Social Security number before he thinks to call his own insurer. He doesn't know he can file the claim himself, for free. He doesn't know he never signs a thing at the door. He doesn't know that "we'll waive your deductible" is a red flag, not a favor. None of that is a failing in him. It's a policy nobody ever walked him through and a claim nobody ever showed him how to make.

And there are doors. Real ones — that protect a homeowner and the house he worked his whole life for — and that almost nobody names for a sixty-seven-year-old man standing in his yard the morning after the storm.

Start with the first thing he needs to hear, before a single door: owning that home after a lifetime of work is a testimony, not a target, and a roof the storm tore open is not his failure. Not knowing how a claim works isn't foolishness — he has never had to file one, nobody teaches it, and the policy was written, on purpose, to be hard to read. Being the first face a storm-chaser looks for — because he's older, because he's alone in the house now, because he's the kind of man who'd rather handle it himself than bother anyone — is not his fault either. And asking his kids to sit with him while he reads a contract, or calling a counselor to explain a word he doesn't know, is not weakness. It's stewardship of the biggest thing he owns. The shame in this story belongs to the crew that circles a wrecked roof looking for a signature — never, ever to him.

So here are the doors almost no one names for him plainly, none ranked, all worth a look. Before he signs a line or pays a dime: there is his **own insurance policy** and its claims process — he calls his insurer at the number on **his own bill or policy** (not a number off a flyer), reports the damage, gets a **claim number** and the company's adjuster, takes photos of everything, and makes only the **temporary repairs** needed to stop further damage — a tarp over the hole, the pot under the drip — keeping every receipt, because his policy usually requires him to prevent it getting worse. There is the **Illinois Department of Insurance** — free help understanding his rights and the process, and the place to **file a complaint** if the company drags its feet, lowballs him, or denies him. There is a **licensed public adjuster** — a professional who works *for him*, not the insurance company, for a percentage of the claim; that's an *option*, never a requirement, and only after he **verifies the license** and reads exactly what it costs him — and it's a different job from the company's adjuster (who works for the insurer) and from a contractor (who works to get the repair job). There is the right way to pick a **contractor** — in writing, more than one estimate, **never** a big deposit or the whole payment up front, license checked, references checked, and the right to cancel a door-to-door contract within a few days. There is the plain truth about the **mortgage** if he still owes a little: the check is often made out to him *and* the servicer and released as the work gets done — that's normal, and it's one more reason a contractor demanding the whole check up front is a warning, not a convenience. If the storm was a **federally declared disaster**, there may be **FEMA** help or an **SBA** disaster loan — but insurance comes first, and a real FEMA inspector never charges a cent. There is free **legal aid** — CARPLS, LAF, Prairie State — if a claim is wrongly denied or a contractor cheats him. And there is **2-1-1**, the free call that opens the rest. That's a whole row of doors — the coverage he paid for, a state office on his side, a professional who can work for him, protection from a bad contract — and he's about to sign it all away to a stranger in a polo shirt because nobody ever told him one of the real ones was already his.

But hear the warning before he signs anything or hands over a number, because these crews hunt a neighborhood the week after a storm. Hear it plainly, and I'll say it again at the end. There is the **storm-chaser** — the door-knocking "roofer" or "restoration crew" the hour after the wind dies, promising a free roof and "no cost to you" if he'll just sign today. There is the **assignment of benefits** — a paper that signs his claim *and his settlement check* over to the contractor, so he loses control of his own money and his own roof. There is the **"we'll waive your deductible"** line — which is insurance *fraud*; the deductible is his to pay, and a contractor "eating" it almost always means padding a fake or inflated claim, which can **void his coverage** and put *him* on the wrong side of the law. There is the **deposit-theft** contractor who takes a big payment up front and does shoddy work or vanishes. There is the **fake "insurance adjuster" or "FEMA inspector"** at the door or on the phone, wanting his policy number, his Social Security number, or his bank information to "process your claim," or a fee to "release" or "expedite" it. There is the **phishing** text — *click here to verify your claim* — that steals his login. And there is the **"recovery" second scam** that circles back weeks later, promising to get a denied claim paid — for a fee upfront. The rule is flat: he signs **nothing** at the door — not a contract, and *never* an assignment of benefits — and pays no full amount or big deposit up front; no honest contractor "waives your deductible," because that's fraud that can cost him his coverage; his insurer and the Illinois Department of Insurance never charge a fee to "process," "release," or "expedite" a claim, because filing is *free*; a real adjuster or FEMA inspector never demands gift cards, a wire, cash, or same-day payment, and never needs his Social Security number or bank login handed to somebody who contacted *him* first; and he confirms everything through his own insurer's number on his policy, the Illinois Department of Insurance's official number, and licenses he looks up himself — never a number off the flyer, the truck door, the text, or the knock.

So he almost signed. Not because he's a fool — a fool doesn't pay off a house over thirty years, doesn't put a tarp up in the rain to keep the damage from spreading, doesn't stand in his yard trying to do right by the home his wife loved. He almost signed because the ceiling was coming down and a confident stranger said *no cost to you, I'll handle everything,* and on that morning that was the only hand anybody had offered.

Here's the thing about the man across town. Same storm, more or less — same block took the wind, same shingles in the yard, same brown stain on the ceiling, same truck in the driveway by mid-morning with the same clipboard and the same *sign here, insurance pays, we'll waive your deductible.* But somebody had already sat with him once and said: *"If a storm ever hits, don't sign a thing at the door. Call your own insurance company first — the number's on your bill — and file it yourself; it's free. If you want somebody in your corner you can hire a licensed public adjuster, but check the license. And listen — anybody offering to 'waive your deductible' is telling you they're about to commit fraud with your name on it."* So he told the crew he'd be in touch, went inside, called his insurer off his own statement, got a claim number and the company's adjuster out, took his photos, kept his receipts, and when he had a question he didn't understand, he called the Illinois Department of Insurance and a legal-aid line instead of the number on the flyer. He wasn't smarter than the man in our pew. He wasn't sharper, and he didn't love his house more. He had one person who knew the doors were there and named them before a stranger got a signature. One man is getting his roof made right with his coverage and his dignity intact. The other almost handed his whole claim — and the check he paid thirty years for — to a man he'd known for four minutes.

The man in our pew can be met before that clipboard is ever signed. Not with a lecture about how he should've read his policy years ago. With a hand on the shoulder and the plain truth that he has real doors, and that a storm tearing open the roof over an honest man's head does not make him a mark for the first voice that says *sign today.*

Because this is where a tool like AI actually meets a man like him — quietly, at the kitchen table, the policy open in front of him and the pot still dripping in the other room. It can be the patient someone that explains, in plain words, what a "deductible" is, what an "assignment of benefits" does, the difference between the company's adjuster and a public adjuster who'd work for him, what a "loss draft" means if he still owes on the house, and what his right to cancel a door-to-door contract is — as many times as he needs to hear it, and never once makes him feel small for not knowing. It can help him find his own insurer's real claims number, the Illinois Department of Insurance, a way to check whether a public adjuster or a contractor is actually licensed, legal aid, the Attorney General, and 2-1-1. It can help him photograph and write down every bit of the damage, keep a simple claim diary of who said what and when, save his receipts, and write out — and say out loud first — the exact questions to ask the adjuster and the contractor, so the whole weight of it doesn't sit on his chest when a stranger picks up.

Not to file the claim for him, and not to be the expert. Let that be plain. The machine is not his insurance company, not his adjuster, not his lawyer, and not his contractor; it can't decide his claim, can't promise him a dollar, can't put a value on his roof, and it must never, ever be fed his Social Security number, his policy or claim number, his bank login, or the details of his claim check — a public AI tool is not the place for a single line of that. Because a "sign here, insurance pays" clipboard and a "pay to release your claim" phone call can strip a wrecked house down to the studs, its very first job is to slow him down — sign nothing at the door, pay no fee, hand over no check and no number — and point him to his own insurer, the Department of Insurance, and someone he trusts.
